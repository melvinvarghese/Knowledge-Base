# Microfinance and Social Entrepreneurship - Muhammad Yunus, Grameen Bank, and Poverty Alleviation

## Overview
Microfinance refers to the provision of small-scale financial services—including microcredit, micro-savings, and micro-insurance—to impoverished individuals excluded from traditional banking systems.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
The Grameen Bank Model & Solidarity Lending: Founded in Bangladesh by economics professor Muhammad Yunus in 1976 (awarded Nobel Peace Prize 2006). Traditional commercial banks denied credit to the rural poor due to a lack of physical collateral, creating severe adverse selection and moral hazard barriers. Yunus pioneered **Solidarity Lending**: extending tiny, collateral-free loans to self-organized groups of five borrowers (primarily impoverished women). If one borrower defaults, the entire group loses access to future credit. This social peer-monitoring replaced physical collateral with **social collateral**, driving loan repayment rates above 95% while empowering female entrepreneurs.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Commercialization, Over-Indebtedness, and Impact Debates: In the 2000s, microfinance attracted private equity and commercial IPOs (e.g., Compartamos in Mexico, SKS Microfinance in India). Commercialization brought predatory interest rates (40-100% APR), aggressive collection tactics, and borrower debt traps. Rigorous randomized controlled trials (RCTs) conducted by Esther Duflo and Abhijit Banerjee (J-PAL, Nobel 2019) demonstrated that while microcredit aids household financial smoothing, it rarely generates transformative long-term poverty escapes.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Catalyzed modern financial technology (Fintech) mobile money innovations like M-Pesa in Kenya and global financial inclusion agendas.

## Key Terminology
- **Microcredit**: Extension of very small collateral-free loans to low-income borrowers to foster micro-enterprise self-employment.
- **Solidarity Lending**: Lending practice where peer groups guarantee each other's loans, utilizing social capital and peer pressure as loan collateral.
- **Financial Inclusion**: Access to useful and affordable financial products and services delivered in a responsible and sustainable way.
