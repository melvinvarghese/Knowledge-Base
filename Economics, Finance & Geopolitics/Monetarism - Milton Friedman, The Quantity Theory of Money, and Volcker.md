# Monetarism - Milton Friedman, The Quantity Theory of Money, and Volcker

## Overview
Monetarism, championed by Milton Friedman, asserts that fluctuations in the money supply are the primary determinant of inflation and business cycle fluctuations.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
The Quantity Theory of Money: Formalized by the Fisher equation of exchange: $M \cdot V = P \cdot Y$, where $M$ is money supply, $V$ is velocity, $P$ is price level, and $Y$ is real output. Assuming velocity is stable in the long run, Friedman famously concluded: 'Inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon.'

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Critique of Discretionary Policy & The Volcker Shock: Monetarists argued that government fine-tuning causes stagflation due to long and variable policy lags, advocating a constant money supply growth rule. In 1979–1982, Federal Reserve Chairman Paul Volcker implemented monetarist principles, hiking the Federal Funds Rate to 20% to break 1970s double-digit stagflation.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Shift of modern macroeconomic consensus toward independent central bank inflation targeting, rejection of Phillips curve tradeoffs in the long run.

## Key Terminology
- **Quantity Theory of Money**: Economic proposition stating that changes in price levels are directly proportional to changes in money supply.
- **Stagflation**: Persistent high inflation combined with high unemployment and stagnant demand in an economy.
- **K-Percent Rule**: Friedman's monetary policy proposal requiring central banks to expand money supply at a fixed annual percentage.
