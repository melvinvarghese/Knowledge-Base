# Game Theory and Oligopoly - Nash Equilibrium, Cournot, Bertrand, and Stackelberg Models

## Overview
Oligopoly is an intermediate market structure dominated by a small number of interdependent firms whose strategic actions, output decisions, and pricing strategies directly impact one another.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Strategic Oligopoly Models: (1) **Cournot Duopoly** (1838): Firms compete by choosing output quantities simultaneously. Market price clears total output; the resulting Cournot-Nash equilibrium produces higher output and lower prices than a monopoly, but lower output than perfect competition. (2) **Bertrand Duopoly** (1883): Firms produce homogeneous goods and compete on price simultaneously. Any firm pricing slightly below its rival captures the entire market, triggering an aggressive price war until $P = MC$—known as the **Bertrand Paradox**, where two firms suffice to recreate perfect competition pricing. (3) **Stackelberg Model** (1934): A dynamic leader-follower game where the first-mover firm commits to an output quantity first, securing a significant first-mover competitive advantage.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Collusion and Cartel Instability: Firms have an incentive to form cartels (e.g., OPEC) to behave like a joint monopoly. However, the Prisoner's Dilemma dynamic reveals that every individual member faces a dominant temptation to secretly cheat and produce above quota, making cartels inherently unstable without coercive enforcement.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Analyzes rivalry between corporate duopolies (Boeing vs Airbus, Visa vs Mastercard, AMD vs Intel) and informs merger simulation guidelines.

## Key Terminology
- **Cournot Competition**: Oligopoly model where firms simultaneously choose output levels, taking their competitors' output choices as given.
- **Bertrand Paradox**: Economic finding that in price competition with identical products and no capacity constraints, duopolists drive prices down to marginal cost.
- **Nash Equilibrium**: A stable state in game theory where no player can benefit by unilaterally changing their chosen strategy.
