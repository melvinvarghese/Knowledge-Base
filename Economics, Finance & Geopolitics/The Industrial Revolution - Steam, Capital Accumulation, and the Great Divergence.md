# The Industrial Revolution - Steam, Capital Accumulation, and the Great Divergence

## Overview
The Industrial Revolution (beginning in Britain in the mid-18th century) was the fundamental transition from agrarian handicraft economies to machine-driven, fossil-fueled factory manufacturing.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Technological Convergence & High-Wage Energy Nexus: Robert Allen's economic thesis demonstrates that the Industrial Revolution originated in Britain because Britain uniquely possessed **high wages and exceptionally cheap energy** (accessible coal deposits). This wage-price ratio made it economically profitable for British entrepreneurs to substitute capital and fossil fuel energy for human labor, incentivizing key breakthroughs: Thomas Newcomen and James Watt's steam engine, James Hargreaves' spinning jenny, and Richard Arkwright's water frame. The mechanization of textiles, coal-powered metallurgy (Bessemer steel), and steam railways unleashed unprecedented, compounding total factor productivity (TFP) growth, escaping the Malthusian trap.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
The Great Divergence: Kenneth Pomeranz (*The Great Divergence*) analyzed why Western Europe pulled ahead of advanced Asian economies (the Yangtze Delta of China, Mughal India) that possessed comparable market institutions. Europe pulled ahead through: (1) Geographic proximity to dense coal beds; (2) Access to the ghost acreage of the Americas, providing sugar, cotton, and timber via colonial exploitation.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Transformed world economic history from millennia of stagnant per-capita GDP into modern exponential economic growth, birthing the urban working class.

## Key Terminology
- **The Great Divergence**: Historical process whereby Western industrial economies dramatically surpassed previously rich Asian economies in per-capita wealth.
- **Malthusian Trap**: Historical condition where technological and agricultural productivity gains resulted solely in population growth rather than rising living standards.
- **Total Factor Productivity (TFP)**: The portion of economic output growth not explained by traditionally measured inputs of labor and physical capital, reflecting technological progress.
