# Stagflation and the 1970s Oil Shocks - Supply Crises and Policy Paralysis

## Overview
Stagflation describes the simultaneous occurrence of stagnant economic growth, high unemployment, and high inflation—a combination previously thought impossible under traditional Keynesian economics.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
The Breakdown of the Phillips Curve: Classical Keynesian macroeconomic models assumed a stable inverse tradeoff between inflation and unemployment. The 1973 OPEC oil embargo (following the Yom Kippur War) and the 1979 Iranian Revolution caused dramatic negative aggregate supply shocks: crude oil prices quadrupled, shifting the short-run aggregate supply (SRAS) curve leftward. This simultaneously reduced real GDP output and spiked the price level, shattering the simplistic Phillips Curve trade-off and confounding fiscal policymakers who could not stimulate demand without exacerbating inflation.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
The Volcker Disinflation & Policy Resolution: Federal Reserve Chairman Paul Volcker initiated aggressive monetarist policy between 1979 and 1982, jacking the federal funds rate above 20%. This induced a severe back-to-back recession (1980, 1981-82) but successfully broke entrenched inflationary expectations, establishing central bank credibility and independent inflation targeting.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Highlights the severe vulnerability of industrialized economies to commodity and energy chokepoints, inspiring the strategic petroleum reserve (SPR) and domestic energy security frameworks.

## Key Terminology
- **Stagflation**: Economic condition characterized by stagnant economic growth, high unemployment, and elevated inflation.
- **Supply Shock**: An unexpected event that suddenly changes the supply of a commodity or service, shifting the aggregate supply curve.
- **Volcker Disinflation**: Aggressive monetary tightening by Fed Chairman Paul Volcker that curtailed 1970s inflation at the expense of a sharp recession.
