# Comparative Advantage and Ricardian Trade Theory

## Overview
David Ricardo's Theory of Comparative Advantage (1817) mathematically proves that international trade benefits all participating nations even if one nation has an absolute advantage in every good.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Mathematical Proof & Opportunity Cost: Suppose Nation A requires 10 hours to produce Wine and 20 hours for Cloth, while Nation B requires 15 hours for Wine and 15 hours for Cloth. While Nation A holds an absolute advantage in wine, Nation B has a lower opportunity cost in cloth ($15/15 = 1 \text{ wine}$ vs Nation A's $20/10 = 2 \text{ wine}$). By specializing according to lower opportunity costs, total world production of both goods increases strictly.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Free Trade vs Protectionism: Ricardo used this principle to successfully argue against the British Corn Laws (tariffs on imported grain), proving that protectionist tariffs protect domestic land rents while impoverishing consumers and shrinking aggregate output.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Pillar of modern international trade agreements (WTO, NAFTA), justification for globalized supply chain specialization.

## Key Terminology
- **Comparative Advantage**: Ability of an economy to produce a particular good or service at a lower opportunity cost than its trading partners.
- **Opportunity Cost**: Value of the best alternative forgone when making a decision between competing options.
- **Corn Laws**: Protectionist tariffs on imported grain enforced in the United Kingdom between 1815 and 1846.
