# The Middle-Income Trap - Wage Pressures, Industrial Upgrading, and Growth Stagnation

## Overview
The Middle-Income Trap is an economic development phenomenon where a rapidly growing low-income developing country reaches middle-income status, but loses momentum and stagnates before reaching high-income parity.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Mechanics & The Squeeze of Comparative Advantage: Introduced by World Bank economists Indermit Gill and Homi Kharas (2007). In initial development stages, poor countries achieve rapid growth through capital accumulation and shifting surplus rural agricultural labor into low-wage, labor-intensive manufacturing (Lewis turning point). As living standards rise, domestic wages increase. Consequently, the country is **squeezed between two competitive forces**: (1) It loses cost competitiveness against low-wage emerging producers (e.g., Vietnam, Bangladesh) in basic commodity manufacturing; (2) It lacks the advanced domestic R&D, patent output, human capital, and strong institutional protections needed to compete against high-income frontier innovators (US, Germany, Japan, South Korea).

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Escaping the Trap: Very few nations escape: of 101 middle-income economies in 1960, only 13 (including South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, and Israel) transitioned into high-income status by 2008. Overcoming the trap requires structural institutional reforms: transitioning from factor accumulation to Total Factor Productivity (TFP) innovation, deep capital market liberalization, and anti-corruption rule of law.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Central to contemporary debates surrounding China's long-term growth trajectory amid demographic decline, debt overhangs, and middle-income transition.

## Key Terminology
- **Middle-Income Trap**: Economic stagnation experienced by developing economies when they attain middle-income status and struggle to transition to high-income innovation.
- **Lewis Turning Point**: Point at which surplus rural agricultural labor is fully absorbed into industrial manufacturing, forcing wages to begin rising.
- **Factor Accumulation**: Economic growth driven solely by increasing the quantity of capital and labor inputs rather than by technological and efficiency improvements.
