# Central Banking and Monetary Policy - Interest Rates, Reserve Ratios, and QE

## Overview
Central Banks manage a nation's fiat currency, money supply, and credit conditions through monetary policy to achieve price stability and maximum employment.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Conventional Policy Tools: 1. Benchmark Interest Rates (e.g., Federal Funds Rate: the rate at which commercial banks lend reserves overnight); 2. Reserve Requirements (mandatory cash reserves banks must hold against deposits); 3. Open Market Operations (buying and selling government bonds to adjust commercial bank liquidity).

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Unconventional Policy - Quantitative Easing (QE): When short-term interest rates hit the Zero Lower Bound (ZLB), central banks engage in QE—creating electronic reserves to purchase hundreds of billions of dollars in long-term government bonds and mortgage-backed securities, lowering long-term borrowing yields and expanding central bank balance sheets.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Lender of last resort during financial panics (2008 Lehman collapse, 2020 COVID shock), inflation targeting frameworks (2% standard).

## Key Terminology
- **Quantitative Easing (QE)**: Monetary policy where a central bank purchases longer-term securities to inject liquidity directly into the economy.
- **Zero Lower Bound**: Macroeconomic situation where short-term nominal interest rates reach zero, limiting central bank conventional rate cuts.
- **Lender of Last Resort**: Institution providing liquidity to solvent financial institutions facing sudden liquidity runs.
