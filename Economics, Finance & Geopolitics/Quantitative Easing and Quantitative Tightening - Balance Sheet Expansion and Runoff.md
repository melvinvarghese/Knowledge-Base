# Quantitative Easing and Quantitative Tightening - Balance Sheet Expansion and Runoff

## Overview
Quantitative Easing (QE) and Quantitative Tightening (QT) are unconventional monetary policy operations involving large-scale open-market balance sheet expansion and contraction by central banks.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Mechanics of QE: When benchmark policy rates reach the effective lower bound (ELB), central banks purchase long-term government bonds, agency mortgage-backed securities (MBS), and corporate debt directly from commercial banks. The central bank credits commercial bank reserve accounts with newly created digital central bank money. This lowers long-term bond yields (flattening the yield curve), compresses term premia, spurs the portfolio balance channel (forcing investors into higher-risk equities and corporate debt), and provides liquidity backstops.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Mechanics of QT: When inflation risks emerge, central banks reverse this process via QT: allowing maturing securities to 'roll off' without reinvesting principal (passive QT) or actively selling bonds on the open market. This drains reserves, steepens yields, and tightens broader financial conditions.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Adopted by the Bank of Japan in 2001, the Federal Reserve post-2008 and 2020, and the ECB. Heavily criticized for inflating equity/real estate asset bubbles and increasing wealth inequality.

## Key Terminology
- **Portfolio Balance Channel**: Mechanism whereby central bank asset purchases remove safe bonds, forcing private investors into riskier assets.
- **Quantitative Tightening (QT)**: Monetary contraction process where a central bank shrinks its balance sheet by letting bonds mature without reinvestment.
- **Term Premium**: Excess yield required by investors for committing capital to a long-term bond rather than rolling over short-term instruments.
