# Whiteness Studies

## Overview
Whiteness studies examines whiteness as a racial identity — not a natural category but a historically constructed, socially maintained, and institutionally privileged position that confers advantages while remaining largely invisible to those who inhabit it.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
W.E.B. Du Bois introduced the 'wages of whiteness' concept — white workers receive a psychological wage of whiteness (solidarity with elites, deference from Black workers) that divides the working class along racial lines, preventing interracial solidarity. David Roediger's 'The Wages of Whiteness' (1991) historicized this: 19th-century Irish and other European immigrant workers became 'white' by distinguishing themselves from Black workers, gaining racial status in exchange for allegiance to racial hierarchy.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Peggy McIntosh's 'Invisible Knapsack' essay popularized the concept of white privilege — the invisible set of unearned assets conferring advantages to white people in housing, employment, education, and criminal justice encounters. Ruth Frankenberg's ethnography of white women showed how white racial identity is simultaneously everywhere (structuring social life) and nowhere (unmarked, seen as the neutral norm against which 'race' is defined). Whiteness is naturalized through its invisibility — it is simply 'normal'.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Critical whiteness studies examines how white identity is reproduced through housing segregation, school funding mechanisms, wealth accumulation through redlining and homeownership, and colorblind ideologies that prevent recognition of white advantage. It addresses the political mobilization of white identity in response to civil rights gains and demographic change, the sociology of white nationalism, and debates about whether white workers can be organized across racial lines for progressive class politics.

## Key Terminology
- **Wages of Whiteness**: Du Bois's concept of the psychological and material benefits accruing to white workers from racial hierarchy
- **White Privilege**: McIntosh's concept of the unearned advantages available to white people in a racist social system
- **Racial Identity**: The social and psychological sense of belonging to a racial group
- **Invisible Knapsack**: McIntosh's metaphor for the invisible set of privileges carried by white people
- **Redlining**: Discriminatory practice denying mortgages and services to Black and minority urban neighborhoods
- **White Normativity**: The treatment of whiteness as the unmarked standard against which other races are defined
