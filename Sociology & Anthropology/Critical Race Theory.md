# Critical Race Theory

## Overview
Critical race theory (CRT) is a legal and scholarly movement arguing that racism is not aberrational or individual but is endemic to American law and social institutions, shaping outcomes through facially neutral policies and colorblind ideologies that perpetuate racial subordination.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Developed by legal scholars including Derrick Bell, Kimberlé Crenshaw, and Mari Matsuda in the late 1980s, CRT challenged the limits of civil rights reform: despite formal legal equality, racial inequality persists and intensifies. Bell's 'interest convergence' thesis argued that racial progress for Black Americans occurs only when it also advances white interests — desegregation served Cold War interests in projecting American democracy; civil rights were not driven by moral awakening but by strategic necessity.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
CRT developed key concepts: colorblindness as ideology (the denial of race's continuing relevance perpetuates inequality while precluding recognition); the property function of whiteness (Harris: whiteness as a legally protected form of property conferring privilege in housing, education, and employment); counter-storytelling (using narrative and lived experience to challenge dominant legal narratives that erase the reality of discrimination). Intersectionality emerged from CRT to analyze overlapping systems of oppression.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
CRT has expanded beyond law into education, sociology, and public health, examining how systemic racism operates in schools, healthcare, housing, and criminal justice. It has become politically polarizing in the United States, with conservative legislators banning CRT from school curricula — often targeting any discussion of structural racism. Scholars argue this suppression itself demonstrates CRT's core claim about the institutional protection of white supremacy through law.

## Key Terminology
- **Critical Race Theory**: A legal and scholarly framework analyzing racism as endemic to American law and institutions
- **Interest Convergence**: Bell's thesis that racial progress occurs only when it converges with dominant group interests
- **Colorblindness Ideology**: The denial of race's continuing relevance, which legitimates persistent racial inequalities
- **Whiteness as Property**: Harris's argument that whiteness functions as a legally protected form of property
- **Counter-Storytelling**: CRT method using narrative to challenge dominant accounts that erase racial experience
- **Systemic Racism**: Racial inequality embedded in social institutions, laws, and practices rather than individual prejudice
