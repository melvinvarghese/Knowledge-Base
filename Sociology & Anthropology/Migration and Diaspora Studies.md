# Migration and Diaspora Studies

## Overview
Migration studies examines the causes, patterns, experiences, and consequences of human movement — voluntary and forced, internal and international — and the social, cultural, and political formations of diaspora communities living between multiple national spaces.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Classical migration theories (push-pull, neoclassical economics) modeled migration as individual rational responses to wage differentials between origin and destination countries. New economics of migration (Stark) shifted focus to household-level risk management strategies. World-systems theory (Sassen, Wallerstein) emphasized structural causes: labor demand in core countries, historical colonial ties generating migration corridors, and capitalist disruption of peripheral economies as root causes.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Transnationalism (Glick Schiller, Basch) describes migrants who maintain simultaneous, meaningful social relationships across national borders — sending remittances, participating in home country politics, maintaining dual cultural identities, and building transnational social fields. This challenged assimilationist models that expected migrants to progressively shed origin culture. Stuart Hall and Paul Gilroy analyzed the Black Atlantic as a transnational culture produced through the experience of slavery, diaspora, and cultural mixing.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Refugee and forced migration studies examines the experiences of those displaced by war, persecution, climate change, and development projects. The 1951 Refugee Convention established protection norms that contemporary border regimes increasingly circumvent. Deportation studies (De Genova) analyzes how state enforcement produces 'deportability' — the structural threat of expulsion that disciplines undocumented migrant labor. Contemporary migration crises reveal tensions between national sovereignty, human rights, and global justice.

## Key Terminology
- **Transnationalism**: The maintenance of meaningful social ties and identities across multiple nation-state borders
- **Diaspora**: A dispersed population sharing ethnic, cultural, or national origin scattered across multiple countries
- **Push-Pull Theory**: Migration theory attributing movement to repelling factors at origin and attracting factors at destination
- **Remittances**: Money sent by migrants to families in their country of origin
- **Deportability**: De Genova's concept of the structural threat of deportation that disciplines undocumented workers
- **Transmigrant**: A migrant who maintains regular and sustained ties to both origin and destination countries
