# Social Movements and Collective Action

## Overview
Social movement theory examines how groups of people organize collective action to challenge or defend social, political, or cultural arrangements, analyzing the conditions, processes, resources, and cultural frames that enable or inhibit sustained mobilization.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Resource mobilization theory (McCarthy, Zald) shifted focus from grievances (always present) to organizational resources — money, networks, skills, media access — that determine whether movements form and succeed. Political process (opportunity) theory (McAdam, Tarrow) emphasized how changing political contexts (opening and closing of opportunity structures) shape the viability of collective action. Cultural approaches added attention to 'framing processes' — how movement actors construct interpretive frames that diagnose problems, propose solutions, and motivate action.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
New social movement theory (Touraine, Melucci) argued that post-industrial movements (feminism, ecology, peace, LGBTQ+) differ from labor movements: they contest not just economic resources but the production of social identities, cultural codes, and ways of life. Identity is both a product and a resource of movement participation. Contemporary movements (Black Lives Matter, #MeToo, climate justice, Occupy) operate across digital platforms, raising questions about online mobilization, leaderless networks, and sustainability.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Research on movement outcomes asks when and why movements achieve policy change, cultural transformation, or repression. Intersectionality has entered social movement analysis, examining how race, gender, and class shape participation, strategy, and leadership within movements. The sociology of countermovement and backlash examines how dominant groups respond to movement challenges.

## Key Terminology
- **Resource Mobilization**: The theory that movement success depends on acquiring and organizing resources
- **Political Opportunity Structure**: The features of political institutions and alignments that facilitate or constrain collective action
- **Framing**: Snow and Benford's concept of how movements construct interpretive packages to motivate participation
- **Repertoire of Contention**: Tarrow's term for the established forms of collective action available to movements
- **New Social Movements**: Post-industrial movements contesting cultural codes and identities rather than economic resources
- **Identity Politics**: Political mobilization around shared identity characteristics such as race, gender, or sexuality
