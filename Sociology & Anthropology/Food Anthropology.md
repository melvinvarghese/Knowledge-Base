# Food Anthropology

## Overview
Food anthropology examines the cultural meanings, social functions, production systems, and political economies of food, arguing that what, how, with whom, and when we eat is never merely nutritional but encodes identity, power, morality, and cosmology.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Claude Lévi-Strauss's 'culinary triangle' (raw/cooked/rotten) proposed that cooking is a universal mediator of nature and culture — a symbolic transformation that distinguishes humans from animals. Mary Douglas's analysis of biblical food taboos showed they are not arbitrary but encode the taxonomy of a cosmological system: forbidden animals are anomalous, violating the classificatory boundaries of Israelite cosmology (Leviticus). These structuralist analyses revealed food as a language encoding cultural categories.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Food functions as a marker of identity and boundary: dietary laws (halal, kosher, vegetarianism) define religious communities; regional and national cuisines signal cultural belonging; class distinctions are inscribed in taste hierarchies (fine dining vs. fast food). Commensality — eating together — creates social bonds and hierarchies simultaneously, as who eats with whom, in what order, and what is shared encodes social relationships. Taboos separate edible from inedible, often along lines of purity and pollution (Douglas).

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Political economy of food examines how global agribusiness, food sovereignty movements, land grabbing, commodity speculation, and climate change intersect to shape who eats what. Food deserts (poor urban areas lacking fresh food) reveal food access as a dimension of social inequality. The anthropology of hunger examines not just caloric deficiency but the social suffering of food insecurity. Food activism (slow food, local food, seed sovereignty) contests corporate food systems.

## Key Terminology
- **Culinary Triangle**: Lévi-Strauss's model of raw/cooked/rotten as a cultural grammar of food preparation
- **Commensality**: The social practice of eating together, creating and maintaining social bonds
- **Food Taboo**: A cultural prohibition on eating certain foods, often encoding cosmological categories
- **Food Sovereignty**: The right of peoples to define their own food systems and agricultural policies
- **Food Desert**: An area lacking affordable, nutritious food options, typically in poor urban neighborhoods
- **Purity and Danger**: Douglas's framework analyzing pollution taboos as violations of classificatory boundaries
