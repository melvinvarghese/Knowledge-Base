# Kinship, Marriage and Family Anthropology

## Overview
Kinship anthropology examines the systems of social relationships based on descent (parent-child) and affinity (marriage) that organize group membership, inheritance, succession, residence, and alliance across human societies, revealing kinship as simultaneously biological, social, and cultural.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Morgan's pioneering classification of kinship terminologies (1871) identified six basic systems (Eskimo, Hawaiian, Iroquois, Crow, Omaha, Sudanese) that encode different principles of classification — distinguishing parallel from cross cousins, bifurcating or merging maternal and paternal kin. Descent rules (patrilineal, matrilineal, bilateral, double) determine which relatives constitute one's social group for inheritance and collective identity. Residence rules (patrilocal, matrilocal, neolocal) determine where couples live after marriage.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Alliance theory (Lévi-Strauss) analyzed marriage not as an individual or family matter but as the exchange of women between groups, creating alliances and social solidarity. Incest taboos force marriage outside one's immediate group, generating the circulation of women (and gifts) that creates broader social networks. The 'elementary structures' of kinship (prescriptive marriage with cross-cousin) and 'complex structures' (prohibitive only) represent different logics of social reproduction.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Contemporary kinship studies have been transformed by new reproductive technologies, same-sex parenting, adoption, global surrogacy, and chosen families, revealing kinship as cultural construction rather than natural fact. Feminist kinship anthropologists challenged Lévi-Strauss's exchange theory as presupposing women's subordination. David Schneider's critique argued American kinship is built on 'biogenetic substance' — an ideology, not a natural fact — opening space for analysis of kinship making in diverse contexts.

## Key Terminology
- **Descent**: Rules determining group membership through parent-child links, either patri- or matrilineal
- **Alliance Theory**: Lévi-Strauss's analysis of marriage as exchange creating inter-group alliances
- **Kinship Terminology**: Systems of classification of relatives that encode social roles and group boundaries
- **Incest Taboo**: Universal prohibition on sexual relations with close kin, analyzed as generating social exchange
- **Cross-Cousin Marriage**: Marriage to the child of one's opposite-sex parent's sibling, common in many societies
- **New Kinship Studies**: Analysis of kinship making through adoption, donor conception, and chosen family
