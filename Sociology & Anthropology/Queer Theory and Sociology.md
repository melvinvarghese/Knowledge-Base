# Queer Theory and Sociology

## Overview
Queer theory, emerging from feminist and gay/lesbian studies in the early 1990s, destabilizes fixed categories of gender and sexuality, analyzes how heteronormativity structures social institutions and identities, and argues for the radical deconstruction of naturalized identity categories.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Judith Butler's 'Gender Trouble' (1990) argued that gender is not an expression of an inner biological or psychological essence but is performatively constituted through repeated citational acts — everyday gestures, speech, clothing, and social practices that cite and iterate gender norms. Gender is 'done' rather than 'being'. There is no original gender behind the performance — the performance is all there is. This denaturalizes the sex/gender distinction and opens space for understanding transgender, non-binary, and intersex experiences.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Heteronormativity (Warner) names the system that naturalizes heterosexuality as normal, natural, and expected — shaping institutions (marriage law, healthcare, housing, adoption), cultural representations (the assumed heterosexual couple in advertising, film, and legal form), and everyday interaction (the assumption of opposite-sex partnership). It operates not through overt coercion but through the structuring of social institutions that make heterosexual coupling the unmarked default and anything else deviant, invisible, or pathological.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Intersectional queer theory (Crenshaw, Collins, Muñoz) addresses the whiteness of mainstream LGBTQ+ politics and the class and race dimensions of queer experience. Crip theory (McRuer) queers ability norms as well as sexuality norms, analyzing compulsory able-bodiedness. Contemporary queer sociology examines LGBTQ+ family formations, the homonormalization of queer culture (Puar's homonationalism), trans sociology, and anti-LGBTQ+ politics in global context.

## Key Terminology
- **Queer Theory**: A theoretical approach denaturalizing fixed gender and sexuality categories and analyzing heteronormativity
- **Gender Performativity**: Butler's concept that gender is enacted through repeated citational acts rather than expressing inner essence
- **Heteronormativity**: The system naturalizing heterosexuality as the normal and expected form of sexuality and intimate life
- **Homonationalism**: Puar's concept of LGBTQ+ identity politics incorporated into nationalist and imperial frameworks
- **Compulsory Heterosexuality**: Rich's concept of the institutional enforcement of heterosexual orientation on women
- **Cisnormativity**: The assumption that people's gender identity always matches the sex assigned to them at birth
