# Family Structures and Kinship

## Overview
The sociology of family examines the diverse forms, functions, and transformations of family life — from nuclear to extended, from patriarchal to egalitarian, from biological to chosen — and how family structures are shaped by and reproduce social inequalities of class, gender, race, and sexuality.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Classical sociology viewed the nuclear family (breadwinner father, homemaker mother, children) as functional for industrial capitalism — the family socializes children into normative roles, provides emotional support for workers, and reproduces labor power. Parsons identified expressive (mother) and instrumental (father) roles within this division. Feminist sociology challenged this as naturalizing women's subordination and ignoring the oppressive dimensions of family life (domestic violence, unpaid labor, reproductive coercion).

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Contemporary family sociology documents profound transformations: rising divorce rates, single-parent households, same-sex families, cohabitation without marriage, blended families, childfree adults, and multigenerational households. These changes reflect shifting gender norms, economic restructuring (women's labor force participation), legal changes (same-sex marriage), and cultural individualization. Cross-cultural comparison reveals enormous diversity: polygyny, polyandry, communal child-rearing, and matrilineal household structures challenge ethnocentric assumptions about 'the family'.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Care sociology examines who performs care work (childcare, eldercare, care for the sick) — predominantly women, often unpaid or low-paid — and how care is distributed across families, markets, and states. The 'care crisis' in developed societies reflects aging populations, cuts to public care services, and women's increased labor force participation without corresponding redistribution of domestic labor to men or the state.

## Key Terminology
- **Nuclear Family**: The household unit of two parents and their children, idealized in Western industrial societies
- **Extended Family**: A kinship unit including multiple generations and collateral relatives living together
- **Care Work**: Labor involved in caring for children, the elderly, and the sick, disproportionately performed by women
- **Domestic Labor**: Unpaid household work including cooking, cleaning, and childcare
- **Blended Family**: A family formed through remarriage or cohabitation that includes children from previous relationships
- **Family Diversity**: The wide range of family forms recognized in contemporary sociology and cross-cultural research
