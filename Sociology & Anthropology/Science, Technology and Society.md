# Science, Technology and Society

## Overview
Science and technology studies (STS) examines science and technology as social and cultural phenomena — how scientific knowledge is produced in laboratories and social settings, how technological artifacts embody social values, and how scientific claims achieve or lose credibility in society.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
David Bloor's strong programme in the sociology of knowledge argued that sociological explanations should apply symmetrically to successful and failed scientific claims alike — not privileging 'true' science as simply reading nature correctly, but analyzing the social conditions enabling some claims to gain acceptance over others. Harry Collins's empirical programme of relativism examined scientific controversies — the detection of gravitational waves, cold fusion, parapsychology — as sites where the social negotiation of credibility is most visible.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Laboratory studies (Latour and Woolgar, 'Laboratory Life', 1979; Knorr-Cetina) opened the black box of scientific practice through ethnographic observation: scientists are shown to be engaged in 'inscription devices', rhetorical persuasion, and negotiation of interpretations — not simply reading nature but constructing facts through material-semiotic practices. Scientific facts are 'immutable mobiles' that hold together networks of humans, instruments, texts, and institutions spanning laboratories, journals, and policy arenas.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Public understanding of science research examines why people accept or reject scientific consensus (vaccination, climate change, evolution), finding that trust in institutions, cultural identity, and worldview are more predictive than scientific literacy. Citizen science, open science, and science communication efforts seek to democratize knowledge production. Feminist STS (Haraway, Keller) critiques gendered assumptions in scientific theories of nature and argues for 'situated knowledge' — acknowledging the partial, embodied character of all scientific claims.

## Key Terminology
- **Strong Programme**: Bloor's sociological approach analyzing all scientific claims symmetrically regardless of their truth
- **Laboratory Ethnography**: Ethnographic study of scientific practice as social and material knowledge production
- **Immutable Mobile**: Latour's concept of inscriptions that travel across networks while maintaining their form
- **Situated Knowledge**: Haraway's feminist epistemology insisting all knowledge is partial, embodied, and located
- **Black Box**: A stabilized technological or scientific fact whose construction is no longer questioned
- **Inscription Device**: Latour's term for instruments that translate phenomena into permanent records and representations
