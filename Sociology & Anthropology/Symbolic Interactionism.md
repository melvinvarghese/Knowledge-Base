# Symbolic Interactionism

## Overview
Symbolic interactionism is a micro-sociological perspective that examines how individuals create shared meaning through social interaction, language, and symbols, and how self and society are mutually constructed through these ongoing processes.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
George Herbert Mead argued that the self emerges through social interaction: the 'I' (spontaneous self) is shaped in relation to the 'Me' (internalized attitudes of others). Children develop self-consciousness through play and game stages, learning to take the perspective of others and eventually the 'generalized other' — the organized attitudes of the community. Herbert Blumer formalized symbolic interactionism's core premises: humans act toward things based on the meanings those things have for them; meanings arise in social interaction; meanings are modified through interpretive processes.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Symbolic interactionism opened rich lines of research in identity formation, stigma (Goffman), labeling and deviance (Becker), medical sociology, and educational attainment (teacher expectations). Its strength is capturing the fluid, negotiated character of social reality and the micro-processes by which culture is reproduced. Critics note it neglects macrostructural forces — class, race, state — and may underplay how interactions are structured by power inequalities.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Erving Goffman extended symbolic interactionism through dramaturgical analysis (social life as theater), frame analysis (how situations are defined), and the study of stigma as a spoiled identity. Contemporary symbolic interactionist work addresses online identity, the sociology of emotions, and the micro-politics of everyday racism and sexism.

## Key Terminology
- **Symbolic Interactionism**: A micro-sociological perspective focused on meaning-making through social interaction
- **The Self**: Mead's concept of the reflexive self emerging through social experience and interaction
- **Generalized Other**: Mead's term for the community's organized attitudes internalized by the individual
- **Definition of the Situation**: W.I. Thomas' principle that situations defined as real have real consequences
- **Meaning**: Blumer's focus on how shared interpretations of symbols guide human action
- **Looking-Glass Self**: Cooley's concept of self-image shaped by imagining how others see us
