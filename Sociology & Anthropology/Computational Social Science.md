# Computational Social Science

## Overview
Computational social science uses large-scale digital data, computational methods, and machine learning to study social phenomena at previously impossible scales, while grappling with questions of data validity, ethical access, platform power, and the relationship between computational and interpretive methods.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Digital trace data — Twitter posts, Facebook interactions, Google search queries, mobile phone records, Wikipedia edits — enable researchers to observe social behavior at massive scale without surveys or experiments. Network analysis maps friendship ties, information flows, and opinion diffusion across millions of nodes. Natural language processing extracts sentiment, topic, and frame from billions of texts. This empirical abundance enables studies of collective behavior, information epidemics, political polarization, and market dynamics at unprecedented resolution.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Social network analysis (SNA) has become central to computational social science: Watts and Strogatz's small-world networks showed that most pairs of nodes are connected through surprisingly few links; Barabási's scale-free networks documented power-law degree distributions (a few highly connected hubs dominate), explaining the viral spread of information and disease. Structural analysis of social networks (centrality, betweenness, clustering) reveals micro-level mechanisms of information diffusion, influence, and social capital.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Critical computational social science raises concerns about: platform-controlled data access (the 'API tax' limiting academic research); demographic biases in digitally generated data (not everyone tweets equally); the opacity of algorithmic classification; the ethics of analyzing behavioral data collected without research consent; and the risk of laundering social inequalities through seemingly objective mathematical models. Calls for 'algorithmic accountability', 'data feminism' (D'Ignazio, Klein), and science and technology studies engagement with computational methods reflect growing awareness of power and politics in data science.

## Key Terminology
- **Digital Trace Data**: Behavioral records produced as a by-product of digital activity, used for social research
- **Social Network Analysis**: Quantitative methods mapping and analyzing relationships and flows in social networks
- **Small World Network**: Watts and Strogatz's finding that most nodes are linked through surprisingly few connections
- **Scale-Free Network**: Barabási's finding that many networks have power-law degree distributions with hub nodes
- **Data Feminism**: D'Ignazio and Klein's approach integrating feminist principles into data collection and analysis
- **Algorithmic Accountability**: The demand for transparency and responsibility in consequential algorithmic decision-making systems
