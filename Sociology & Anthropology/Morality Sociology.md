# Morality Sociology

## Overview
The sociology of morality examines how moral beliefs, norms, and judgments are socially produced, shared, and contested, analyzing morality as a sociological phenomenon rather than a philosophical given — how moral orders vary across societies, how moral boundaries are drawn, and how moral emotions regulate social life.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Durkheim grounded morality in society: moral obligations are social facts, binding individuals through collective consciousness and enforced by ritual and sanction. The sacred/profane distinction is fundamentally a moral boundary — the sacred commands respect, awe, and protection; the profane is the domain of ordinary, unremarkable life. Weber analyzed value rationality — action oriented toward absolute values — alongside instrumental rationality as competing logics of modern moral life.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Moral boundary work (Lamont) examines how people draw distinctions between themselves and others based on moral, cultural, and socioeconomic criteria, reinforcing social hierarchies. Working-class Americans in Lamont's research prized hard work, integrity, and care for family as moral virtues distinguishing themselves from both wealthy elites (seen as dishonest and selfish) and poor minorities (seen as lazy and irresponsible). Moral boundary drawing is a key mechanism of class and racial differentiation.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Jonathan Haidt's moral foundations theory proposes cross-cultural moral universals: care/harm, fairness/reciprocity, loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion, sanctity/degradation, and liberty/oppression. Conservatives emphasize more foundations; liberals primarily care and fairness. This psychological framework is controversial in sociology — critics argue it naturalizes conservative morality and neglects the structural politics of moral claims. Pragmatist sociologies of morality (Boltanski, Thevenot) analyze how actors justify their actions by appealing to different 'orders of worth' or evaluative regimes.

## Key Terminology
- **Moral Boundary**: Distinctions drawn between worthy and unworthy people based on moral evaluations
- **Moral Order**: The shared system of values, norms, and sanctions governing right conduct in a community
- **Moral Foundations Theory**: Haidt's psychological framework proposing universal moral modules across cultures
- **Orders of Worth**: Boltanski and Thevenot's concept of distinct evaluative regimes used to justify actions
- **Moral Emotions**: Emotions (guilt, shame, indignation) motivating compliance with moral norms and sanctioning violation
- **Value Rationality**: Weber's concept of action oriented by commitment to absolute values regardless of consequences
