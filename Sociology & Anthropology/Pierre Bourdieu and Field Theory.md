# Pierre Bourdieu and Field Theory

## Overview
Pierre Bourdieu's field theory analyzes social life as a set of differentiated, relatively autonomous 'fields' in which agents compete for position using multiple forms of capital, guided by dispositions (habitus) that reflect their socialized class background.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
A 'field' is a structured space of positions with its own logic, rules, and stakes (academic, artistic, economic, political). Agents occupy positions based on the volume and composition of their capital: economic (money), cultural (credentials, knowledge, taste), social (networks), and symbolic (recognition, prestige). Habitus is the durable, embodied system of dispositions — ways of perceiving, thinking, and acting — acquired through early socialization that structures practice without mechanically determining it.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Bourdieu used field theory to analyze education in 'Reproduction in Education, Society and Culture' (with Passeron, 1977): schools reward and transmit the cultural capital of dominant classes, misrecognizing this advantage as academic merit and thereby reproducing class inequalities while appearing meritocratic. 'Distinction' (1979) mapped how cultural tastes (in music, food, art, sport) function as class markers, with working-class 'taste for necessity' contrasted to elite 'aesthetic disposition'.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Field theory is criticized for being potentially deterministic (habitus seems to reproduce class position with little room for agency), for difficulties in operationalizing concepts empirically, and for underweighting resistance and social change. Nevertheless, Bourdieu's framework has become indispensable in education sociology, cultural sociology, organizational analysis, and political sociology.

## Key Terminology
- **Habitus**: The embodied system of durable dispositions that shape perceptions, judgments, and practices
- **Field**: A structured social space with its own rules, positions, and forms of capital
- **Cultural Capital**: Bourdieu's term for knowledge, skills, and tastes valued in dominant culture
- **Social Capital**: Resources derived from durable networks of relationships and social recognition
- **Symbolic Violence**: The imposition of categories of perception and valuation that naturalize domination
- **Distinction**: Bourdieu's concept of how cultural preferences mark and reproduce social class
