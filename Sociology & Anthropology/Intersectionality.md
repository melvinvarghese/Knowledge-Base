# Intersectionality

## Overview
Intersectionality, coined by legal scholar Kimberlé Crenshaw (1989), holds that systems of oppression — race, gender, class, sexuality, disability — overlap and interact, producing qualitatively different experiences of discrimination that cannot be understood by analyzing any single axis in isolation.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Crenshaw developed the concept to address how Black women's experiences of employment discrimination were invisible in legal frameworks that treated race and sex as separate categories. A Black woman's experience was not simply the sum of racism and sexism but their intersection, creating unique vulnerabilities. Intersectional analysis revealed that universal categories ('woman', 'Black') flatten the diversity of experience and can serve interests of more privileged group members.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Intersectionality has expanded beyond law into sociology, political science, public health, and education. Research demonstrates how intersecting identities shape access to healthcare (Black maternal mortality), criminal justice outcomes (Black women in prison), immigration experiences, and workplace discrimination. Policy intersectionality analyzes how laws targeting one axis may disadvantage those multiply marginalized.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Critics within sociology debate whether intersectionality is best understood as a theoretical framework, a methodological approach, or an empirical claim about identities. Some criticize it as proliferating oppression categories without adequate theory of their articulation. Others argue it has been domesticated in academic and corporate diversity initiatives, losing its radical, structural critique of systemic inequality.

## Key Terminology
- **Intersectionality**: A framework analyzing how multiple systems of oppression interact to shape experience
- **Matrix of Domination**: Patricia Hill Collins' concept of interlocking systems of oppression in social structures
- **Structural Intersectionality**: How intersecting social positions shape vulnerability to discriminatory systems
- **Political Intersectionality**: How multiply marginalized groups are overlooked in single-issue political movements
- **Representational Intersectionality**: How cultural images erase or distort the experiences of multiply marginalized people
- **Standpoint Epistemology**: A view that social location shapes the knowledge one can produce
