# Religion and Society

## Overview
The sociology of religion examines how religious beliefs, practices, and institutions relate to social order, identity, legitimation, conflict, and change — from Durkheim's functionalism to Weber's historical comparative sociology to contemporary debates over secularization and religious revival.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Durkheim argued religion is ultimately about society worshipping itself through the medium of sacred symbols — the totemic emblem represents collective social force projected onto a sacred object. Shared religious rituals generate collective effervescence and social solidarity. Weber's comparative sociology traced how different religious traditions shaped economic and political development: the Protestant Ethic's elective affinity with capitalism's spirit; Confucian rationalism's compatibility with Chinese political order; Indian caste dharma's barriers to capitalist accumulation.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
The secularization thesis (Wilson, Berger) predicted that modernization would progressively marginalize religion through differentiation (religion losing functions to other institutions), rationalization (science displacing religious worldviews), and privatization (religion retreating to private life). But global religious revitalization — Pentecostal growth in Africa and Latin America, Islamic revival, Hindu nationalism, American religious conservatism — led Berger to revise the thesis: secularization is an exception, Europe is the deviant case, the world remains 'as furiously religious as ever'.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Contemporary sociology of religion examines megachurches and market models of religion, digital religion and online communities of faith, religious nationalism, Islamophobia, conversion, religious diaspora, healing and prosperity gospels, and the intersection of religion with gender, sexuality, race, and class. 'Nones' (the religiously unaffiliated) are the fastest-growing religious category in developed societies, raising questions about secular spirituality, moral communities, and meaning-making.

## Key Terminology
- **Secularization**: The process by which religion loses social significance as societies modernize
- **Protestant Ethic**: Weber's concept of Calvinist doctrines fostering attitudes conducive to capitalist development
- **Sacred**: Durkheim's category for objects, persons, and places set apart as extraordinary and inviolable
- **Religious Pluralism**: The coexistence of diverse religious traditions within a society
- **Civil Religion**: Bellah's concept of quasi-religious beliefs and rituals surrounding national identity
- **Disenchantment**: Weber's concept of the removal of magical and sacred elements from the world through rationalization
