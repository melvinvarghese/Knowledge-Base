# Digital Society and Technology

## Overview
The sociology of technology examines how technological systems — from industrial machinery to digital platforms and AI — are shaped by and in turn shape social relations, inequalities, power structures, and ways of life, rejecting both technological determinism and social essentialism.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Science and technology studies (STS) demonstrated that technologies are not neutral tools but social constructs: they embody values, assumptions, and social interests. Winner's 'Do Artifacts Have Politics?' (1980) showed Robert Moses's deliberately low-clearance bridges excluding buses (and therefore poor and Black New Yorkers) from Jones Beach — a canonical example of built-in social bias. SCOT (Social Construction of Technology) analyzes how relevant social groups contest technological design, with eventual 'closure' around stabilized meanings.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Algorithmic systems now mediate consequential decisions in hiring, lending, parole, medical diagnosis, and content recommendation. Research reveals systematic racial, gender, and class biases in facial recognition (higher error rates for darker-skinned women), natural language processing, and predictive policing algorithms — reflecting training data biases and design choices by predominantly white, male engineering teams. The 'black-boxing' of algorithms obscures accountability for discriminatory outcomes.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Automation anxiety centers on whether intelligent machines will displace human labor at unprecedented scale and speed. Historical evidence suggests technological unemployment is temporary — new jobs emerge — but the transition costs are unevenly distributed (falling disproportionately on less-educated, minority workers in routine cognitive and physical tasks). Platformization (Uber, Amazon) restructures work through algorithmic management, surveillance, and performance metrics, extending market discipline into previously non-market social relations.

## Key Terminology
- **SCOT**: Social Construction of Technology: the approach analyzing how social groups shape technological design
- **Actor-Network Theory**: Latour's approach treating technological artifacts as active participants in social networks
- **Algorithmic Bias**: Systematic errors in algorithmic systems reflecting biases in training data or design choices
- **Platformization**: The restructuring of economic and social life around digital platform infrastructures
- **Black-Boxing**: The concealment of a technology's internal workings, limiting accountability and transparency
- **Technological Determinism**: The view that technology causes social change independently of human decisions and social forces
