# Poststructuralism and Sociology

## Overview
Poststructuralism, drawing on Foucault, Derrida, and Deleuze, challenges the stable structures of meaning, identity, and social order posited by structuralism, emphasizing the instability of signification, the productivity of power-knowledge, and the discursive constitution of subjects.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Michel Foucault rejected the idea of a sovereign subject whose knowledge reveals objective truth. Instead, knowledge is constituted within historically specific regimes of truth — 'discourse' — that determine what counts as truth, who can speak authoritatively, and what objects of knowledge can exist. His archaeological and genealogical methods traced how modern institutions (prison, clinic, school, psychiatry) produce docile, self-regulating subjects through surveillance, normalization, and disciplinary practices rather than simply suppressing a pre-existing subject.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Power, for Foucault, is not a possession wielded by rulers over subjects but a productive force circulating through social relations, constituting both subjects and knowledge. Biopower describes modern states' management of life and population through statistics, public health, sexuality regulation, and demographic techniques. This framework opened new approaches to the sociology of health, sexuality, education, and penality.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Sociologists have adopted poststructuralist tools selectively: discourse analysis (Fairclough, Laclau, Mouffe), post-Foucauldian governmentality studies, and queer theory draw on these frameworks while remaining attentive to material inequalities. Critics charge poststructuralism with overemphasizing discourse, neglecting economic structures, and producing politically paralysing relativism.

## Key Terminology
- **Discourse**: Foucault's term for systematically structured fields of knowledge and practice
- **Power-Knowledge**: Foucault's concept of the inseparability of power and knowledge in producing truth
- **Discipline**: Foucault's analysis of normalization through surveillance, examination, and hierarchical observation
- **Biopower**: Foucault's concept of modern state power operating through management of populations and life
- **Governmentality**: Foucault's concept of rationalities and techniques through which populations are governed
- **Normalization**: The production and enforcement of norms that evaluate and regulate individual conduct
