# Body Sociology

## Overview
The sociology of the body examines how the physical body is not a pre-social biological given but is shaped, inscribed, regulated, and experienced through social processes — how culture, power, and social structure are literally embodied in posture, appearance, illness, aging, and sensation.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Foucault's analysis of disciplinary power showed how bodies are trained, normalized, and monitored in modern institutions: prison, hospital, school, and barracks produce docile, useful bodies through regularized timetables, spatial organization, and examination. The body is a primary site of disciplinary technology — the soul is 'the prison of the body'. Bourdieu's habitus is embodied class: the practical sense of appropriate social action is inscribed in the hexis — bodily schema of posture, gesture, gait — through early socialization.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Feminist body sociology examines how the female body is subjected to greater surveillance, control, and aesthetic regulation than male bodies. Bordo's analysis of anorexia and bodily ideals showed how patriarchal culture produces gendered body disciplines — women's bodies are sites of intense normative pressure regarding weight, appearance, and deportment. The medicalization of women's bodies — from menstruation and childbirth to menopause and 'female sexual dysfunction' — extends medical authority over naturally variable processes.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Contemporary body sociology addresses: the body in consumer culture (cosmetic surgery, fitness culture, body modification as self-expression); health and embodied inequality (how poverty, racism, and work conditions are literally inscribed in body mass, disease, and life expectancy); trans and intersex bodies and the politics of medical gatekeeping; disability, chronic illness, and the phenomenology of impairment; and the biosociality of genomics — how genetic knowledge reshapes identity, kinship, and illness experience.

## Key Terminology
- **Embodiment**: The incorporation of social experience into the physical body through habitual practice
- **Bodily Hexis**: Bourdieu's concept of the durable postures and gestures through which habitus is physically expressed
- **Docile Body**: Foucault's concept of bodies rendered useful and obedient through disciplinary training
- **Body Projects**: Shilling's concept of the body as a malleable site for identity work and self-expression in modernity
- **Biosociality**: Rabinow's concept of new social identities and communities formed around genetic and biological categories
- **Somatic Society**: Turner's concept of a society in which major political and personal problems are expressed through the body
