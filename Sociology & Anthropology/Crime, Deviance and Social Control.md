# Crime, Deviance and Social Control

## Overview
The sociology of crime and deviance examines rule-breaking behavior in social context, analyzing who is defined as deviant, how social control is exercised, what structural conditions produce criminal behavior, and how punishment systems function to reproduce social order and inequality.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Sociological approaches to crime move beyond individual psychology to examine social structures: anomie/strain (crime as response to blocked opportunity), social disorganization (crime concentrated in economically disadvantaged, residentially unstable communities), labeling (crime defined by law and enforcement, not behavior), and conflict (crime law reflecting ruling class interests). Edwin Sutherland's differential association theory argued crime is learned in intimate groups — criminal values and techniques are transmitted culturally, not genetically.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Feminist criminology challenged male-biased models by examining women's pathways into crime (abuse, poverty, running from violence) and the gendered nature of victimization (domestic violence, sexual assault). Critical criminology (Taylor, Walton, Young) analyzed crime law and criminal justice as instruments of class power. Penal sociology examines how imprisonment functions — from rehabilitation fantasies to warehousing surplus populations — and its racialized application in the United States (mass incarceration).

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Contemporary criminology grapples with cybercrime, state crime, corporate crime ('crime in the suites'), genocide, terrorism, and climate crimes. The 'new penology' thesis (Feeley, Simon) argues that actuarial risk management has replaced rehabilitation as the dominant logic of criminal justice, targeting aggregate populations rather than reforming individuals. Abolition and transformative justice movements challenge the legitimacy and efficacy of incarceration itself.

## Key Terminology
- **Social Disorganization**: The theory linking crime rates to community instability, poverty, and weak social ties
- **Differential Association**: Sutherland's theory that criminal behavior is learned through social interaction
- **New Penology**: The shift in criminal justice toward actuarial risk management of dangerous populations
- **Corporate Crime**: Illegal acts committed by corporations or their executives in the course of business
- **Abolitionism**: The movement to end incarceration and transform social responses to harm
- **Recidivism**: The tendency of a convicted person to reoffend after release from prison
