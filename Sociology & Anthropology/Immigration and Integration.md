# Immigration and Integration

## Overview
Immigration sociology examines the processes by which migrants incorporate into host societies — or are prevented from doing so — analyzing models of assimilation and multiculturalism, the politics of immigration control, and the experience of living between cultures.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Classical assimilation theory (Park, Gordon) modeled immigrant integration as a linear, generational process of cultural and structural incorporation: first-generation immigrants maintain home culture; second generation becomes bicultural; third generation becomes indistinguishable from mainstream. This assimilationist model presupposed an Anglo-American norm and proved inadequate for non-white immigrants facing systematic racial exclusion regardless of cultural adaptation. Segmented assimilation theory (Portes, Zhou) proposed multiple trajectories: some immigrant groups assimilate upward into the middle class; others assimilate downward into inner-city poverty; yet others maintain 'selective acculturation' through strong ethnic community ties.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Multiculturalism as policy (Canada, UK, Netherlands) recognizes and accommodates cultural differences among immigrant groups rather than demanding cultural assimilation, raising democratic debates about national cohesion, group rights, and limits of tolerance. Critics from left and right argue that multiculturalism can entrench community boundaries, undermine cross-community solidarity, and fail to challenge structural discrimination. After the 2001 Oldham riots and 2004 Dutch film director van Gogh murder, several European states formally abandoned multiculturalism in favor of integration demands.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Contemporary immigration sociology addresses the global refugee crisis and asylum policy; undocumented migration and deportation regimes; the second generation and the 1.5 generation (childhood arrivals); immigrant health paradox (immigrants often healthier than native-born despite lower SES); religious diversity and accommodation (Muslim integration in Europe); and the political sociology of immigration opinion and party politics.

## Key Terminology
- **Assimilation**: The process by which immigrants adopt the culture and social practices of the host society
- **Segmented Assimilation**: Portes and Zhou's model of divergent integration trajectories for different immigrant groups
- **Multiculturalism**: A policy framework recognizing and accommodating cultural diversity among immigrant communities
- **Transnationalism**: The maintenance of meaningful ties and identities across national borders by migrants
- **Immigration Paradox**: The finding that recent immigrants often have better health outcomes than expected given their socioeconomic position
- **Nativism**: Political hostility to immigration based on nationalist or racial conceptions of national identity
