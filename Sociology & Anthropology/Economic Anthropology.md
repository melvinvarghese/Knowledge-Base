# Economic Anthropology

## Overview
Economic anthropology examines economic life — production, distribution, exchange, and consumption — across human societies, questioning whether Western formal economic theory is universally applicable or whether diverse economic institutions require distinct analytical frameworks.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
The formalist-substantivist debate (1960s) divided the field: formalists (Robbins, Firth) argued standard economic analysis (rational choice, optimization) applies universally to all resource allocation decisions. Substantivists (Polanyi, Dalton) argued the economy in pre-capitalist societies is 'embedded' in social institutions — kinship, religion, politics — and cannot be analyzed through market models. Karl Polanyi's 'The Great Transformation' (1944) traced the historical disembedding of markets from society in capitalist development as a 'double movement' of market expansion and protective counter-movement.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Polanyi identified three principles of economic integration: reciprocity (balanced exchange within social relationships), redistribution (centralized collection and re-distribution by a chief or state), and market exchange (price-driven transaction for profit). These are ideal types that coexist in all societies, though market exchange becomes dominant under capitalism. Primitive money, credit, debt, and investment have been found in pre-market societies, complicating simple contrasts.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Contemporary economic anthropology examines financialization (the penetration of financial logics into everyday life), debt (Graeber's 'Debt: The First 5,000 Years'), informal economies, labor migration, global commodity chains, and the anthropology of money. Research reveals how ostensibly universal economic categories (property, contract, money) encode specific cultural and power relations.

## Key Terminology
- **Embeddedness**: Polanyi's concept that pre-capitalist economies are integrated into social and cultural institutions
- **Reciprocity**: Polanyi's principle of balanced exchange based on symmetric social relationships
- **Redistribution**: Polanyi's principle of centralized collection and reallocation of resources by authority
- **Formalism**: The view that Western economic theory applies universally to all resource allocation decisions
- **Substantivism**: The view that diverse economic systems require culturally specific analytical frameworks
- **Moral Economy**: The normative expectations governing legitimate economic exchange in a community
