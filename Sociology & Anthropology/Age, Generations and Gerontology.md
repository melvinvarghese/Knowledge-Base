# Age, Generations and Gerontology

## Overview
The sociology of aging examines how age operates as a dimension of social stratification and identity, how aging processes are shaped by social inequalities, and how demographic shifts toward aging populations transform economies, families, and welfare states.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Life course sociology (Elder) examines how individual biographies unfold across time, shaped by historical context (cohort effects), linked lives (interdependence with family members), and timing of transitions (early or late parenthood, job entry, retirement). Mannheim's concept of generations argues that cohorts sharing formative historical experiences (Depression, World War II, the 1960s counterculture) develop distinctive political and cultural orientations — a framework applied (controversially) to Baby Boomers, Millennials, and Generation Z.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Ageism — prejudice and discrimination based on age, particularly against older people — operates through stereotypes (the senile, dependent, burdensome elderly), institutional practices (mandatory retirement, employment discrimination), and media representations. The biomedical framing of aging as decline contrasts with 'active aging' and 'successful aging' frameworks that promote autonomy and engagement. Critical gerontology (Estes) examines how the 'aging enterprise' — medical, pharmaceutical, and care industries — commodifies aging and constructs the elderly as a problem.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Demographic aging in developed societies poses fiscal challenges to pension and healthcare systems. The dependency ratio (workers supporting retirees) is shifting adversely as Baby Boomers retire. Cross-national comparison reveals that aging is experienced very differently depending on welfare state generosity, labor market integration, and cultural norms around filial support. Research on the 'fourth age' — severe dependency in advanced old age — challenges positive aging ideologies and raises hard questions about care, dignity, and end-of-life ethics.

## Key Terminology
- **Life Course**: The sequence of socially defined events and roles that individuals traverse over a lifetime
- **Cohort Effect**: The distinctive social experiences shared by those born in the same historical period
- **Ageism**: Discrimination and stereotyping against individuals based on their age
- **Gerontology**: The interdisciplinary study of aging processes and older persons
- **Dependency Ratio**: The ratio of non-working-age to working-age population in a society
- **Active Aging**: A policy and research framework promoting health, participation, and security in older age
