# Incarceration and Mass Imprisonment

## Overview
Mass incarceration in the United States — the unprecedented expansion of imprisonment since the 1970s, disproportionately affecting Black and Latino men — is analyzed by sociologists as a system of racial and class control rather than a rational response to crime, constituting a fundamental transformation of governance and inequality.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
The US incarcerates 2.1 million people (the world's largest prison population), with rates seven times higher than Western European democracies. Michelle Alexander's 'The New Jim Crow' (2010) argued mass incarceration constitutes a new racial caste system: the drug war selectively targets Black and Latino communities, conviction strips voting rights, employment prospects, housing eligibility, and welfare benefits — 'felon' becomes a legally sanctioned permanent stigma recreating racial subordination under a formally colorblind system.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Loic Wacquant analyzed mass incarceration as a 'carceral-assistential' complex — the expansion of prisons alongside the retraction of welfare serving to manage 'problem populations' (poor, Black, urban) produced by neoliberal economic restructuring. The 'prison industrial complex' (Davis) describes interlocking political-economic interests (construction firms, guard unions, rural communities dependent on prison employment, surveillance technology companies) with structural incentives to expand incarceration regardless of crime rates.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Mass incarceration has profound collateral consequences: it destabilizes families and communities (removing fathers and breadwinners), reduces political participation in already marginalized communities, imposes permanent employment barriers, and amplifies housing insecurity. Reentry sociology examines the near-impossible challenges facing released prisoners. Abolition movements argue the prison system is unreformable and that transformative justice alternatives — based on healing, accountability, and community — must replace carceral approaches entirely.

## Key Terminology
- **Mass Incarceration**: The unprecedented expansion of imprisonment in the US since the 1970s, disproportionately affecting minorities
- **New Jim Crow**: Alexander's argument that mass incarceration creates a racial caste system through the drug war
- **Prison Industrial Complex**: The interlocking economic and political interests benefiting from prison expansion
- **Carceral State**: The institutions, laws, and practices constituting the state's expanding apparatus of punishment and control
- **Collateral Consequences**: The civil penalties (voting disenfranchisement, employment bars) imposed by criminal conviction beyond incarceration
- **Transformative Justice**: An approach to harm that prioritizes healing, accountability, and community repair over punishment
