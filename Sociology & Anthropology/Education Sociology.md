# Education Sociology

## Overview
The sociology of education examines how educational institutions and processes reproduce, challenge, or transform social inequalities, analyzing the relationship between schooling and social class, race, gender, and national identity.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Functionalist approaches (Parsons, Davis and Moore) viewed education as a meritocratic sorting mechanism: schools identify talent and reward achievement, allocating individuals to social positions on the basis of ability rather than ascription. Critics demonstrated systematic class, race, and gender biases in testing, tracking, and teacher expectations that undermine this meritocratic ideal. Bourdieu and Passeron's 'Reproduction' (1977) showed schools reward middle-class cultural capital while penalizing working-class children for lacking it, misrecognizing social advantage as academic merit.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Samuel Bowles and Herbert Gintis's 'Schooling in Capitalist America' (1976) proposed the 'correspondence principle': schools reproduce capitalist social relations by mirroring the authority structures of the workplace — hierarchy, obedience to rules, deferred gratification. The 'hidden curriculum' — unstated lessons about authority, punctuality, competition, and deference — prepares working-class students for subordinate positions while elite school environments cultivate independence, creativity, and leadership.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Resistance theory (Willis's 'Learning to Labour', 1977) showed how working-class 'lads' actively resisted school authority through a counter-school culture that paradoxically reproduced their class position by orienting them toward manual labor. Contemporary education sociology addresses achievement gaps by race and class, the expansion of standardized testing, school privatization, digital learning, the global education 'industry', and university credentialism.

## Key Terminology
- **Hidden Curriculum**: Unstated lessons in schools about authority, discipline, and social roles beyond formal content
- **Credential Inflation**: The process by which rising educational attainment reduces the value of existing credentials
- **Cultural Capital**: Bourdieu's concept of knowledge, skills, and tastes that confer educational advantage
- **Tracking**: The practice of assigning students to different curriculum streams based on assessed ability
- **Correspondence Principle**: Bowles and Gintis's argument that schools mirror capitalist workplace authority relations
- **Educational Achievement Gap**: Persistent differences in educational outcomes by race, class, and gender
