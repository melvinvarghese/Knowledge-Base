# Comparative Sociology

## Overview
Comparative sociology uses systematic comparison of societies, institutions, and social processes across national and historical contexts to identify similarities and differences, test theoretical claims, and avoid ethnocentric generalizations derived from single-society studies.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Classical comparative sociology (Durkheim, Weber, Marx) compared civilizations to identify structural causes of modernity, capitalism, and social pathology. Durkheim's comparative suicide study used cross-national data on suicide rates to infer structural causes — integration and regulation — moving sociology toward empirical generalization. Weber's comparative historical sociology traced how different religious traditions (Protestantism, Confucianism, Hinduism) produced different orientations toward economic and political rationalism.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Contemporary comparative sociology uses multiple approaches: Most Similar Systems Design (MSSD) compares structurally similar countries to identify key causal variables; Most Different Systems Design (MDSD) finds common outcomes across diverse systems, suggesting minimal sufficient causes. Ragin's Qualitative Comparative Analysis (QCA) enables systematic comparison of multiple cases using Boolean logic, bridging quantitative and qualitative approaches. Cross-national surveys (World Values Survey, ISSP) enable statistical comparison of attitudes across dozens of countries.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Esping-Andersen's welfare state typology, Acemoglu and Robinson's comparative institutional economics, Huntington's clash of civilizations, and Inglehart's values surveys are examples of influential comparative work generating typologies and theoretical propositions. Critiques include the difficulty of achieving true equivalence in cross-national measurement, the reduction of complex national cases to typological slots, and the persistence of Eurocentric reference points and normative assumptions.

## Key Terminology
- **Comparative Method**: Systematic comparison across societies or historical periods to test sociological propositions
- **MSSD**: Most Similar Systems Design: comparing similar countries to isolate key causal differences
- **MDSD**: Most Different Systems Design: identifying common outcomes across diverse contexts
- **QCA**: Qualitative Comparative Analysis: set-theoretic method for systematic small-N comparison
- **Welfare State Typology**: Esping-Andersen's classification of welfare states as liberal, conservative, or social-democratic
- **Cross-National Survey**: Surveys using harmonized instruments to compare attitudes and values across multiple countries
