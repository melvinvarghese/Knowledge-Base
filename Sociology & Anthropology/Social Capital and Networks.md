# Social Capital and Networks

## Overview
Social capital refers to the resources — information, support, influence, solidarity — embedded in and accessed through social networks and relationships, with significant effects on economic mobility, health, civic participation, and collective action.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Bourdieu defined social capital as actual or potential resources linked to membership in a group — institutionalized relationships of mutual recognition. For Bourdieu, social capital works to reproduce class advantage: elite networks (old-boy networks, professional clubs) are real resources accessible only to insiders. James Coleman emphasized social capital as a feature of social structure facilitating action — norms, trust, and information channels within communities enable cooperation and collective goods.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Robert Putnam popularized the concept in 'Bowling Alone' (2000), distinguishing bonding capital (ties within homogeneous groups) from bridging capital (ties across diverse groups). He documented declining civic participation in the United States — fewer people joining clubs, attending religious services, voting, or trusting neighbors — and linked this to weakened community problem-solving capacity, poorer health outcomes, and reduced economic growth.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Critiques note that social capital can be used to explain poverty as a cultural deficit (blaming communities for lacking the right networks), obscuring structural causes. Network sociology has extended the concept with formal models of weak ties (Granovetter) — acquaintances providing access to non-redundant information — and structural holes (Burt) — gaps between network clusters that brokers exploit for advantage.

## Key Terminology
- **Social Capital**: Resources embedded in social networks that facilitate action and cooperation
- **Bonding Capital**: Putnam's term for social capital within tight-knit, homogeneous groups
- **Bridging Capital**: Social capital linking diverse groups, enabling information flow and coalition building
- **Weak Ties**: Granovetter's finding that casual acquaintances are often crucial for job information
- **Structural Holes**: Burt's concept of gaps between unconnected network clusters exploited by brokers
- **Civic Engagement**: Participation in community life: volunteering, voting, association membership
