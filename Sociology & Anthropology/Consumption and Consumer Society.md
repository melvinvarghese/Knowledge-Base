# Consumption and Consumer Society

## Overview
Consumer society theories examine how consumption — once primarily a means of satisfying needs — has become a dominant mode of social integration, identity formation, status signaling, and cultural life in late capitalism, transforming the meaning of goods and reshaping social relations.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Jean Baudrillard argued that in advanced capitalism, the use-value and exchange-value of commodities are subordinated to sign-value: goods are consumed as signs within a coded system of social difference. We consume not products but meanings — brands, lifestyles, images. 'Simulacra and Simulation' (1981) extended this to argue that postmodern culture is characterized by hyperreality: the sign system has detached from any real referent, and simulations precede and generate 'reality'.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Thorstein Veblen's 'Theory of the Leisure Class' (1899) introduced conspicuous consumption: the wealthy display consumption publicly to signal status. Mike Featherstone analyzed consumer culture as a lifestyle project: post-Fordist consumption enables individuals to construct distinctive identities through taste, leisure, and aesthetic choices. Marxist political economy emphasizes commodification — the reduction of social life, relationships, and nature to exchange value — as a defining feature of capitalism's expansion.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Consumer society generates significant environmental burdens (resource extraction, waste, carbon emissions) while promising personal freedom and self-realization. Consumer activism, ethical consumption, and the circular economy are contested responses. Critics note ethical consumption displaces systemic critique onto individual choices, while structuralists argue that consumer culture masks and reproduces class-based inequality.

## Key Terminology
- **Sign-Value**: Baudrillard's concept of the social meaning or status conferred by consuming a commodity
- **Hyperreality**: Baudrillard's concept of a simulation system that supplants and precedes any real referent
- **Conspicuous Consumption**: Veblen's term for the public display of expensive goods to signal wealth and status
- **Commodification**: The process of turning social relations, activities, and nature into exchangeable market commodities
- **Consumer Identity**: The construction of self through choices of products, brands, and lifestyle consumption
- **Planned Obsolescence**: Deliberate design of products to become quickly outdated, driving replacement consumption
