# Reflexivity in Anthropology

## Overview
Reflexivity in anthropology refers to the critical examination of how the researcher's social position, assumptions, and power relations shape fieldwork, interpretation, and representation, moving the discipline toward greater epistemic humility and collaborative, decolonial practice.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
The 'writing culture' critique (Clifford, Marcus, 1986) argued that ethnographic texts are literary constructions — they select, narrate, and frame material through the anthropologist's cultural assumptions — rather than transparent transcriptions of cultural reality. 'The authority' of ethnographic writing derives from conventions (the 'I was there' claim, present-tense description) that obscure the partiality, power relations, and narrative construction involved in fieldwork and writing.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Feminist anthropology demanded reflexive attention to how gender shapes fieldwork: access, relationships, topics researchable, and interpretations produced. Postcolonial critique went further: the foundational anthropological relationship — a white Western researcher studying non-Western 'Others' — reproduces colonial power relations of observation, naming, and representation, however benevolent the intent. Decolonizing anthropology requires genuine collaboration, co-authorship, and redistribution of research benefits to communities.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Reflexive practice in contemporary anthropology includes: positionality statements disclosing the researcher's social location; collaborative and participatory research designs; indigenous-led research; long-term accountability to research communities; ethical protocols addressing data sovereignty and consent. The tension between reflexivity and research paralysis is real — excessive navel-gazing can displace attention from actual social phenomena — but reflexivity remains a necessary ethical and epistemological commitment.

## Key Terminology
- **Reflexivity**: The critical examination of how the researcher's position shapes knowledge production
- **Writing Culture**: Clifford and Marcus's critique of ethnographic texts as constructed representations
- **Positionality**: The researcher's social location and its effects on what can be known and said
- **Epistemic Humility**: Acknowledgment of the limits and partiality of one's own knowledge and perspective
- **Collaborative Ethnography**: Research designs that involve community members in planning, conducting, and writing
- **Data Sovereignty**: The right of communities to control data collected about them and to determine its use
