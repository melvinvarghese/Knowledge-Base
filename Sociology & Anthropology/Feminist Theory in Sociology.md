# Feminist Theory in Sociology

## Overview
Feminist sociology examines how gender operates as a fundamental axis of social organization, critiques the androcentrism of classical sociological theory, and develops frameworks — from liberal feminism to intersectional and poststructuralist approaches — to understand and challenge gender inequality.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Liberal feminism sought equal rights and opportunities, focusing on legal discrimination and socialization barriers. Socialist/Marxist feminism analyzed patriarchy as intertwined with capitalism: women's domestic labor reproduces labor power for capital without compensation (wages for housework debate). Radical feminism identified patriarchy as an independent, primary system of male domination rooted in control of women's bodies, sexuality, and reproduction.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Standpoint feminism (Dorothy Smith, Sandra Harding) argued that women's marginalized social location produces distinctive epistemic vantage points unavailable to dominant groups, enriching sociological knowledge. Patricia Hill Collins developed Black feminist thought as an intersectional framework centering the experiences of Black women at the intersection of race, gender, and class. Poststructuralist feminism (Judith Butler) questioned the binary sex/gender distinction, arguing gender is performatively constituted through repeated citational acts rather than expressing a natural core identity.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Contemporary feminist sociology addresses the global gender pay gap, care work and reproductive labor, gender-based violence, LGBTQ+ inequalities, and the gendering of organizations and professions. It increasingly works through an intersectional lens, refusing 'universal woman' in favor of diverse, situated analyses of gendered experience across class, race, and culture.

## Key Terminology
- **Patriarchy**: A social system in which men hold primary power in political, social, and economic institutions
- **Standpoint Theory**: A feminist epistemology holding that social location shapes knowledge production
- **Gender Performance**: Butler's concept that gender is enacted through repeated citational acts, not natural expression
- **Care Work**: Domestic and reproductive labor, disproportionately performed by women, often unwaged
- **Reproductive Labor**: The labor of reproducing workers and social life, primarily assigned to women in capitalism
- **Black Feminist Thought**: Collins' intersectional framework centering the experience and knowledge of Black women
