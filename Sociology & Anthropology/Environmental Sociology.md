# Environmental Sociology

## Overview
Environmental sociology examines the relationships between society and the natural environment, analyzing how social structures, institutions, and inequalities drive environmental degradation, and how environmental problems in turn shape social conflict, inequality, and change.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
The human exemptionalism paradigm (HEP) assumed human societies transcend ecological limits through culture, technology, and social organization — a stance mainstream sociology shared with other social sciences. Catton and Dunlap's 'new ecological paradigm' (NEP, 1978) challenged this by insisting that societies are embedded in and dependent on the biophysical environment, and that sociological theory must incorporate ecological constraints.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Schnaiberg's 'treadmill of production' analyzed how the competitive logic of capitalist accumulation drives ever-increasing resource extraction and pollution regardless of ecological costs, as firms must invest in expansion to remain competitive and workers must support growth for jobs. Ecological modernization theory (Mol, Spaargaren) countered that institutional and technological innovation can decouple economic growth from environmental impact, citing renewable energy, circular economy, and corporate sustainability initiatives.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Environmental justice sociology demonstrates that environmental burdens (pollution, climate vulnerability, toxic waste) are systematically concentrated in communities of color and low-income communities — a 'sacrifice zone' dynamic. Climate sociology analyzes public opinion on climate change, climate denial as organized counter-movement (Oreskes, Conway), fossil fuel industry political influence, and the sociology of climate inaction. Degrowth advocates challenge the ecological modernization thesis, arguing capitalist growth imperatives are fundamentally incompatible with ecological sustainability.

## Key Terminology
- **New Ecological Paradigm**: Catton and Dunlap's framework insisting human societies are embedded in ecological systems
- **Treadmill of Production**: Schnaiberg's model of capitalism driving ever-increasing resource extraction due to competitive logic
- **Ecological Modernization**: The theory that environmental problems can be solved through technological and institutional innovation within capitalism
- **Environmental Justice**: The movement addressing disproportionate environmental burdens on marginalized communities
- **Sacrifice Zone**: A community or region that disproportionately bears the costs of industrial or military pollution
- **Degrowth**: The movement advocating reduction of economic throughput to achieve ecological sustainability
