# Feminist Anthropology

## Overview
Feminist anthropology emerged in the 1970s to challenge the androcentrism of classical anthropology — its male-biased theories, male ethnographers studying male-dominated public spheres, and male informants speaking for whole communities — and to center women's experiences, knowledge, and agency in anthropological research.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Margaret Mead's 'Sex and Temperament in Three Primitive Societies' (1935) argued that masculine and feminine temperaments varied dramatically across cultures, challenging biological determinism. However, her work was criticized for romanticizing and sometimes distorting its subjects. Second-wave feminist anthropology (Rosaldo, Lamphere, 1974) systematically analyzed gender subordination cross-culturally: Rosaldo's domestic/public distinction argued women's universal subordination stems from their association with the domestic sphere, later critiqued as ethnocentric.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Ortner asked 'Is Female to Male as Nature is to Culture?' — arguing women are universally associated with nature (through reproduction and child-rearing) and therefore subordinated in cultures that value the cultural transformation of nature. Strathern's 'The Gender of the Gift' (1988) challenged Western categories of gift and commodity, public and private, as themselves culturally specific, unable to capture Melanesian gender relations. Feminist kinship studies contested Lévi-Strauss's exchange theory as presupposing women's passivity.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Contemporary feminist anthropology is explicitly intersectional, addressing how gender intersects with race, class, colonialism, and sexuality. It examines gender-based violence cross-culturally without cultural imperialism; reproductive politics including global surrogacy, maternal health, and reproductive rights; and LGBTQ+ experiences in diverse cultural contexts. Collaborative, reflexive methodologies center research subjects' interpretations and work toward feminist solidarity rather than extractive knowledge production.

## Key Terminology
- **Feminist Anthropology**: An approach centering women's experiences and challenging androcentric biases in the discipline
- **Domestic/Public Dichotomy**: Rosaldo's framework attributing women's subordination to their association with the domestic sphere
- **Nature/Culture**: Ortner's argument that women's universal subordination reflects their association with nature
- **Androcentrism**: The centering of male experience and perspectives as the norm in social and cultural analysis
- **Feminist Kinship**: Studies challenging androcentric kinship theories and centering women's agency in exchange systems
- **Gender Violence**: Physical, sexual, psychological, and economic harm based on gender inequalities
