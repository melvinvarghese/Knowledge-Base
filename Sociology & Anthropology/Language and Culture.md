# Language and Culture

## Overview
Linguistic anthropology examines the relationship between language and culture — how language shapes and is shaped by social life, how linguistic diversity encodes cultural knowledge, how speech acts constitute social relationships, and how language is implicated in power and identity.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
The Sapir-Whorf hypothesis (linguistic relativity) holds that the structure of language influences or determines thought: speakers of different languages perceive and think about reality differently. Strong versions ('linguistic determinism') have been largely rejected, but weaker versions find empirical support: language shapes habitual attention, color discrimination, spatial reasoning, and temporal concepts. Hopi verb structure (Whorf claimed) encodes a fundamentally different conception of time than Indo-European languages.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Sociolinguistics examines language variation by social class (Labov's New York vowel studies), gender (Tannen, Lakoff), ethnicity, and region — 'code-switching' between linguistic varieties signals social identity and context. Austin and Searle's speech act theory analyzed how utterances perform social actions (promising, naming, declaring) rather than merely describing — 'I now pronounce you married' changes social reality. Bourdieu's linguistic marketplace analyzed how linguistic capital — accent, vocabulary, register — confers or denies social access.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Language revitalization movements work to maintain and restore endangered Indigenous languages facing extinction under colonial language pressure. Contemporary linguistic anthropology examines multilingualism in global cities, language policy, sign languages as full natural languages, and the politics of translation. Discourse analysis (Fairclough, van Dijk) examines how texts and talk reproduce or challenge power relations — the linguistic dimensions of racism, sexism, and political ideology.

## Key Terminology
- **Sapir-Whorf Hypothesis**: The proposal that language structure shapes or determines thought and perception
- **Linguistic Relativity**: The weaker claim that language influences habitual attention and cognitive patterns
- **Speech Act**: Austin's concept of utterances that perform social actions rather than merely describe the world
- **Code-Switching**: The practice of alternating between languages or linguistic varieties depending on social context
- **Linguistic Capital**: Bourdieu's concept of linguistic competence as a form of capital conferring social advantage
- **Language Revitalization**: Efforts to maintain and restore endangered languages facing extinction
