# Healthcare Sociology

## Overview
Medical sociology examines health, illness, and healthcare as social phenomena — how social factors (class, race, gender, poverty) produce health inequalities, how medicine operates as a social institution, and how processes of medicalization expand medical authority over human experience.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Talcott Parsons' 'sick role' (1951) analyzed illness as a social status with obligations (seeking care, desiring recovery) and exemptions (from normal work duties), which requires legitimation by medical authority. This functionalist model was criticized for ignoring power relations, chronic illness, and contested diagnoses. The sociology of medical knowledge (Freidson, Starr) examined how the medical profession secured cultural authority, state licensing, and market monopoly over disease definitions and treatment.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Medicalization (Zola, Conrad) describes the process by which non-medical problems are redefined as medical conditions requiring medical treatment: childbirth, menopause, attention deficit, shyness, addiction, and unhappiness have all been medicalized, expanding pharmaceutical markets and medical jurisdiction. Conrad's concept of 'pharmaceuticalization' extends this to the solution-by-pill logic dominating contemporary healthcare. Critical analyses reveal how medicalization can both offer relief and function as social control.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Health inequalities research consistently demonstrates that socioeconomic position is the most powerful predictor of health and longevity — operating through material deprivation, psychosocial stress, and health behaviors. The 'social determinants of health' framework (Marmot) identifies income, education, housing, work conditions, and social support as fundamental causes. Racial health disparities in the US (Black maternal mortality, COVID-19 disproportionate mortality) reveal how racism operates as a fundamental cause of disease.

## Key Terminology
- **Sick Role**: Parsons' concept of illness as a social status with specific rights and obligations
- **Medicalization**: The process by which non-medical problems are redefined as medical conditions
- **Social Determinants of Health**: Non-medical factors — income, education, housing, work — that shape health outcomes
- **Medical Authority**: The institutionalized power of medicine to define health, illness, and treatment
- **Pharmaceuticalization**: The increasing use of pharmaceutical interventions to address human problems
- **Health Inequalities**: Systematic differences in health status across social groups, particularly by class and race
