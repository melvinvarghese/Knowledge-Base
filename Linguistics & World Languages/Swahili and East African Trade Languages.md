# Swahili and East African Trade Languages

## Overview
Originating in coastal Bantu city-states along the Indian Ocean, Swahili (*Kiswahili*) grew through centuries of maritime trade with Arab, Persian, and Indian merchants into East and Central Africa's premier lingua franca — uniting ~200 million speakers across Tanzania, Kenya, Uganda, and eastern Congo.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Beneath heavy Arabic vocabulary (~20–30% of lexicon, including numbers *sita, saba* and cultural terms *kitabu, safari, dunia*), Swahili remains unmistakably Bantu at its grammatical core: 15+ noun classes with rhythmic *m-/wa-*, *ki-/vi-* concord prefixes and agglutinative verb templates packing subject, tense, relative marker, object, and root into single words (*ni-li-ku-on-a* 'I-past-you-see').

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Written historically in Arabic *Ajami* script for classical Swahili poetry before 20th-century Latin standardization, Swahili was championed after independence by Julius Nyerere as Tanzania's unifying national tongue.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Now an official working language of the African Union, Swahili proves indigenous African languages can anchor modern statecraft, higher education, and regional commerce.

## Key Terminology
- **Kiswahili**: Bantu lingua franca of East Africa uniting native coastal *Waswahili* speakers and massive inland L2 populations.
- **Ajami script**: Use of adapted Arabic script to write African languages such as Swahili, Hausa, and Wolof.
- **Verb template (Swahili)**: Fixed agglutinative slot order: Subject + Tense + Relative + Object + Root + Extension + Final Vowel.
- **Arabic lexical stratum**: Extensive trade, legal, and religious Arabic loanwords integrated into Swahili's Bantu noun classes.
- **Ujamaa language policy**: Julius Nyerere's Tanzanian nation-building campaign elevating Swahili across schools and government.
- **Bantu noun concord**: Prefix agreement linking Swahili nouns to adjectives, possessives, and verbs.
