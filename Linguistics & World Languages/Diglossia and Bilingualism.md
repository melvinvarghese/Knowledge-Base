# Diglossia and Bilingualism

## Overview
Far from being rare, multilingualism is the global human norm. Sociolinguists distinguish individual bilingualism from 'diglossia' — a stable societal division where a community reserves a prestigious High variety for law and literature while speaking a distinct Low vernacular at home.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Charles Ferguson coined 'diglossia' in 1959 from classic cases like Arabic (Modern Standard Arabic vs. Egyptian or Levantine colloquial), Swiss German (Standard German vs. *Schwyzertütsch*), and Haitian Creole vs. French. The High (H) variety is learned in school and commands formal prestige, yet nobody speaks it natively to babies; the Low (L) variety is acquired naturally at home yet rarely written in formal newspapers.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
At the individual level, psycholinguists show bilingual brains keep *both* languages continuously active in parallel — requiring subtle prefrontal inhibitory control to select the target tongue. While early-20th-century myths claimed childhood bilingualism caused confusion, modern science proves balanced bilinguals match monolinguals in total conceptual vocabulary and gain lifelong cognitive reserve delaying dementia symptoms by ~4–5 years.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Understanding diglossia and translanguaging guides heritage-language schooling, medical interpreter standards, and inclusive national language planning across Switzerland, Singapore, India, and the Arab world.

## Key Terminology
- **Diglossia**: Societal arrangement dividing a prestigious High formal language variety from an everyday Low home vernacular.
- **Simultaneous vs. Sequential bilingualism**: Acquiring two languages from birth versus learning a second language after early childhood.
- **Heritage speaker**: Individual raised speaking a minority family language at home while educated in a dominant societal language.
- **Translanguaging**: Fluid, natural deployment of a multilingual speaker's full linguistic repertoire across language boundaries.
- **Additive vs. Subtractive bilingualism**: Learning a second language while preserving the first versus replacing one's home language.
- **Domain separation**: Societal norms assigning specific languages to distinct spheres (home, religion, government, trade).
