# Bengali, Marathi, and Regional Indo-Aryan

## Overview
Beyond Hindi-Urdu, South Asia's eastern and western Indo-Aryan languages — led by Bengali (~230M+ speakers), Marathi (~83M+), Punjabi, and Gujarati — boast centuries-old literary traditions, distinct scripts, and pivotal civic histories.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Bengali (Bangla), spoken across Bangladesh and India's West Bengal, evolved from Eastern Magadhi Prakrit, losing grammatical gender entirely while preserving rich honorific pronoun tiers and literary genius culminating in Nobel laureate Rabindranath Tagore. Crucially, the 1952 Bengali Language Movement (*Bhasha Andolon*) — protesting imposition of Urdu in East Pakistan — birthed UNESCO's International Mother Language Day.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Marathi in western India sits directly along the Indo-Aryan/Dravidian frontier, preserving three grammatical genders (including neuter) and inclusive/exclusive 'we' borrowed from Dravidian neighbors, while Punjabi uniquely developed lexical tones among Indo-Aryan languages after losing voiced aspirates (*gh, bh, dh*).

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Together, these major languages highlight South Asia's deep multilingual vitality and script diversity (Eastern Nagari, Devanagari, Gurmukhi).

## Key Terminology
- **International Mother Language Day**: UNESCO February 21 observance honoring the 1952 Bengali Language Movement martyrs.
- **Magadhi Prakrit**: Eastern Middle Indo-Aryan ancestor of Bengali, Assamese, and Odia.
- **Punjabi tonogenesis**: Rare emergence of three lexical tones in an Indo-Aryan language via loss of breathy-voiced consonants.
- **Gurmukhi**: Abugida script standardized by Sikh Guru Angad for writing Punjabi.
- **Sādhu-bhāṣā vs. Chalita-bhāṣā**: Historical Bengali diglossia between Sanskritized literary prose and colloquial Calcutta speech.
- **Marathi clusivity**: Dravidian-influenced distinction between inclusive *āpaṇ* and exclusive *āmhī* ('we').
