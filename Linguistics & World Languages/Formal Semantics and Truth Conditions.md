# Formal Semantics and Truth Conditions

## Overview
Semantics studies how linguistic expressions encode literal meaning. Formal semantics, pioneered by Richard Montague and Barbara Partee, applies predicate logic and set theory to explain how finite words compose systematically into infinite truth-evaluable propositions.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Gottlob Frege's Principle of Compositionality anchors formal semantics: the meaning of a complex sentence is determined by the meanings of its parts and how they are syntactically combined. Distinguishing 'sense' (intension — conceptual mode of presentation, e.g., 'the Morning Star') from 'reference' (extension — the actual object, Venus) solves why 'The Morning Star is the Evening Star' is informative rather than trivial.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Formal semanticists model predicates as sets, quantifiers (*every*, *some*, *most*) as generalized relations between sets, and modal auxiliaries (*must*, *might*) via 'possible worlds' semantics. This formal machinery rigorously explains entailment (if A is true, B must be true), presupposition (background assumptions surviving negation, like 'Have you stopped smoking?'), and scope ambiguity ('Every student read a book').

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Truth-conditional semantics directly shapes legal contract interpretation, formal ontology engineering, database query translation (NL-to-SQL), and logic benchmarks in artificial intelligence.

## Key Terminology
- **Principle of Compositionality**: Frege's rule that sentence meaning derives systematically from lexical meanings and syntactic combination.
- **Sense vs. Reference**: Frege's distinction between an expression's conceptual meaning (intension) and the world entity it denotes (extension).
- **Entailment**: Logical relation where the truth of one sentence guarantees the truth of another.
- **Presupposition**: Background assumption taken for granted by an utterance that persists even when the sentence is negated.
- **Quantifier scope**: Structural reach of logical operators like 'every' and 'some,' generating distinct readings when they interact.
- **Possible worlds semantics**: Framework evaluating modal claims ('might,' 'necessarily') across hypothetical alternative states of affairs.
