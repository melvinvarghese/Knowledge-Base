# Writing Direction, Typography, and Script Reform

## Overview
From ancient boustrophedon ('ox-turning') inscriptions and vertical East Asian columns to modern right-to-left Arabic and global Unicode standards, writing direction, typography, and deliberate script reforms shape literacy, identity, and digital infrastructure.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Early scripts experimented freely with direction: Egyptian hieroglyphs read toward whichever way animal figures faced; early Greek alternated left-to-right and right-to-left on consecutive lines (*boustrophedon*); and traditional Chinese and Japanese ran top-to-bottom in columns moving right-to-left (though modern horizontal web text reads left-to-right). Arabic and Hebrew run right-to-left, requiring 'bidirectional' (BiDi) layout algorithms whenever Latin numerals or URLs embed inside Arabic sentences.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Governments periodically overhaul scripts to modernize or realign geopolitically: in 1928, Mustafa Kemal Atatürk replaced Ottoman Arabic script with a phonetic Latin Turkish alphabet overnight, boosting literacy rapidly; post-Soviet Central Asian states shifted between Arabic, Latin, and Cyrillic across decades; and Simplified Chinese and simplified Japanese *Shinjitai* streamlined stroke counts.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Today, the Unicode Consortium maintains over 150,000 standardized code points across 160+ scripts, ensuring minority and Indigenous alphabets function natively on global smartphones.

## Key Terminology
- **Boustrophedon**: Ancient bidirectional writing style reversing direction on alternating lines ('as the ox plows').
- **Bidirectional text (BiDi)**: Digital rendering rules mixing right-to-left Arabic/Hebrew text with left-to-right numbers or Latin words.
- **Atatürk's 1928 Script Reform**: Rapid Turkish transition from Perso-Arabic script to a tailored phonetic Latin alphabet.
- **Unicode**: Universal character-encoding standard assigning unique digital code points to every global writing system.
- **Ligature**: Two or more graphemes joined into a single unified typographic glyph (essential in Arabic and Devanagari).
- **Romanization**: Systematic transcription of non-Latin scripts into the Latin alphabet (e.g., Pinyin, Hepburn).
