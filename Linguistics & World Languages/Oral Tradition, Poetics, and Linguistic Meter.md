# Oral Tradition, Poetics, and Linguistic Meter

## Overview
Long before writing, human societies preserved epic histories, laws, and genealogies through rhythmic oral poetics. Linguistic analysis of meter, alliteration, and Milman Parry's 'oral-formulaic' theory reveals how memory constraints and phonological structure shape poetic art across cultures.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Different languages build poetic meter out of their most salient phonological features: Ancient Greek and Sanskrit used quantitative syllable weight (long vs. short morae in dactylic hexameter), English and German used accentual-syllabic stress patterns (iambic pentameter), Old English and Old Norse bound half-lines via initial *alliteration*, and Japanese haiku counted exact *morae* (5-7-5). Meanwhile, Biblical Hebrew and Finnic *Kalevala* poetry relied on semantic parallelism — echoing a thought in varied synonyms.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
In the 1930s, Milman Parry and Albert Lord recorded living South Slavic *guslari* bards in Yugoslavia who improvised thousand-line epics without memorizing word-for-word scripts. Instead, bards composed fluently in performance using rhythmic 'formulae' ('rosy-fingered Dawn,' 'swift-footed Achilles') tailored to fit exact metrical slots — solving the mystery of how Homer's *Iliad* and *Odyssey* survived centuries orally.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Oral poetics bridges linguistics, folklore, and cognitive memory science, explaining why rhyme, rhythm, and formulaic cadence remain humanity's oldest mnemonic technologies.

## Key Terminology
- **Oral-formulaic theory**: Parry and Lord's discovery that oral epic bards compose live using metrically fitted stock phrases.
- **Quantitative vs. Accentual meter**: Poetic rhythm based on syllable duration/weight (Greek, Sanskrit) versus stressed beats (English).
- **Alliterative verse**: Early Germanic poetic structure linking half-lines via shared initial consonant or vowel sounds.
- **Semantic parallelism**: Poetic symmetry repeating or contrasting an idea across paired lines (Hebrew Psalms, Finnic epics).
- **Kenning**: Old Norse and Old English poetic compound metaphor (e.g., 'whale-road' for ocean).
- **Mnemonic rhythm**: Use of rhyme, alliteration, and regular meter to stabilize long-term verbatim oral recall.
