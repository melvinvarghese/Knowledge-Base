# Nahuatl and Mayan Languages of Mesoamerica

## Overview
Nahuatl (language of the Mexica/Aztec Empire) and the 30+ Mayan languages (K'iche', Yucatec, Tzotzil, Q'eqchi') represent Mesoamerica's premier surviving Indigenous linguistic heritages — boasting ancient literary monuments and vibrant modern communities numbering in the millions.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Classical Nahuatl (Uto-Aztecan) is richly agglutinative and mildly polysynthetic, incorporating noun objects directly into verbs (*ni-tlaxcal-chīhua* 'I-tortilla-make') and featuring *reverential* honorific suffixes calibrated to social respect. Recorded extensively in 16th-century alphabetic *Florentine Codex* chronicles alongside Spanish, Nahuatl gifted global languages *cacao, coyote, chili, tomato, chocolate*.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Mayan languages (spoken across Guatemala, southern Mexico, and Belize) showcase ergative-absolutive alignment, vigesimal (base-20) numeral systems, and 'positional' verb roots capturing exact physical posture (kneeling, leaning, stacked). Ancient Classic Maya stone inscriptions preserve over 1,500 years of royal history.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Today, bilingual radio, Maya glyph revitalization workshops, and digital keyboards sustain Nahuatl and Mayan languages as living modern tongues.

## Key Terminology
- **Noun incorporation**: Syntactic-morphological compounding embedding a direct object noun directly inside a Nahuatl verb.
- **Reverential register**: Nahuatl honorific verb and noun morphology expressing respect toward elders or sacred beings.
- **Mayan ergativity**: Split-ergative alignment marking transitive subjects differently from intransitive subjects and objects.
- **Positional roots**: Distinct Mayan word class specifying exact physical orientation, shape, and support posture.
- **Vigesimal counting**: Base-20 numeral system shared across Mesoamerican languages and calendars.
- **Florentine Codex**: Monumental 16th-century bilingual Nahuatl-Spanish encyclopedia compiled by Bernardino de Sahagún and Nahua scholars.
