# Pragmatics and Conversational Implicature

## Overview
Pragmatics investigates how speakers communicate far more than their literal words say by relying on shared context, social norms, and inferred intentions. Grounded in J.L. Austin's Speech Act Theory and Paul Grice's Cooperative Principle, pragmatics bridges linguistics with social cognition.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
In *How to Do Things with Words* (1962), J.L. Austin showed utterances do not merely describe reality — they perform actions ('I pronounce you married,' 'I apologize,' 'I promise'). John Searle refined Speech Act Theory into locutionary (literal utterance), illocutionary (intended force like warning or requesting), and perlocutionary (effect on listener) acts, explaining indirect requests like 'Can you pass the salt?' (literally a yes/no ability question, pragmatically an imperative request).

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Paul Grice (1975) explained how listeners bridge literal meaning and intended subtext via the Cooperative Principle and four Conversational Maxims: Quantity (be informative enough), Quality (be truthful), Relation (be relevant), and Manner (be clear). When a professor writes a reference letter saying only 'Jones has neat handwriting and punctual attendance,' flouting Quantity generates a devastating 'conversational implicature' that Jones lacks intellectual brilliance. Penelope Brown and Stephen Levinson further mapped Politeness Theory around protecting positive face (desire for approval) and negative face (desire for autonomy).

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Pragmatics illuminates cross-cultural miscommunication, courtroom perjury vs. implication, irony, sarcasm, and why literal chatbots struggle with unstated human subtext.

## Key Terminology
- **Speech Act Theory**: Austin and Searle's framework analyzing utterances as performative actions (promising, requesting, declaring).
- **Cooperative Principle**: Grice's assumption that conversational partners cooperate to achieve mutually coherent communication.
- **Conversational implicature**: Unstated meaning inferred by listeners when a speaker appears to flout a Gricean maxim.
- **Deixis**: Context-dependent expressions (*I*, *here*, *tomorrow*, *that*) whose reference shifts with speaker, time, and place.
- **Face (Politeness Theory)**: Social self-image individuals claim and protect during interaction (positive approval vs. negative autonomy).
- **Illocutionary force**: Speaker's intended communicative act (e.g., warning, promising, apologizing) behind an utterance.
