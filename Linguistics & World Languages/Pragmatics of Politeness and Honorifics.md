# Pragmatics of Politeness and Honorifics

## Overview
Every language encodes social hierarchy, intimacy, and respect into speech — whether through subtle English indirect requests, European *tu/vous* pronouns, or elaborate East Asian grammatical honorifics that transform verbs and vocabulary based on who speaks to whom.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Roger Brown and Albert Gilman's classic 1960 study mapped Europe's *T/V* pronoun distinction (French *tu/vous*, Spanish *tú/usted*, German *du/Sie*) along two axes: Power (asymmetric respect upward) and Solidarity (mutual closeness among equals). Over the 20th century, egalitarian shifts steadily expanded symmetrical *T* usage while retaining *V* for formal distance.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
In Japanese (*keigo*) and Korean, politeness is grammaticalized far more deeply into verb morphology and distinct lexical synonyms. Speakers constantly calibrate two independent axes: *respectful/humble* language elevating the listener or lowering oneself relative to the person talked *about*, and *polite/plain* speech levels calibrated to the immediate addressee. Javanese similarly divides entire vocabularies across *Ngoko* (intimate) and *Krama* (high formal) registers.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Honorific systems illustrate how social structure embeds directly into syntax — posing delicate challenges for machine translation and cross-cultural business etiquette.

## Key Terminology
- **T/V distinction**: Binary pronoun contrast encoding social familiarity versus formal respect (e.g., *tú/usted*, *tu/vous*).
- **Keigo**: Japanese honorific system dividing speech into polite, respectful (sonkeigo), and humble (kenjōgo) forms.
- **Power vs. Solidarity**: Brown and Gilman's two social dimensions governing asymmetric deference versus mutual intimacy.
- **Addressee vs. Referent honorifics**: Grammatical respect directed toward the listener versus respect toward a person mentioned in the sentence.
- **Positive vs. Negative politeness**: Appealing to warmth and shared belonging versus respecting autonomy and avoiding imposition.
- **Register shift**: Adjusting lexical and grammatical formality dynamically as social roles change.
