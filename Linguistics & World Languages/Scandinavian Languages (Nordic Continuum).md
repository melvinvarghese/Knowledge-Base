# Scandinavian Languages (Nordic Continuum)

## Overview
Descending from Viking Age Old Norse, the North Germanic languages divide into an extraordinarily mutually intelligible Continental dialect continuum (Danish, Norwegian, Swedish) and conservative Insular Norse (Icelandic, Faroese).

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Norwegian, Swedish, and Danish share a suffixed definite article (*hus* 'house' -> *huset* 'the house'), V2 word order, and mostly shared core vocabulary — enabling cross-border Scandinavian communication ('semicommunication'), though Danish's dramatic consonant lenition and glottal *stød* make spoken comprehension asymmetric. Norway uniquely maintains *two* official written standards: *Bokmål* (Danish-influenced) and *Nynorsk* (synthesized by Ivar Aasen from rural western dialects).

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
By contrast, isolated Iceland preserved Old Norse's four noun cases, three genders, and intricate verb inflections nearly unchanged since medieval sagas, coining pure native words for modern technology rather than borrowing English.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Scandinavian languages provide a premier laboratory for receptive multilingualism and written standard dualism.

## Key Terminology
- **Suffixed definite article**: North Germanic feature attaching 'the' to the end of nouns (*boken* 'the book').
- **Bokmål vs. Nynorsk**: Norway's two co-official written standards balancing urban Dano-Norwegian and rural native dialects.
- **Stød**: Characteristic Danish creaky-voice laryngealization functioning phonemically like tone.
- **Receptive multilingualism**: Speakers using their own native Swedish, Norwegian, or Danish while understanding neighbors effortlessly.
- **Old Norse**: Medieval North Germanic ancestor recorded in Younger Futhark runes and Icelandic Eddas/Sagas.
- **Tonal accent (Swedish/Norwegian)**: Two-way lexical pitch-accent contrast distinguishing homophones like *anden* ('the duck' vs. 'the spirit').
