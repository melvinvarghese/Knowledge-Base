# Hebrew: Ancient, Biblical, and Modern

## Overview
Hebrew represents history's most celebrated linguistic revival — transforming from an ancient Canaanite Semitic tongue preserved for two millennia as a sacred liturgical and scholarly medium into a vibrant, native spoken national language.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Biblical Hebrew (10th–4th centuries BCE) preserved the Hebrew Bible (*Tanakh*) in a 22-letter consonantal abjad alongside triconsonantal roots and VSO syntax. After the Babylonian exile and Roman period, Aramaic and Greek replaced Hebrew in daily street speech by c. 200 CE, yet Rabbinic, medieval poetic, and philosophical Hebrew (*Mishnaic* and *Medieval Hebrew*) remained continuously written across Jewish communities worldwide.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
In the late 19th century, Eliezer Ben-Yehuda and early Zionist educators pioneered Modern Hebrew (*Ivrit*) as a spoken vernacular — coining modern words from Semitic roots, simplifying Biblical verb syntax toward SVO, and raising the first native Hebrew-speaking children in 1,700 years.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Today spoken natively by millions in Israel, Modern Hebrew blends Semitic root morphology with European-influenced phonology and serves as an inspiring blueprint for global language reclamation movements.

## Key Terminology
- **Biblical vs. Modern Hebrew**: Classical VSO liturgical/literary stage versus revived SVO contemporary spoken *Ivrit*.
- **Eliezer Ben-Yehuda**: Lexicographer and activist central to reviving Hebrew as a modern everyday spoken language.
- **Niqqud**: Optional system of Hebrew vowel dots and dashes added beneath consonants in Bibles and primers.
- **Mishnaic Hebrew**: Post-biblical Rabbinic Hebrew stage recorded in the Mishnah (c. 200 CE).
- **Canaanite branch**: Northwest Semitic subgroup uniting Hebrew, Phoenician, and Moabite.
- **Ashkenazi vs. Sephardi pronunciation**: Historical regional liturgical traditions that shaped Modern Hebrew's vowel and consonant inventory.
