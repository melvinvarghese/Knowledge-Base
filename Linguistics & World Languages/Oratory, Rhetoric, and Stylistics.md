# Oratory, Rhetoric, and Stylistics

## Overview
Rhetoric and stylistics examine how word choice, rhythmic cadence, and figurative structure persuade audiences and shape literary voice. Tracing from Aristotle's triad of ethos, pathos, and logos to modern computational stylistics, rhetorical craft turns grammar into memorable civic power.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Aristotle's *Rhetoric* (4th century BCE) systematized persuasion around three appeals: *ethos* (speaker credibility and moral character), *pathos* (emotional resonance), and *logos* (logical argument and evidence), organized through classical canons of invention, arrangement, style, memory, and delivery. Classical orators deployed syntactic figures like *anaphora* (repeating openings: 'We shall fight on the beaches, we shall fight...'), *chiasmus* (criss-cross reversal: 'Ask not what your country can do for you...'), and the rhythmic *tricolon* ('veni, vidi, vici').

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Modern stylistics bridges linguistics and literary criticism by analyzing how authors manipulate register, passive vs. active agency, free indirect discourse, and meter to guide reader empathy. Cognitive stylistics further connects foregrounded linguistic surprises to attentional salience.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
From Lincoln's Gettysburg Address and Martin Luther King Jr.'s 'I Have a Dream' to contemporary speechwriting and legal advocacy, rhetorical structure remains central to democratic persuasion.

## Key Terminology
- **Ethos, Pathos, Logos**: Aristotle's three rhetorical modes of persuasion: credibility, emotion, and logical reasoning.
- **Anaphora (Rhetoric)**: Repetition of a word or phrase at the beginning of successive clauses for rhythmic emphasis.
- **Chiasmus**: Inverted parallel structure (AB-BA) creating memorable aphoristic balance.
- **Tricolon**: Rhetorical series of three parallel words, phrases, or clauses ('life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness').
- **Free indirect discourse**: Narrative style blending third-person grammar with a character's inner emotional voice.
- **Foregrounding**: Stylistic deviation from ordinary language norms to draw heightened reader attention.
