# Sociolinguistics and Language Variation

## Overview
Sociolinguistics studies how language varies systematically across social class, region, gender, age, and situational context. Founded by William Labov in the 1960s, variationist sociolinguistics proved that seemingly messy 'errors' or dialect shifts follow strict, measurable social rules.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Before Labov, linguistics often idealized a homogeneous 'native speaker.' In his iconic 1966 New York City department store study, Labov asked clerks at Saks (upper-class), Macy's (middle-class), and Klein's (working-class) where items were located ('fourth floor'), documenting how rhotic /r/ pronunciation rose smoothly with store prestige and careful repetition. Earlier on Martha's Vineyard, he proved subtle vowel shifts signaled local islander identity resisting summer tourists.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Sociolinguists distinguish 'overt prestige' (standard broadcast speech associated with education and power) from 'covert prestige' (non-standard vernaculars signaling solidarity, toughness, or in-group authenticity). Every speaker also shifts 'register' and 'style' effortlessly between job interviews and family dinners, while regional dialectology maps geographic boundaries called 'isoglosses' (such as US *soda* vs. *pop* vs. *coke*).

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Crucially, sociolinguistics affirms that vernaculars like African American Vernacular English (AAVE) — with systematic aspect markers like habitual *be* ('she be working') — are grammatically rule-governed dialects rather than careless mistakes, shaping equitable courtroom interpreting and literacy education.

## Key Terminology
- **Variationist sociolinguistics**: Quantitative study of how linguistic variables correlate with social class, age, gender, and style.
- **Overt vs. Covert prestige**: Status attached to official standard dialects versus solidarity value attached to local vernaculars.
- **Isogloss**: Geographic boundary line on a dialect map separating areas that use different pronunciations or words.
- **Vernacular**: Natural, everyday unmonitored speech of a community acquired prior to formal schooling.
- **AAVE (African American Vernacular English)**: Rule-governed North American English variety featuring systematic phonological and aspectual grammar.
- **Accommodation theory**: Howard Giles's model explaining how speakers converge toward or diverge from a partner's speech style.
