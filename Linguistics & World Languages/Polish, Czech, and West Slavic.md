# Polish, Czech, and West Slavic

## Overview
Spoken across Central Europe, the West Slavic languages — principally Polish, Czech, and Slovak — pair conservative Slavic noun-case and verbal-aspect grammar with Latin-script orthographies adapted via digraphs and Czech *háček* diacritics.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Polish (~40M+ speakers) stands out within Slavic for preserving Old Slavic nasal vowels (*ą, ę*) alongside dense, crisp consonant clusters (*chrząszcz* 'beetle') and predictable penultimate syllable stress. Czech and Slovak instead lost nasal vowels, innovated contrastive vowel length (*á, é, í*), and placed stress strictly on the *first* syllable — famously allowing vowelless sentences built on syllabic /r/ and /l/ (*Strč prst skrz krk* 'Stick a finger through the throat').

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Culturally, Jan Hus's 15th-century Czech orthography introduced the *háček* caron (č, š, ž, ř), replacing clumsy multi-letter spellings and later inspiring Croatian, Slovene, Baltic, and IPA phonetic symbols.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
West Slavic languages anchor vibrant Central European literature, cinema, and EU multilingual translation.

## Key Terminology
- **Háček (Caron)**: V-shaped diacritic (č, š, ž) pioneered in Czech to mark palatalized/postalveolar consonants cleanly.
- **Penultimate vs. Initial stress**: Fixed second-to-last syllable stress in Polish versus strict word-initial stress in Czech and Slovak.
- **Polish nasal vowels**: Rare preservation of Proto-Slavic nasalized *ą* and *ę* lost in Russian and Czech.
- **Syllabic consonants**: Consonants (/r, l/) functioning as full syllable nuclei without vowels in Czech and Slovak.
- **Seven-case system**: Preservation of nominative, genitive, dative, accusative, instrumental, locative, and vocative noun cases.
- **Diacritic vs. Digraph**: Czech single-letter accents (š) contrasted with Polish two-letter pairings (sz, cz).
