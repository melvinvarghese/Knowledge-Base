# Phonology and Sound Systems

## Overview
Whereas phonetics studies raw physical speech sounds, phonology investigates how individual languages organize sounds into abstract mental categories (phonemes), syllable templates, and rule-governed alternations.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
A phoneme is the smallest contrastive sound unit capable of distinguishing word meanings in a specific language — proven via 'minimal pairs' like *pat* vs. *bat*. Physically different sounds that never contrast meaning in that language are 'allophones' of a single phoneme: English speakers aspirate /p/ in *pin* [pʰ] yet leave it unaspirated in *spin* [p], treating both as /p/, whereas Hindi treats aspirated and unaspirated stops as entirely separate phonemes. Phonologists analyze sounds as bundles of binary distinctive features ([±voice], [±nasal]) explaining why natural classes pattern together.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Beyond individual segments, phonology governs syllable structure (onset, nucleus, coda) and phonotactics — rules dictating permissible clusters (why *blick* sounds plausibly English while *bnick* does not). Generative phonology (Chomsky and Halle's *Sound Pattern of English*, 1968) modeled derivations through ordered rewrite rules, later challenged by Prince and Smolensky's Optimality Theory (1993), where universal ranked constraints compete to select the optimal surface pronunciation.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Phonological theory informs childhood reading instruction (phonemic awareness), spelling reform, speech recognition error modeling, and language documentation.

## Key Terminology
- **Phoneme vs. Allophone**: Abstract contrastive sound category versus its predictable, context-dependent physical realizations.
- **Minimal pair**: Two words differing in only a single sound in the same position, proving phonemic contrast.
- **Phonotactics**: Language-specific constraints governing permissible sequences of consonants and vowels in syllables.
- **Distinctive features**: Minimal articulatory/acoustic properties (e.g., [+voice], [+continuant]) grouping natural sound classes.
- **Optimality Theory**: Constraint-based phonological framework ranking universal markedness and faithfulness constraints.
- **Assimilation**: Phonological process where a sound becomes more like an adjacent sound (e.g., *in- + possible -> impossible*).
