# Lexicography and Dictionary Making

## Overview
Lexicography is the art and science of compiling dictionaries — documenting how words are spelled, pronounced, inflected, etymologized, and actually used across communities. Spanning Samuel Johnson's 1755 monument and the Oxford English Dictionary to digital crowdsourced lexicons, dictionaries sit at the crossroads of scholarship and public debate.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Early wordlists served practical translation (Akkadian-Sumerian clay tablets) or explained 'hard words' in Renaissance English. In 1755, Samuel Johnson's *Dictionary of the English Language* codified literary usage with illustrative quotations, while Noah Webster's 1828 *American Dictionary* standardized distinct US spellings (*color*, *center*) as cultural independence. Beginning in 1857, James Murray and volunteer readers assembled millions of paper citation slips for the *Oxford English Dictionary* (OED), charting every word's historical birth and semantic drift.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Modern lexicography divides sharply over philosophy: *prescriptivism* (legislating 'correct' prestige usage) versus *descriptivism* (recording objectively how speakers actually use language). Following the 1961 controversy over *Webster's Third New International Dictionary* — attacked by critics for including *ain't* without moralizing — professional lexicographers overwhelmingly embrace descriptive corpus evidence while marking register labels (*slang*, *formal*, *archaic*).

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Digital dictionaries update quarterly with neologisms (*selfie*, *doomscroll*), integrate audio pronunciation, and partner with computational ontologies like WordNet to power search and translation.

## Key Terminology
- **Descriptivism vs. Prescriptivism**: Documenting how speakers actually use language versus prescribing rules of 'proper' prestige usage.
- **Citation slip**: Historical paper card recording an authentic published sentence illustrating a word's meaning and date.
- **Neologism**: Newly coined word, phrase, or borrowed sense entering a language's lexicon.
- **Register**: Style variation calibrated to social context (e.g., formal academic, casual slang, technical jargon).
- **Oxford English Dictionary (OED)**: Comprehensive historical dictionary tracing English word origins and semantic shifts via dated quotations.
- **Lemmatization**: Grouping inflected forms (*am, is, were*) under a single canonical dictionary headword (*be*).
