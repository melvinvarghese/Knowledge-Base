# Jane Eyre - Charlotte Brontë, Moral Autonomy, and the Victorian Social Order

## Overview
Charlotte Brontë's *Jane Eyre* (1847, published under the male pseudonym Currer Bell) is a pioneering Victorian Bildungsroman, chronicling an impoverished orphan's quest for love, independence, and moral integrity.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
The Five Stages of Jane's Journey: The novel traces Jane's development across five distinct institutional spaces: (1) **Gateshead Hall**: The unloved orphan subjected to cruel abuse by her aunt Mrs. Reed and locked in the terrifying Red Room; (2) **Lowood School**: The harsh, hypocritical charity school where Jane's saintly friend Helen Burns dies of typhus, instilling Christian resilience; (3) **Thornfield Hall**: Jane becomes a governess and falls in love with the brooding, sarcastic master **Edward Fairfax Rochester**; (4) **Moor House**: After discovering Rochester is already married, Jane flees into the moors, finding refuge with her cousins and refusing St. John Rivers' cold, loveless missionary marriage proposal; (5) **Ferndean**: Reunited as an independent, self-funded equal with a blinded, maimed Rochester after Thornfield burns.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
The Madwoman in the Attic: On Jane's wedding day, the wedding is dramatically stopped: Rochester's violent, mentally ill Creole first wife, **Bertha Mason**, is secretly imprisoned in the attic of Thornfield. Feminist and post-colonial critics (Sandra Gilbert and Susan Gubar, *The Madwoman in the Attic*; Jean Rhys, *Wide Sargasso Sea*) interpret Bertha as Jane's suppressed, rebellious double and the avatar of British colonial exploitation.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Transformed the 19th-century English novel by putting the inner psychological life, voice, and moral autonomy of an ordinary, plain woman at the center of serious literature.

## Key Terminology
- **Bildungsroman**: A literary genre focusing on the psychological and moral growth of the protagonist from youth into adulthood.
- **The Red Room**: The terrifying room at Gateshead where Jane was locked as a child, symbolizing patriarchal oppression and psychological trauma.
- **Reader, I married him.**: The celebrated opening sentence of the final chapter asserting Jane's agency in choosing her marital union.
