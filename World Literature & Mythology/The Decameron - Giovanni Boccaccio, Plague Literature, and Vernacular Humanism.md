# The Decameron - Giovanni Boccaccio, Plague Literature, and Vernacular Humanism

## Overview
Giovanni Boccaccio's *Decameron* (c. 1349–1353) is a monumental Italian vernacular narrative collection of 100 novellas, written in the immediate shadow of the catastrophic 1348 Black Death in Florence.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Frame Story & The Brigata: The opening provides a harrowing, eyewitness historical account of the social, moral, and psychological disintegration of Florence during the Black Death: bodies rotting in streets, laws abandoned, and families fleeing one another. Ten wealthy, educated young Florentines—seven women and three men (the *brigata*)—flee the plague-ridden city to a secluded countryside villa in Fiesole for two weeks. To pass the time civilly, each person tells one story every day for ten days (hence *Decameron*, Greek for 'ten-day event'), electing a daily King or Queen to dictate the theme.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Themes & Vernacular Humanism: The 100 tales span comic bawdiness, tragic romance, religious hypocrisy, and clever wit. Unlike Dante's transcendent spiritual focus in *The Divine Comedy*, Boccaccio's *Decameron* celebrates earthly humanism: celebrating merchant cunning, female agency, sexual pleasure, and resilience against blind fortune (*Fortuna*).

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Established Italian vernacular prose style, served as the primary narrative model for Geoffrey Chaucer's *Canterbury Tales*, and birthed the modern Western novella.

## Key Terminology
- **The Brigata**: The company of seven young women and three men who flee plague-stricken Florence to tell stories in the countryside.
- **Novella**: A compact prose fictional tale popular in Renaissance Italy, shorter than a novel but longer than a short fable.
- **Fortuna**: The classical and Renaissance concept of unpredictable fate or luck that human beings must navigate using wit and ingenuity.
