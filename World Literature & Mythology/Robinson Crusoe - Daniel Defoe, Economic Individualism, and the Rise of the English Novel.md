# Robinson Crusoe - Daniel Defoe, Economic Individualism, and the Rise of the English Novel

## Overview
Daniel Defoe's *Robinson Crusoe* (1719) is widely regarded as the first true English novel, inaugurating modern prose realism through the detailed chronicle of an isolated castaway.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Narrative Realism & Desert Island Solitude: Inspired in part by the real-life ordeal of marooned Scottish sailor Alexander Selkirk. Crusoe is shipwrecked on an uninhabited tropical island off the coast of South America for 28 years. Defoe replaces fantastic allegory with relentless, documentary-like **verisimilitude**: meticulously cataloging inventory, carpentry, agriculture (planting barley and rice), animal husbandry (taming wild goats), and journal-keeping.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Economic Individualism & Friday: Karl Marx and economic historians analyzed Crusoe as the prototype of *Homo Economicus*—the self-reliant capitalist individualist who masters nature through labor, technology, and ledger bookkeeping. The arrival of **Friday**, an Indigenous man Crusoe rescues from cannibals, crystallizes the imperialist dynamic: Crusoe names him, clothes him, teaches him English, and converts him to Christianity, establishing a benevolent master-servant colonial relationship.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Spawned the 'Robinsonade' genre (Johann David Wyss's *The Swiss Family Robinson*, William Golding's *Lord of the Flies*) and established the conventions of English realist fiction.

## Key Terminology
- **Verisimilitude**: The appearance or semblance of truth and concrete reality in literary fiction.
- **Homo Economicus**: The theoretical concept of humans as entirely self-interested, rational, optimizing economic agents.
- **Robinsonade**: A literary genre centered on characters marooned on desert islands forced to rebuild civilization from scratch.
