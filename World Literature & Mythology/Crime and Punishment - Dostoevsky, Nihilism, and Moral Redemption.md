# Crime and Punishment - Dostoevsky, Nihilism, and Moral Redemption

## Overview
Fyodor Dostoevsky's *Crime and Punishment* (1866) is the supreme Russian psychological masterpiece analyzing ideological delusion, guilt, and Christian redemption.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
The Extraordinary Man Theory: Impoverished ex-student Rodion Raskolnikov rationalizes murdering an old, abusive pawnbroker, formulating a theory that 'extraordinary' men (like Napoleon) possess the moral right to transgress ethical boundaries for the greater good.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Psychological Torment & Spiritual Resurrection: Immediately following the double murder, Raskolnikov's intellectual hubris collapses into feverish paranoia and physical illness, pursued by cunning magistrate Porfiry Petrovich. He finds moral confession and spiritual salvation through the saintly, self-sacrificing prostitute Sonya Marmeladova, accepting exile and spiritual rebirth in Siberia.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Profound critique of 19th-century utilitarianism and nihilism, pioneering exploration of subconscious psychological guilt.

## Key Terminology
- **Raskolnikov**: Protagonist whose theoretical justification for murder collapses into psychological and moral agony.
- **Nihilism**: Philosophical viewpoint rejecting traditional moral values and religious authority in 19th-century Russia.
- **Sonya Marmeladova**: Devout Christian figure whose self-sacrificing love guides Raskolnikov to moral confession and redemption.
