# Tartuffe and The Misanthrope - Molière and Neoclassical Character Comedy

## Overview
Jean-Baptiste Poquelin (Molière, 1622–1673) is the supreme comedic dramatist of the French Golden Age, elevating character comedy to profound social and philosophical satire under King Louis XIV.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Tartuffe (The Impostor, 1664): A scathing exposure of religious hypocrisy. **Tartuffe**, a cunning fraud masquerading as a devout Catholic ascetic, ingratiates himself with the wealthy, gullible patriarch Orgon. Orgon becomes so blinded by Tartuffe's performative piety that he signs away his family fortune and promises his daughter's hand to him. Orgon's wife Elmire finally exposes Tartuffe by having Orgon hide under a table while Tartuffe brazenly tries to seduce her. Banned for five years by the Archbishop of Paris and Catholic factions, the play survives as the quintessential study of pious fraud.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
The Misanthrope (1666): Molière's psychological masterpiece. **Alceste**, a man who refuses all social pleasantries and flattery, demanding total absolute honesty in all human relationships. Ironically, the misanthropic Alceste is hopelessly in love with **Célimène**, a frivolous, coquettish, gossip-loving court widow. When Célimène's hypocrisy is exposed and she refuses to abandon Parisian society to live with him in isolation, Alceste retreats into bitter hermit solitude.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Masterpieces of the French alexandrine twelve-syllable rhyming couplet, shaping European stage comedy from the Restoration down to the modern era.

## Key Terminology
- **Tartuffe**: A hypocrite who pretends to possess religious devotion and moral virtues while pursuing corrupt private gain.
- **Misanthrope**: A person who dislikes humankind and avoids human society, embodied by Molière's Alceste.
- **Alexandrine**: The classical French verse meter consisting of twelve syllables with a caesura after the sixth syllable.
