# Sir Gawain and the Green Knight - The Pearl Poet, Chivalric Integrity, and the Beheading Game

## Overview
*Sir Gawain and the Green Knight* (late 14th century) is an anonymous Middle English chivalric alliterative masterpiece written in the West Midlands dialect by the 'Pearl Poet', testing Arthurian chivalry against elemental nature and human weakness.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
The Challenge & The Beheading Game: During New Year's festivities at Camelot, a colossal, supernatural green knight riding a green steed and wielding an axe issues a festive Christmas challenge: any knight may strike him with his axe, on condition that the challenger receive a return blow in one year and a day at the Green Chapel. **Sir Gawain**, youngest nephew of King Arthur and epitome of chivalric virtue, accepts to defend Arthur's honor and cleanly decapitates the knight. Miraculously, the Green Knight picks up his bleeding severed head, commands Gawain to seek him at the Green Chapel, and gallops away.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
The Pentangle & The Bedroom Temptations: Gawain rides through the wilderness, bearing the **Pentangle** on his shield—an endless knot symbolizing the five chivalric virtues (faultless in five senses, five fingers, devotion to the five wounds of Christ, the five joys of Mary, and fifth, fellowship, purity, and courtesy). Gawain is sheltered at the castle of Lord Bertilak, agreeing to an 'Exchange of Winnings' game. While Bertilak hunts deer, boar, and fox, Bertilak's seductive lady attempts to seduce Gawain in his bedroom. Gawain resists adultery but secretly accepts a magical **green silk girdle** that promises to protect him from death.

The Strike at the Green Chapel: At the Green Chapel, the Green Knight is revealed as Bertilak transformed by Morgan le Fay. He feints twice and nicks Gawain's neck on the third blow—punishing him only for keeping the girdle out of fear of death. Gawain returns to Camelot in humble shame, wearing the green sash as a badge of his mortal imperfection, which the Knights of the Round Table adopt as an emblem of collective honor.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
The crowning jewel of the 14th-century Middle English Alliterative Revival, masterfully balancing chivalric romance, Christian moral testing, and pagan seasonal myth.

## Key Terminology
- **Pentangle**: The five-pointed endless knot on Gawain's shield symbolizing the five interconnected virtues of chivalric perfection.
- **Alliterative Verse**: A poetic form where the primary unifying principle is the repetition of initial consonant sounds across lines rather than end-rhyme.
- **Bob and Wheel**: A metrical device consisting of a short line of one foot (bob) followed by four three-beat lines (wheel) at the end of alliterative stanzas.
