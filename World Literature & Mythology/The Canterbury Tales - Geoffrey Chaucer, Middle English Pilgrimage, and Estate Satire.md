# The Canterbury Tales - Geoffrey Chaucer, Middle English Pilgrimage, and Estate Satire

## Overview
Geoffrey Chaucer's *The Canterbury Tales* (written between 1387 and 1400) is the foundational masterpiece of Middle English literature, bringing together diverse social estates in an energetic frame narrative pilgrimage.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
The General Prologue & Estate Satire: Set in April at the Tabard Inn in Southwark. A group of 29 pilgrims from every strata of late-medieval English society assemble to travel to Canterbury Cathedral to visit the shrine of Saint Thomas Becket. The host, Harry Bailly, proposes a storytelling contest to pass the time on the road: two tales on the way, two on the return. Chaucer deploys **Estate Satire** to critique the Three Medieval Estates (clergy, nobility, and peasantry): highlighting the hypocrisies of church figures (the corrupt Pardoner peddling fake pig-bone relics, the worldly Monk, the wanton Friar) alongside the chivalric Knight and the diligent Plowman.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Literary Polyphony & The Wife of Bath: The tales range across genres: chivalric romance (*The Knight's Tale*), bawdy fabliau (*The Miller's Tale*), animal fable (*The Nun's Priest's Tale*), and moral sermon. Preeminent is the **Wife of Bath's Prologue and Tale**: Alison of Bath, who has had five husbands, delivers an outspoken, proto-feminist defense of female sexuality and marital sovereignty (*maistrye*), arguing that what women desire most in the world is sovereignty over their husbands.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Elevated London Middle English from a vulgar vernacular into a prestigious, expressive literary language.

## Key Terminology
- **Estate Satire**: Medieval literary genre that satirizes the abuses and moral shortcomings of the three social classes (clergy, nobility, commoners).
- **Fabliau**: A comic, ribald medieval verse tale characterized by bawdy bedroom trickery, greed, and bodily humor.
- **Maistrye**: Middle English term for marital mastery, power, or sovereignty contested in The Wife of Bath's Tale.
