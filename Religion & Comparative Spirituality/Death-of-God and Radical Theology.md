# Death-of-God and Radical Theology

## Overview
Confronting 20th-century Nazi atrocities, Hiroshima, and modern scientific secularism, 'radical' and existential theologians—led by Dietrich Bonhoeffer, Paul Tillich, and 1960s 'Death of God' thinkers—dismantled the traditional sky-dwelling monarch God to reimagine faith in a 'world come of age.'

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Writing from a Nazi prison cell before his 1945 execution, German Lutheran pastor Dietrich Bonhoeffer called for 'religionless Christianity': living ethically in solidarity with suffering neighbors without leaning on a lazy 'God of the gaps' who patches scientific ignorance. Paul Tillich defined God not as one supreme *being* alongside others, but as the 'Ground of Being' itself—framing faith as 'ultimate concern' rather than credulous dogma.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
In the 1960s, Thomas Altizer, William Hamilton, and Jewish post-Holocaust thinker Richard Rubenstein shocked Time Magazine with 'Death of God' theology, arguing traditional omnipotent providence died at Auschwitz and demanding radical human responsibility.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
These currents seeded Process Theology, secular-ritual communities, and modern progressive theology.

## Key Terminology
- **God of the gaps**: Critiqued apologetic tactic inserting God into unexplained scientific puzzles that shrink over time.
- **Ground of Being**: Paul Tillich's non-anthropomorphic concept of God as Being-itself rather than a cosmic super-object.
- **Religionless Christianity**: Dietrich Bonhoeffer's prison vision of ethical faith stripped of clerical privilege and metaphysical escape.
- **Ultimate concern**: Tillich's existential definition of genuine faith as whatever commands one's deepest unconditional devotion.
- **Post-Holocaust theology**: Jewish and Christian reflection grappling with covenant and divine silence after Auschwitz.
- **A/theology**: Postmodern radical theology exploring sacred meaning after abandoning literal metaphysics.
