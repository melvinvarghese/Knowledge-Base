# Biblical Criticism and Scriptural Hermeneutics

## Overview
Modern biblical criticism applies philology, archaeology, and historical-literary methods to understand how ancient Jewish and Christian scriptures were composed, edited, and transmitted—coexisting alongside traditional theological readings.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Pioneered by Baruch Spinoza and 19th-century German scholars, the 'historical-critical method' asks what texts meant to their original audiences. Julius Wellhausen's Documentary Hypothesis argued the Pentateuch weaves together distinct literary sources (J, E, D, P) edited over centuries, while New Testament scholars traced shared oral traditions behind the Synoptic Gospels (Markan priority plus the hypothetical 'Q' sayings source).

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
The 1947 discovery of the Dead Sea Scrolls at Qumran revolutionized textual criticism by revealing Jewish manuscripts a thousand years older than surviving medieval copies—confirming remarkable scribal fidelity alongside fluid pre-standardized textual variants. Scholars also pair historical criticism with canonical, feminist, and liberationist hermeneutics.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Understanding scriptural genres—separating mythic poetry, prophetic metaphor, ancient law codes, and historical chronicle—enriches both academic scholarship and mature religious faith.

## Key Terminology
- **Historical-critical method**: Scholarly approach reading ancient scriptures within their original languages, history, and literary genres.
- **Documentary Hypothesis**: Theory proposing the Torah combines distinct ancestral narrative and priestly strands.
- **Synoptic Problem**: Literary relationship among Matthew, Mark, and Luke, widely explained via Markan priority and Q.
- **Dead Sea Scrolls**: 1st-century BCE–1st-century CE Jewish manuscripts found near Qumran transforming biblical archaeology.
- **Exegesis vs. Eisegesis**: Drawing meaning carefully *out* of a text in context versus reading one's own bias *into* it.
- **Canonization**: Historical process by which religious communities recognized certain books as authoritative scripture.
