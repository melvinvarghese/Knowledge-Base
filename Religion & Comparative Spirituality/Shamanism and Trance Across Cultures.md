# Shamanism and Trance Across Cultures

## Overview
Originating from the Evenki Tungusic word *šaman* ('one who knows') in Siberia, shamanism describes diverse indigenous practices where a specialist enters controlled altered states of consciousness—often via rhythmic drumming—to journey into spirit worlds for healing, divination, and community balance.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
In *Shamanism: Archaic Techniques of Ecstasy* (1951), Mircea Eliade characterized classic Siberian and Arctic shamans by 'soul flight': crossing the *axis mundi* cosmic tree with helper animal spirits to retrieve lost soul fragments or guide the dead. Anthropologists later cautioned against flattening vast global differences—distinguishing ecstatic 'soul flight' (Siberia, Amazonia) from 'spirit possession' (Korean *mudang*, Afro-Caribbean *Vodou*), where spirits enter the ritualist's body.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Neuroanthropologists note rhythmic ~4–7 Hz frame-drumming, fasting, and sacred plant entheogens (such as Amazonian *ayahuasca* or Mazatec mushrooms) reliably shift brainwave synchrony and quiet ego-monitoring.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Today, indigenous shamans safeguard ecological botanical knowledge while resisting commercialized 'neo-shamanic' cultural appropriation.

## Key Terminology
- **Šaman (Shaman)**: Siberian Evenki term for a ritual specialist navigating spirit realms via controlled trance.
- **Soul flight vs. Spirit possession**: Shamanic journeying outward into spirit worlds versus hosting spirits within one's body.
- **Entheogen**: Psychoactive plant or fungus used reverently in traditional ceremonial healing and vision quests.
- **Soul loss**: Traditional cross-cultural diagnosis attributing chronic illness or trauma to fragmented spiritual vitality.
- **Psychopomp**: Ritual specialist guiding newly deceased souls safely into the afterlife.
- **Neo-shamanism**: Modern Western eclectic adaptation of indigenous drumming and vision practices outside traditional lineages.
