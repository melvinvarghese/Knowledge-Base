# Kabbalah and Jewish Mysticism

## Overview
Kabbalah ('that which is received') is the esoteric mystical tradition of Judaism, seeking direct experiential insight into the infinite divine essence (*Ein Sof*) and the hidden spiritual architecture of creation.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Early Merkabah ('Chariot') mysticism contemplated Ezekiel's heavenly throne visions before medieval Kabbalah crystallized in 12th–13th century Provence and Spain—culminating in Moses de León's *Zohar* ('Book of Splendor'). Kabbalah maps creation through ten *Sefirot* (divine emanations—Wisdom, Understanding, Lovingkindness, Judgment, Beauty, Sovereignty) bridging unknowable *Ein Sof* and the physical cosmos.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
In 16th-century Safed, Isaac Luria ('The Ari') forged Lurianic Kabbalah around *Tzimtzum* (God's self-contraction to make room for creation), *Shevirat HaKelim* (shattering of divine vessels), and *Tikkun Olam* (cosmic repair through human ethical and ritual *mitzvot*). In 18th-century Eastern Europe, the Baal Shem Tov launched *Hasidism*, democratizing Kabbalah into joyful song, storytelling, and everyday devotion.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Concepts like *Tikkun Olam* ('repairing the world') now inspire Jewish social justice globally alongside academic Gershom Scholem's historical scholarship.

## Key Terminology
- **Ein Sof**: The infinite, unknowable, transcendent essence of God beyond human attributes.
- **Sefirot**: Ten dynamic divine emanations or channels through which God sustains creation.
- **Zohar**: 13th-century Aramaic mystical commentary on the Torah, central text of Kabbalah.
- **Tzimtzum**: Lurianic concept of divine self-withdrawal creating conceptual space for a finite universe.
- **Tikkun Olam**: Kabbalistic repair of cosmic brokenness, widely adapted today for ethical social action.
- **Hasidism**: 18th-century Eastern European revival movement emphasizing ecstatic joy and divine immanence.
