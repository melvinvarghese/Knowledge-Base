# Comparative Theodicy: Karma vs. Divine Grace

## Overview
How are broken or suffering beings rescued? Comparative soteriology reveals two grand spiritual grammars—'Self-Power' (*jiriki*) versus 'Other-Power' (*tariki*), or moral Karma versus unmerited Divine Grace—that surprisingly reappear *inside* both Eastern and Western traditions.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
At first glance, Dharmic traditions emphasize strict moral self-effort (*karma*: you reap precisely what your own intentions sow across lifetimes), whereas Protestant Christianity and Islam emphasize transcendent divine mercy and grace (*sola gratia*, *ar-Raḥmān*). Yet closer comparative study uncovers striking internal parallels! In South India, Shri Vaishnava Bhakti split delightfully over 'Monkey-baby' theology (effortful clinging to God) versus 'Kitten' theology (helpless surrender carried wholly by God's mouth).

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Similarly, Japanese Pure Land Buddhism's Shinran taught pure *tariki* ('Other-Power' grace of Amitābha Buddha) mirroring Martin Luther's *Sola Fide* centuries before European contact, while Catholic and Sufi traditions balance divine grace with active human cooperation.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Comparative soteriology illuminates universal human tensions between moral accountability and humble gratitude.

## Key Terminology
- **Soteriology**: Theological study of salvation, liberation, and how human beings overcome alienation or suffering.
- **Jiriki vs. Tariki**: Japanese Buddhist distinction between 'self-power' meditative effort and 'other-power' surrender to grace.
- **Sola Gratia**: Reformation teaching that salvation is wholly an unearned gift of divine mercy.
- **Monkey vs. Kitten analogy**: Classical Indian Bhakti debate contrasting active human effort against passive surrender to divine grace.
- **Prapatti**: Devotional self-surrender to Vishnu/God in South Indian Shri Vaishnavism.
- **Merit transfer**: Mahayana Buddhist practice dedicating one's own virtuous karma to benefit suffering beings or ancestors.
