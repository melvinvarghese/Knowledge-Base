# The Protestant Reformation and Denominationalism

## Overview
Launched in 1517 when Martin Luther challenged papal indulgences in Wittenberg, the Protestant Reformation fractured Western Christendom around *Sola Scriptura* (Scripture alone) and *Sola Fide* (faith alone)—unleashing vernacular Bibles, literacy, and centuries of denominational diversity.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Printing presses carried Luther's Ninety-Five Theses across Europe rapidly. Early reformers converged on key 'Solas': salvation is an unearned gift through faith (*Sola Fide*, *Sola Gratia*) rather than purchased merit, and Scripture—not papal decrees—holds supreme authority (*Sola Scriptura*), paired with the 'priesthood of all believers.' Yet reformers soon diverged: Lutheranism kept liturgical continuity; Reformed/Calvinist churches (Huldrych Zwingli, John Calvin in Geneva) stressed divine sovereignty and stripped church images; Henry VIII's Anglicanism forged a *via media* national church; and radical Anabaptists rejected infant baptism and state coercion.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Later waves generated Methodism (John Wesley's revivalist holiness), Baptists, Quakers (inner light pacifism), and Abolitionist social reform.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Sociologist Max Weber famously linked Calvinist worldly calling to early modern capitalism, while Protestant emphasis on personal Bible reading propelled mass vernacular literacy worldwide.

## Key Terminology
- **Sola Scriptura**: Reformation principle holding the Bible as the sole infallible authority for Christian faith.
- **Sola Fide**: Doctrine that sinners are justified before God by faith alone rather than meritorious works.
- **Priesthood of all believers**: Principle that all baptized Christians have direct access to God without priestly mediation.
- **Predestination**: Calvinist teaching emphasizing God's sovereign eternal election of souls.
- **Anabaptism**: Radical Reformation movement advocating adult believer's baptism, pacifism, and church-state separation.
- **Via media**: Anglican 'middle way' balancing Catholic liturgical heritage with Reformed theology.
