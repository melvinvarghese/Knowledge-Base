# Vodou and Afro-Caribbean Religions

## Overview
Forged in the crucible of Caribbean plantation slavery, Haitian *Vodou*, Cuban *Palo*, and Jamaican *Rastafari* wove West and Central African spirit cosmologies, Catholic iconography, and Indigenous Taíno elements into resilient traditions of healing, ancestral memory, and liberation.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
In Haiti, *Vodou* (from Fon *vodún*, 'spirit') honors supreme distant Creator *Bondye* ('Good God') while working intimately with *lwa* (spirits such as *Papa Legba* guardian of crossroads, *Erzulie* of love, and *Ogou* of iron/strength) through rhythmic *radà* drumming, intricate cornmeal *vèvè* floor drawings, and ritual spirit possession ('riding the horse') to heal sickness and mend community strife. Crucially, the 1791 Bois Caïman Vodou ceremony helped ignite the Haitian Revolution.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
In 1930s Jamaica, *Rastafari* emerged as an Afrocentric messianic and anti-colonial movement revering Ethiopian Emperor Haile Selassie I (*Jah*), practicing *Ital* natural living, dreadlocks, and reggae spiritual critique of oppressive 'Babylon.'

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Demonized by Hollywood zombie clichés, Vodou and Afro-Caribbean faiths remain vital pillars of Caribbean art, music, and post-disaster community care.

## Key Terminology
- **Bondye**: Supreme transcendent Creator in Haitian Vodou, approached through intermediary *lwa* spirits.
- **Lwa (Loa)**: Ancestral and natural spirits serving as moral and healing intermediaries in Vodou.
- **Vèvè**: Intricate sacred geometric emblems traced in cornmeal or ash on temple floors to summon specific *lwa*.
- **Bois Caïman (1791)**: Historic nocturnal Vodou gathering catalyzing the Haitian slave revolution.
- **Rastafari**: 20th-century Jamaican Afrocentric spiritual movement honoring Haile Selassie I and *Ital* natural living.
- **Spirit possession (Mounting)**: Sacred trance state where a *lwa* temporarily embodies a dancer to offer counsel and healing.
