# Jehovah's Witnesses and Adventist Movements

## Overview
Growing out of 19th-century American millennial expectation, Seventh-day Adventism and Jehovah's Witnesses developed distinct Saturday-Sabbath health reforms, non-Trinitarian Christologies, and landmark Supreme Court free-speech victories.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
After William Miller's predicted 1844 Second Coming failed ('Great Disappointment'), Ellen G. White helped organize Seventh-day Adventists (~22M globally today) around Saturday Sabbath observance, holistic plant-based health (influencing Kellogg's Battle Creek sanitarium), and a massive global hospital and university network. Independently, Charles Taze Russell founded the Bible Students (renamed *Jehovah's Witnesses* in 1931 under Joseph Rutherford), teaching Jehovah alone is Almighty God, rejecting the Trinity and Hellfire, and expecting Christ's earthly Kingdom.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Known for door-to-door ministry and *Watchtower* publications, Jehovah's Witnesses refuse blood transfusions, military service, and flag salutes—enduring Nazi camps and winning pivotal US Supreme Court First Amendment protections (*West Virginia v. Barnette*, 1943).

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Both movements illustrate how 19th-century prophetic expectation institutionalized into disciplined global communities.

## Key Terminology
- **Seventh-day Adventist**: Protestant denomination observing Saturday Sabbath, holistic health reform, and imminent Second Coming.
- **Jehovah's Witnesses**: Restorationist non-Trinitarian movement known for door-to-door evangelism and political neutrality.
- **Watch Tower Society**: Publishing and administrative organization guiding Jehovah's Witnesses worldwide.
- **Investigative Judgment**: Adventist doctrine interpreting 1844 as Christ entering a heavenly sanctuary review.
- **Flag-salute cases**: Landmark 1943 US Supreme Court ruling protecting conscientious refusal of compulsory patriotic pledges.
- **Conscientious medical refusal**: Witness rejection of whole-blood transfusions stimulating modern 'bloodless surgery' techniques.
