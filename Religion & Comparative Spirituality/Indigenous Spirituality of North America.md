# Indigenous Spirituality of North America

## Overview
Encompassing hundreds of distinct Native American and First Nations traditions—from Lakota *Wakan Tanka* and Haudenosaunee Thanksgiving Addresses to Pueblo *kachina* dances—North American Indigenous spiritualities share deep relational kinship with land, animals, and cyclical ceremony.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Resisting Eurocentric 'world religion' boxes, Indigenous elders emphasize that spirituality is inseparable from daily ecology, language, and treaty land: animals, rivers, and mountains are non-human relatives ('all my relations') deserving reciprocal gratitude rather than inert resources. Ceremonies such as the Diné (Navajo) Blessingway sandpaintings, Lakota *Inipi* (sweat lodge purification) and Sun Dance, and Pacific Northwest Potlatch restore cosmic and social balance.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Brutally suppressed by 19th–20th century US/Canadian assimilation boarding schools and bans on Ghost Dances and potlatches (not repealed in the US until the 1978 American Indian Religious Freedom Act), traditions survived underground and surged in resilience.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Today, Indigenous spiritual ethics lead North American sacred-site protection (Bears Ears, Standing Rock) and ecological fire-stewardship.

## Key Terminology
- **Relational kinship**: Indigenous worldview treating rivers, plants, and animals as moral relatives rather than property.
- **Wakan Tanka**: Lakota concept of the Great Sacred Mystery permeating all existence.
- **Inipi (Sweat lodge)**: Purification ceremony using heated stones and prayer to renew physical and spiritual balance.
- **Kachina**: Ancestral spirit messengers honored in Pueblo seasonal rain and harvest dances.
- **American Indian Religious Freedom Act**: 1978 US federal statute finally protecting Indigenous ceremonial access and sacred practices.
- **Seven Generations principle**: Haudenosaunee stewardship ethic weighing decisions against impacts seven generations ahead.
