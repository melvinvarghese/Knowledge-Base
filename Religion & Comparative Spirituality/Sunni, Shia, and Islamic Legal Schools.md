# Sunni, Shia, and Islamic Legal Schools

## Overview
Shortly after Prophet Muhammad's death in 632 CE, a leadership succession dispute split Islam into its two enduring branches—Sunni (~85–90%) and Shia (~10–15%)—while classical Islamic jurists developed *Sharia* and *fiqh* (jurisprudence) as pluralistic legal traditions.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Sunnis ('people of the Sunnah and community') accepted the historical succession of the four 'Rightly Guided Caliphs' (Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman, Ali) as political guardians of the community. Shias (*Shi'at Ali*, 'partisans of Ali') held that Muhammad designated his cousin and son-in-law Ali ibn Abi Talib as spiritual-political *Imam*, viewing subsequent caliphs as usurpers—a rift deepened tragically at the 680 CE martyrdom of Ali's son Husayn at Karbala, commemorated annually on Ashura.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Within Sunni Islam, four major legal schools (*madhhabs*—Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i, Hanbali) emerged using *ijtihad* (independent reasoning), consensus (*ijma*), and analogy (*qiyas*) to derive practical *fiqh* rulings from the Qur'an and Hadith; Twelver Shia Islam similarly preserves living *marja'* scholar-jurists alongside expectation of the Hidden Twelfth Imam.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Contrary to caricatures reducing *Sharia* solely to harsh criminal penalties, classical *fiqh* devotes most volumes to prayer, contracts, charity, marriage, and moral conscience.

## Key Terminology
- **Sunni vs. Shia**: Major Islamic branches divided initially over prophetic succession (elected Caliphs versus hereditary Imams from Ali's lineage).
- **Sharia vs. Fiqh**: Divine eternal moral path versus fallible human jurisprudential interpretation of legal rules.
- **Madhhab**: Classical school of Islamic legal methodology (Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i, Hanbali, Ja'fari).
- **Imamate (Shia)**: Doctrine of divinely guided spiritual leadership vested in descendants of Ali and Fatima.
- **Karbala / Ashura**: 680 CE martyrdom of Imam Husayn, foundational symbol of justice and sacrifice in Shia Islam.
- **Ijtihad**: Disciplined scholarly legal reasoning applied to derive rulings on novel ethical or social questions.
