# Jainism: Ahimsa and Ascetic Discipline

## Overview
Emerging in ancient India alongside Buddhism and codified by the 24th *Tīrthaṅkara*, Mahāvīra (6th century BCE), Jainism places radical nonviolence (*ahiṃsā*) toward every living creature—down to microscopic insects—at the center of ethics and liberation.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Rejecting Vedic animal sacrifices and creator-god theology, Jains hold the universe is eternal and uncreated, populated by countless infinite souls (*jīvas*) weighed down by sticky subtle karmic matter accumulated through passion and violence. Liberation (*kevala-jñāna* omniscience) requires purifying the soul through the Three Jewels (right faith, right knowledge, right conduct) and Five Great Vows: *ahiṃsā* (non-harm), truthfulness, non-stealing, celibacy, and non-possessiveness (*aparigraha*).

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Philosophically, Jainism contributed *Anekāntavāda* ('many-sidedness'): reality is infinitely complex, so any single human perspective captures only partial truth (*syādvāda*—'in some respect'), cultivating profound intellectual tolerance. Monks and nuns sweep paths gently and wear mouth-cloths to avoid harming tiny organisms, dividing historically into sky-clad *Digambara* and white-clad *Śvetāmbara* orders.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Though numbering ~5 million adherents, Jain ethics profoundly shaped vegetarianism in India and directly inspired Mahatma Gandhi's nonviolent resistance.

## Key Terminology
- **Ahiṃsā**: Absolute nonviolence in thought, word, and deed toward all living beings—Jainism's supreme ethical principle.
- **Tīrthaṅkara**: 'Ford-maker,' one of 24 perfected spiritual teachers (ending with Mahāvīra) who cross *saṃsāra*.
- **Anekāntavāda**: Jain epistemological doctrine of 'many-sidedness,' affirming truth has multiple valid facets.
- **Aparigraha**: Vow of non-possessiveness and detachment from excessive material accumulation.
- **Digambara vs. Śvetāmbara**: Major Jain divisions: 'sky-clad' (renouncing even clothes for senior monks) versus 'white-clad.'
- **Sallekhanā**: Rare, medically supervised peaceful fasting at natural end-of-life practiced by advanced ascetics.
