# Methodism and the Holiness Movement

## Overview
Launched as a disciplined 18th-century renewal movement inside Anglicanism by brothers John and Charles Wesley, Methodism combined heartfelt personal conversion, small-group accountability, social holiness, and thousands of hymns into a global Protestant force.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Experiencing his 'strangely warmed' Aldersgate conversion in 1738, John Wesley rode 250,000 miles on horseback preaching open-air sermons to neglected coal miners and organizing lay 'class meetings' for mutual confession and charity. Wesley's 'Quadrilateral' guided theology through Scripture, Tradition, Reason, and Experience, while Arminian free-will grace stood against strict Calvinist predestination.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
In the 19th century, American Methodism's frontier circuit riders and 'Holiness Movement' pursuit of entire sanctification directly seeded modern Pentecostalism and the Salvation Army alongside founding Black denominations like the African Methodist Episcopal (AME) Church.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Methodism's twin focus on personal piety and social reform fueled abolitionism, temperance, and global hospitals and universities.

## Key Terminology
- **Wesleyan Quadrilateral**: Methodist theological method balancing Scripture, Tradition, Reason, and personal Experience.
- **Social holiness**: Wesley's conviction that authentic Christian faith necessarily manifests in caring for the poor and prisoners.
- **Class meeting**: Small weekly Methodist accountability group nurturing spiritual discipline.
- **Arminianism**: Theological affirmation of universal grace and human free will against strict predestination.
- **Holiness Movement**: 19th-century revivalist stream pursuing post-conversion moral purity ('Christian perfection').
- **Circuit rider**: Itinerant frontier Methodist preacher riding horseback across rural American settlements.
