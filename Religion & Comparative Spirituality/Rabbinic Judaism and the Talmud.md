# Rabbinic Judaism and the Talmud

## Overview
Following the fall of the Second Temple in 70 CE, Rabbinic Judaism preserved Jewish continuity through the *Talmud*—a monumental multi-generational compendium of legal debate, ethical parables, and scriptural interpretation compiled across Judea and Babylonia.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Rabbinic tradition holds that Moses received both the Written Torah and an Oral Torah at Sinai. Around 200 CE, Rabbi Judah the Prince codified the *Mishnah* in concise Hebrew—organizing Jewish civil, ritual, and agricultural law into six orders. Over the next three centuries, academies in Galilee and Sura/Pumbedita (Babylonia) debated the Mishnah in Aramaic, producing the *Gemara*, which together with the Mishnah forms the *Talmud* (Jerusalem and the authoritative Babylonian Talmud).

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Uniquely, the Talmud preserves minority dissenting opinions alongside majority rulings (*'These and those are the words of the living God'*), modeling dialectical reasoning (*pilpul*) and *Midrash* (homiletic narrative exposition). Medieval commentators like Rashi and Maimonides later distilled Talmudic debates into accessible legal codes like the *Shulchan Aruch*.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Today, daily *Daf Yomi* ('page-a-day') study unites hundreds of thousands globally in a seven-year cycle, sustaining an unbroken culture of rigorous textual questioning.

## Key Terminology
- **Mishnah**: 200 CE Hebrew compilation of Jewish Oral Law organized into six topical orders.
- **Gemara**: Aramaic rabbinic commentary and legal debate analyzing the Mishnah.
- **Babylonian Talmud (Bavli)**: Authoritative 6th-century encyclopedic record of Rabbinic law and lore.
- **Midrash**: Rabbinic method of creative biblical exegesis uncovering legal and moral insights.
- **Halakha vs. Aggadah**: Legal/normative Talmudic rulings versus narrative, ethical, and theological parables.
- **Rashi**: 11th-century French scholar whose concise commentaries remain indispensable to Torah and Talmud study.
