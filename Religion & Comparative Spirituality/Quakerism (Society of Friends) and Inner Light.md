# Quakerism (Society of Friends) and Inner Light

## Overview
Founded in 17th-century England by George Fox during the English Civil War, the Religious Society of Friends (Quakers) dispensed with salaried clergy, creeds, and sacraments—gathering in expectant silence to listen for the universal 'Inner Light' of God within every human being.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Nicknamed 'Quakers' for trembling under spiritual conviction, early Friends shocked contemporaries by refusing hat-honoring deference to aristocrats, using egalitarian 'thee/thou,' encouraging women preachers (Margaret Fell), and pioneering nonviolent 'Peace Testimony.' William Penn established Pennsylvania as a 'Holy Experiment' in religious toleration and fair Indigenous treaties.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Modern Friends span unprogrammed silent meetings (sitting together in stillness until someone feels led to speak) alongside programmed pastoral Evangelical Friends in East Africa and Latin America. Consistently opposing slavery (John Woolman), prison cruelty, and war, Quakers earned the 1947 Nobel Peace Prize.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Quaker consensus decision-making ('sense of the meeting') profoundly influenced modern activist organizing and peaceful mediation.

## Key Terminology
- **Inner Light**: Quaker belief in direct, unmediated divine presence accessible inside every human conscience.
- **Unprogrammed worship**: Silent communal Quaker waiting without clergy, sermon, or pre-planned liturgy.
- **Peace Testimony**: Historic Quaker commitment rejecting warfare and addressing root causes of conflict.
- **Sense of the meeting**: Quaker non-voting discernment seeking spiritual unity rather than majoritarian override.
- **William Penn**: Quaker founder of Pennsylvania as a tolerant haven for persecuted sects.
- **AFSC (American Friends Service Committee)**: Nobel Peace Prize-winning Quaker humanitarian and peace organization.
