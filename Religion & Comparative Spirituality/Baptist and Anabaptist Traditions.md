# Baptist and Anabaptist Traditions

## Overview
Rejecting infant baptism in favor of conscious adult commitment, 16th-century Anabaptists and 17th-century Baptists pioneered church-state separation, congregational self-governance, and grassroots missionary expansion.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Emerging in 1525 Zurich, Radical Reformation Anabaptists ('re-baptizers'—Mennonites, Hutterites, and later Amish) practiced strict nonresistance, refused oaths and military service, and faced brutal execution from both Catholics and magisterial Protestants. Independently, English Puritan separatists John Smyth and Thomas Helwys launched the Baptist movement in 1609 Amsterdam, demanding total liberty of conscience for Jews, Muslims, and heretics alike.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Organized without bishops via autonomous local congregations ('congregational polity'), Baptists exploded across the American South—forging both the Black Baptist prophetic church central to the Civil Rights Movement and the Southern Baptist Convention.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Meanwhile, Old Order Amish preserve selective technological boundaries (*Ordnung*) to protect tight-knit face-to-face agrarian family life.

## Key Terminology
- **Believer's baptism**: Practice reserving baptism strictly for individuals old enough to profess personal faith consciously.
- **Congregational polity**: Church governance placing final local authority inside each autonomous congregation rather than bishops.
- **Anabaptism**: 16th-century Radical Reformation movement emphasizing discipleship, pacifism, and church-state separation.
- **Ordnung**: Unwritten community discipline guiding Old Order Amish clothing, work, and technology limits.
- **Soul competency**: Baptist theological principle affirming each person's direct responsibility before God.
- **Black Baptist tradition**: Independent African American church network anchoring abolition, mutual aid, and civil rights.
