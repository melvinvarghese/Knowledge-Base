# Philosophy of Religion: Arguments for God

## Overview
Philosophy of religion applies rigorous logic and metaphysics to evaluate religious claims—most famously classical arguments for and against God's existence across cosmological, teleological (design), ontological, and moral frameworks.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Cosmological arguments (Aristotle's Unmoved Mover, Aquinas's Five Ways, Islamic *Kalām* argument revived by William Lane Craig) ask why contingent reality exists rather than nothing, concluding an eternal, necessary First Cause must ground the universe. Teleological (design) arguments evolved from William Paley's 1802 Watchmaker analogy—weakened by Darwinian natural selection—into modern 'fine-tuning' arguments noting how narrowly physical constants (gravity, cosmological constant) permit life.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Most audaciously, Anselm of Canterbury's 1078 Ontological Argument reasoned purely from definition: God is 'that than which nothing greater can be conceived'; since existing in reality is greater than existing only in mind, God *must* exist—famously challenged by Kant ('existence is not a predicate') yet refined via modal logic by Alvin Plantinga.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Contemporary philosophers pair these with Richard Swinburne's cumulative Bayesian case alongside rigorous atheist critiques by David Hume, Bertrand Russell, and J.L. Mackie.

## Key Terminology
- **Cosmological argument**: Inference from the existence or beginning of contingent things to a necessary First Cause.
- **Teleological argument**: Design argument inferring purposeful intelligence from cosmic order or physical fine-tuning.
- **Ontological argument**: A priori deduction attempting to prove God's existence purely from the concept of maximal greatness.
- **Fine-tuning**: Observation that fundamental physical constants fall within razor-thin ranges required for complex life.
- **Principle of Sufficient Reason**: Leibnizian axiom that every contingent fact must possess an explanation for why it is so.
- **Pascal's Wager**: Pragmatic decision-theoretic argument weighing infinite potential reward of belief against finite cost.
