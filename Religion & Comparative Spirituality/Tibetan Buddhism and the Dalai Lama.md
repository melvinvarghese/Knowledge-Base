# Tibetan Buddhism and the Dalai Lama

## Overview
Preserving late Indian monastic scholarship (*Nālandā* logic) alongside Tantric Vajrayana meditation and indigenous Tibetan *Bön* motifs, Tibetan Buddhism forged a rich spiritual civilization centered on compassion, *mandala* visualization, and reincarnated *tulku* lineages.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Established in Tibet during the 7th–11th centuries through King Songtsen Gampo, Tantric master Padmasambhava, and scholar Atiśa, Tibetan Buddhism developed four major lineages—Nyingma, Kagyu, Sakya, and Gelug (founded by Tsongkhapa, combining strict monastic celibacy with rigorous philosophical debate). Rather than rejecting emotion, Tantric practices transform anger, desire, and pride via sacred *mandalas*, *mantras* (such as *Om Mani Padme Hum*), and *Lojong* ('mind training' exchanging self and others).

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Uniquely, Tibetan institutions sustain spiritual continuity through recognized *tulkus*—lamas believed to choose intentional rebirth to continue guiding students—most famously the Dalai Lama (viewed as an emanation of Avalokiteśvara, Bodhisattva of Compassion).

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Following 1959 Chinese annexation and exile in Dharamsala, the 14th Dalai Lama Tenzin Gyatso pioneered dialogue between Buddhist mind-science and Western neuroscientists via the Mind and Life Institute.

## Key Terminology
- **Tulku**: Recognized reincarnated Tibetan spiritual teacher choosing rebirth out of compassion to continue teaching.
- **Dalai Lama**: Spiritual leader of the Gelug school and historic head of Tibet, revered as compassion incarnate.
- **Bardo**: Intermediate transitional state between death and rebirth mapped in the *Tibetan Book of the Dead*.
- **Mandala**: Sacred geometric cosmogram (often built in colored sand) representing an awakened Buddha's pure palace.
- **Tonglen**: Tibetan compassion meditation breathing in others' suffering and breathing out relief.
- **Nālandā tradition**: Classical Indian monastic university tradition of rigorous philosophical debate preserved in Tibet.
