# Chinese Traditional Architecture and Feng Shui

## Overview
Traditional Chinese architecture spans over three millennia of timber-frame engineering, axial courtyard compounds, and sweeping upturned ceramic roofs. Governed by cosmological principles of harmony between heaven, earth, and human hierarchy (feng shui), the system scaled seamlessly from humble rural homes to the imperial Forbidden City.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Unlike Western stone masonry, Chinese monumental architecture relied on a flexible post-and-beam wooden skeleton supporting heavy glazed tile roofs. Its signature structural innovation is the dougong — an interlocking system of wooden brackets stacked atop columns without nails to transfer immense roof loads outward while flexing safely during earthquakes. Raised stone podiums protected timber columns from ground moisture, and standardized modular bays (jian) allowed builders to prefabricate components rapidly, codified in the Song-dynasty building manual Yingzao Fashi (1103).

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Spatially, Chinese architecture favored horizontal courtyard complexes (siheyuan) over vertical towers. Homes, temples, and palaces arranged inward-facing pavilions around sunlit courtyards along a strict north-south axis, shielding private family life from street dust while expressing Confucian social hierarchy — culminating in Beijing's Forbidden City (1406–20), a vast sequence of ceremonial gates, marble terraces, and yellow-roofed halls aligned with the cosmic pole star. Site selection and orientation followed feng shui ('wind-water'), balancing mountains behind (north) for protection against cold winds with flowing water and sunlight in front (south) to cultivate vital qi.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Chinese timber-frame engineering directly shaped Korean and Japanese architecture. Following 20th-century rapid urbanization and modernist concrete towers, contemporary Chinese architects like Wang Shu (Pritzker Prize, 2012) and Ma Yansong recycle traditional grey bricks, courtyard voids, and landscape metaphors into award-winning contemporary civic buildings.

## Key Terminology
- **Dougong**: Interlocking wooden bracket system atop Chinese timber columns distributing heavy overhanging roof loads without nails.
- **Siheyuan**: Traditional Chinese rectangular courtyard house enclosing four inward-facing pavilions around a central open court.
- **Feng shui**: Ancient Chinese geomantic practice aligning buildings with terrain, wind, water, and solar orientation to harmonize qi.
- **Yingzao Fashi**: 1103 Song-dynasty state architectural treatise standardizing timber modules, joinery, and construction costs.
- **Jian**: Standard rectangular spatial bay defined by four timber columns, serving as the modular building block of Chinese halls.
- **Spirit screen (Yingbi)**: Freestanding decorative wall placed inside a courtyard entrance to block evil spirits and preserve domestic privacy.
