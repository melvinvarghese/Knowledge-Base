# Indian Classical Music

## Overview
Indian classical music comprises two major related traditions — Hindustani (North India) and Carnatic (South India) — both built on the raga (melodic framework) and tala (rhythmic cycle) as their fundamental organizing principles. Among the world's most sophisticated and ancient living musical traditions, Indian classical music encompasses an elaborate theoretical system, an improvisation-centered performance practice, and a guru-shishya (teacher-student) transmission tradition of unbroken continuity.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
The theoretical foundation of Indian classical music is documented in the Natyashastra of Bharata Muni (c. 200 BCE–200 CE), which codified the connection between music, drama, and emotion (rasa theory). The raga — from the Sanskrit rāga, meaning 'color' or 'emotion' — is a melodic framework specifying a scale (a subset of pitches from the chromatic gamut), characteristic ascent and descent patterns (ārohana and avarohana), characteristic ornaments (gamak, meend), and an emotional mood (rasa) associated with particular times of day, seasons, and contexts. There are hundreds of ragas in Hindustani music (the North Indian tradition, influenced by Persian and Mughal court culture) and an analogous number in Carnatic music (the South Indian tradition, more insulated from Islamic influence). The tala — rhythmic cycle — specifies a repeated time unit divided into sections with characteristic patterns of emphasis; common Hindustani talas include Teentaal (16 beats), Jhaptaal (10), and Rupak (7); Carnatic music uses an overlapping but distinct set including Adi (8 beats, the most common), Rupaka (6), and Misra Chapu (7). The first beat of a cycle (sam) is a structural anchor around which improvisation is organized.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Performance in both traditions follows a broadly similar formal arc. In Hindustani music, an instrumental (sitar, sarod, bansuri, santoor) or vocal performance typically begins with an extended alap (free, unaccompanied melodic exploration of the raga's character and mood), followed by a jor (rhythmic alap introducing pulse without formal tala), and vilambit (slow) and drut (fast) sections in formal tala accompanied by tabla drums. The vocalist performs khyal (the dominant vocal form since the 18th century), which involves two compositions (bandish) in slow and fast tempo, over which the singer improvises in elaborate melodic variations. In Carnatic music, the kriti — a composed song in three sections (pallavi, anupallavi, charanam) — is the central form, and performances include elaborate improvised sections (niraval, swarakalpana) built on the kriti's melodic material. Carnatic music is performed by voice or on violin, vina, flute, or mridangam (double-headed barrel drum) and ghatam (clay pot percussion). The guru-shishya parampara (teaching lineage) transmits not only repertoire but subtle performance nuances impossible to convey in notation; the tradition is essentially oral even where notation exists.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Indian classical music had a transformative influence on Western music in the 1960s when Ravi Shankar's collaborations with Yehudi Menuhin and the Beatles (George Harrison studying sitar with Shankar) introduced Western audiences to its aesthetic. Terry Riley and Philip Glass absorbed raga concepts of extended improvisation over a drone into American Minimalism. Jazz musicians (John Coltrane's modal explorations, particularly A Love Supreme, 1964, and his later late work) drew on Indian concepts of improvisation over drone and the relationship between melodic scale and spiritual state. Contemporary fusion (jazz-Indian, Western classical-Indian) is a substantial genre. Ravi Shankar's Concerto for Sitar and Orchestra (1971, 1981) brought the traditions into formal dialogue. Indian classical music maintains a large and committed global audience through the diaspora and through digital streaming that has made recordings of the great 20th-century masters (Ravi Shankar, Ali Akbar Khan, Bismillah Khan, Pandit Bhimsen Joshi, M.S. Subbulakshmi) widely accessible.

## Key Terminology
- **Raga**: Melodic framework specifying scale, characteristic phrases, ornaments, and emotional mood; the foundational concept of Indian classical music.
- **Tala**: Rhythmic cycle of a fixed number of beats divided into sections; the fundamental rhythmic framework of Indian classical performance.
- **Alap**: Opening section of a Hindustani performance: slow, free, unaccompanied melodic exploration of the raga's character.
- **Sam**: The first beat of a tala cycle; a structural anchor to which improvised melodic phrases return.
- **Khyal**: The dominant North Indian vocal form (from the Persian for 'thought/imagination'); involves a composed bandish and extensive improvisation.
- **Guru-shishya parampara**: The teacher-student lineage system through which Indian classical music is transmitted, emphasizing personal oral instruction over notation.
