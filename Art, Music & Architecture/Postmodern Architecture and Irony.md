# Postmodern Architecture and Irony

## Overview
Postmodern architecture emerged in the late 1960s and 1970s as a witty, pluralistic rebellion against the austere dogma of High Modernism. Reclaiming historical ornament, bold color, symbolic narrative, and vernacular pop references, Postmodernists replaced Mies van der Rohe's 'Less is more' with Robert Venturi's playful retort: 'Less is a bore.'

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
The movement crystallized around two pivotal critiques. Jane Jacobs's urbanism attacked modernist highway and tower-block clearances for killing street life, while Robert Venturi's Complexity and Contradiction in Architecture (1966) and Learning from Las Vegas (1972, with Denise Scott Brown and Steven Izenour) urged architects to embrace ambiguity, commercial signage, and decorated sheds rather than pure abstract boxes. In 1972, the demolition of the troubled Pruitt-Igoe modernist housing complex in St. Louis was famously declared by critic Charles Jencks as the exact moment Modern architecture died.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Postmodern architects deployed 'double coding' — speaking simultaneously to elite architectural history and everyday popular culture. Philip Johnson topped Manhattan's AT&T Building (1984) with a giant Chippendale-furniture broken pediment; Michael Graves adorned the Portland Building (1982) with pastel garlands and oversized keystones; Charles Moore staged playful Italianate piazzas in New Orleans's Piazza d'Italia (1978); and James Stirling fused stone classicism with bright pink and green tubular handrails at Stuttgart's Neue Staatsgalerie (1984). In Japan, Arata Isozaki and Kisho Kurokawa blended classical European geometry with Metabolism and local craft.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
By the 1990s, critics attacked commercial Postmodernism as superficial 'theme-park' pastiche, paving the way for Deconstructivism (Frank Gehry, Zaha Hadid) and sleek Neo-Modernism. Yet Postmodernism permanently liberated architecture from stylistic orthodoxy, legitimizing historic preservation, contextual sensitivity, and symbolic meaning.

## Key Terminology
- **Double coding**: Charles Jencks's term for Postmodern buildings communicating simultaneously to architectural insiders and general public.
- **Decorated shed**: Venturi and Scott Brown's concept of a simple functional structure adorned with applied symbolic ornament or signage.
- **Duck (architecture)**: Building whose overall sculptural shape literally symbolizes its function (e.g., a duck-shaped poultry shop).
- **Pastiche**: Playful or ironic collage of historical styles, columns, and motifs detached from original structural logic.
- **Deconstructivism**: Late-1980s successor movement (Gehry, Eisenman, Hadid) fracturing rectilinear geometry into colliding, unstable forms.
- **Contextualism**: Design approach tailoring new buildings to echo the scale, materials, and streetscape of surrounding historic neighborhoods.
