# Oceanic and Indigenous Pacific Art

## Overview
Oceanic art encompasses the rich visual and performative traditions of Melanesia, Polynesia, Micronesia, and Aboriginal Australia. Rooted in ancestral lineage, spiritual mana, navigation, and community ritual, Pacific arts span carved meeting houses, barkcloth textiles, tattooing, ephemeral dance masks, and millennia-old rock paintings.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
In Polynesia, artistic energy centered on hereditary prestige and sacred power (mana and tapu). Māori artisans in Aotearoa (New Zealand) carved intricate spiral motifs (koru) into cedarwood meeting houses (wharenui), war canoes, and nephrite jade pendants (hei-tiki), while preserving genealogy through sacred facial tattooing (tā moko). Across Tonga, Samoa, and Fiji, women beat inner paper-mulberry bark into patterned barkcloth (tapa or siapo) stained with earthy pigments for ceremonial exchange. On Rapa Nui (Easter Island), monumental volcanic tuff moai embodied deified ancestors watching over clan territories.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Melanesian art — notably along Papua New Guinea's Sepik River and in New Ireland — prioritized theatrical ceremony, initiation, and ancestral spirit presence. Artists crafted towering polychrome spirit boards, woven yam masks, and intricate openwork malagan carvings burned or discarded after mortuary rites because their power lay in the ritual act rather than permanent display. In Australia, Aboriginal artists sustained the world's oldest continuous visual tradition — from Arnhem Land X-ray rock art to Central Desert acrylic dot paintings launched at Papunya Tula in 1971 to map ancestral Dreaming (Tjukurrpa) tracks without revealing sacred secrets to outsiders.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Early-20th-century European avant-gardes — especially German Expressionists like Emil Nolde and Surrealists like André Breton — avidly collected Melanesian sculpture for its bold formal invention, though often stripping it of indigenous cultural context. Today, contemporary Pacific artists like Michael Tuffery, Lisa Reihana, and Emily Kame Kngwarreye lead global biennials, asserting living indigenous sovereignty and environmental stewardship.

## Key Terminology
- **Mana**: Polynesian concept of spiritual power, prestige, and ancestral potency embodied in sacred objects and leaders.
- **Wharenui**: Carved Māori communal meeting house symbolizing the physical body of a founding ancestor.
- **Tapa (Barkcloth)**: Non-woven textile made across Polynesia and Fiji by beating and felting wet inner bark, decorated with geometric prints.
- **Malagan**: Intricately carved New Ireland wooden sculptures created for mortuary ceremonies and subsequently destroyed or retired.
- **Tā moko**: Sacred Māori tattooing using chisels to carve genealogical and status designs into skin.
- **Tjukurrpa (The Dreaming)**: Aboriginal Australian spiritual framework linking ancestral creation stories, land geography, and visual iconography.
