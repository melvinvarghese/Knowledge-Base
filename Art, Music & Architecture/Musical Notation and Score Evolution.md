# Musical Notation and Score Evolution

## Overview
Musical notation is the visual encoding of pitch, duration, dynamics, and expression, allowing complex compositions to outlive human memory and be faithfully realized across centuries. From ancient Greek letter symbols and medieval neumes to 20th-century graphic scores and digital MIDI files, notation actively shaped what composers could imagine.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Oral transmission governed early music, yet expanding monastic liturgies demanded reliable memory aids. In the 9th century, scribes added neumes — curving strokes above Latin chant texts — to indicate rising or falling melodic contours. Around 1025, Guido d'Arezzo introduced a four-line staff with colored lines and clefs, fixing exact relative pitch for the first time, alongside solfège syllables. By the 13th century, mensural notation (codified by Franco of Cologne and Philippe de Vitry) added distinct note shapes to specify exact rhythmic durations, enabling intricate polyphony to align vertically.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Printing technology revolutionized distribution when Ottaviano Petrucci published the first polyphonic music using movable type in Venice (1501). Across the Baroque and Classical eras, scores standardized the five-line staff, barlines, key signatures, Italian tempo markings (allegro, adagio), and dynamic hairpins, giving composers meticulous control over 90-piece orchestras. In the mid-20th century, however, avant-garde composers felt traditional staff notation constrained electronic timbres and indeterminate forms: George Crumb bent staves into spirals, Karlheinz Stockhausen wrote proportional time grids, and Cornelius Cardew and John Cage created purely visual graphic scores inviting open performer interpretation.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Notation is never neutral: staff notation's grid favors discrete 12-tone pitches and rational meter, struggling to capture microtonal bends, swung jazz grooves, or West African timelines. Today, computer engraving software (LilyPond, Dorico, Sibelius), digital MIDI piano rolls, and spectrogram displays coexist with centuries-old staff notation.

## Key Terminology
- **Mensural notation**: Late-medieval system using distinct notehead and stem shapes to specify exact proportional rhythmic durations.
- **Clef**: Symbol placed at the start of a musical staff assigning a specific pitch to a reference line (e.g., Treble G, Bass F).
- **Tablature**: Instrument-specific notation showing finger positions on frets or strings rather than abstract pitch names.
- **Graphic notation**: 20th-century scoring using abstract shapes, lines, or diagrams instead of standard staves to invite free interpretation.
- **Full score**: Large-format master notation stacking every orchestral or ensemble part vertically for the conductor.
- **MIDI (Musical Instrument Digital Interface)**: 1983 digital protocol encoding pitch, velocity, and timing events as structured data rather than audio waveforms.
