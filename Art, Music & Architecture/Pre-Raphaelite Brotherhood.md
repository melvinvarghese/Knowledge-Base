# Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood

## Overview
Founded in London in 1848 by seven young artists and writers — led by Dante Gabriel Rossetti, John Everett Millais, and William Holman Hunt — the Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood rebelled against the Royal Academy's formulaic adherence to Raphael and dark academic chiaroscuro, advocating luminous color, botanical fidelity, and medieval or literary subjects.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
The Brotherhood rejected what they saw as the theatrical conventions introduced after Raphael, looking instead to 15th-century Italian and Flemish painting for sincerity, crisp detail, and moral seriousness. Championed by critic John Ruskin, who urged artists to 'go to nature in all singleness of heart,' they painted directly onto wet white grounds to make colors glow like stained glass. Millais's Ophelia (1851–52) combined painstakingly observed riverbank flora painted outdoors with tragic Shakespearean drama; his Christ in the House of His Parents (1849–50) shocked Victorian critics by portraying the Holy Family in a gritty, unidealized carpenter's shop. Hunt's The Awakening Conscience (1853) and Ford Madox Brown's Work (1852–65) addressed contemporary Victorian morality, class, and labor.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
By the mid-1850s the original Brotherhood dissolved, bifurcating into distinct trajectories. Rossetti, joined by Edward Burne-Jones and William Morris, pivoted toward aestheticism, Arthurian legend, and languid, sensual portraiture (Proserpine, 1874) that anticipated Symbolism. Morris expanded Pre-Raphaelite medievalism into the Arts and Crafts movement, founding Morris & Co. (1861) to revive hand-printed wallpapers, stained glass, tapestries, and book design (Kelmscott Press) as an antidote to industrial alienation. Female artists and models such as Elizabeth Siddal, Jane Morris, and Evelyn De Morgan played vital creative roles, though Victorian historiography long marginalized their contributions.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Though mocked by modernists as overly literary and sentimental, the Pre-Raphaelites reshaped Victorian visual culture and design. Their decorative revival fed directly into Art Nouveau and the Arts and Crafts ethos, while their dreamlike medievalism influenced Symbolist painters, early cinema set design, and fantasy illustration. Today their works remain among the most popular holdings of Tate Britain and the Birmingham Museum.

## Key Terminology
- **Wet-white ground**: Pre-Raphaelite technique of painting translucent colors over a fresh white primer to maximize luminosity.
- **Aestheticism**: Late-Victorian movement asserting 'art for art's sake,' prioritizing sensory beauty over didactic moralizing.
- **Arts and Crafts movement**: Design reform movement led by William Morris advocating handmade craftsmanship over industrial mass production.
- **Kelmscott Press**: Private press founded by William Morris in 1891 to revive medieval typography, woodcut borders, and fine bookmaking.
- **Typological symbolism**: Visual method encoding religious or moral prophecy within realistic everyday objects (favored by William Holman Hunt).
- **Stunner**: Pre-Raphaelite slang for their favored model type — women with heavy-lidded eyes, strong jawlines, and flowing hair.
