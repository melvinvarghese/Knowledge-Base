# Minimalism in Music

## Overview
Musical Minimalism emerged in the United States in the 1960s as a reaction against serial complexity and academic Modernism. Using repetition, gradual process, rhythmic phase-shifting, and tonal consonance, composers Philip Glass, Steve Reich, Terry Riley, and La Monte Young created hypnotic, long-duration works that reached audiences that serialism had alienated, eventually influencing pop, electronic, and film music.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Terry Riley's In C (1964) is often identified as the first Minimalist work: a collection of 53 short melodic fragments, each repeated at the performer's discretion, played by any ensemble over a steady C pulse — a deliberately open structure allowing emergence from the collective activity of performers. La Monte Young had earlier explored extended duration and drone (The Well-Tuned Piano, begun 1964) drawing on Indian classical music's sustained tonal centers. Steve Reich's tape pieces — It's Gonna Rain (1965) and Come Out (1966) — discovered phasing: two copies of the same recording played simultaneously gradually drift out of synchronization, creating an ever-shifting composite of overlapping repetitions. He extended this into instrumental form in Piano Phase (1967) and Violin Phase (1967), and to larger ensembles in Drumming (1970–71) and Music for 18 Musicians (1976). Philip Glass developed a New York Minimalist idiom of rapid arpeggiated figures in additive and subtractive rhythmic processes: his collaborations with director Robert Wilson produced the operas Einstein on the Beach (1976), Satyagraha (1980), and Akhnaten (1983), reaching audiences outside traditional concert halls.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Minimalism's formal procedures include repetition (the same material repeated many times before any change), gradual process (change occurring over very long time spans — Reich's Gradual Process manifesto, 1968, stipulated that the process must be audible and inevitable), phase-shifting (systematic temporal displacement of identical patterns creating rhythmic interference), additive rhythm (building rhythmic cycles by adding one note at a time), and tonal stasis (remaining in one key or modal area without traditional harmonic movement). The aesthetic was influenced by African drumming (Reich studied Ghanaian drumming in Ghana, 1970), Balinese gamelan (which both Reich and Glass encountered), Indian classical music (Riley and Glass studied with Pandit Pran Nath), and the extended duration of La Monte Young's Fluxus events. The Estonian composer Arvo Pärt developed an independent parallel path: Tintinnabuli ('little bells'), a technique of combining a single voice moving in a scale with another voice arpeggiated from a triad, producing a serene, bell-like consonance heard in works like Spiegel im Spiegel (1978) and the Berliner Messe (1990).

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Minimalism transformed the landscape of new music by demonstrating that contemporary composition could reach general audiences. Glass and Reich became among the best-known living composers by the late 20th century. Film scoring absorbed Minimalist technique: Glass's scores for Koyaanisqatsi (1982), The Hours (2002), and Notes on a Scandal (2006); Jóhann Jóhannsson; Ennio Morricone's late style; and John Adams's chamber music all draw on Minimalist process. Electronic dance music (techno, house, trance) is structurally indebted to Minimalism's repetition and gradual process. Post-Minimalism produced a generation of American composers (John Adams, David Lang, Michael Gordon, Julia Wolfe) who maintained Minimalism's tonal accessibility while reintroducing narrative complexity and emotional directness. World music — especially rhythmically complex African and South Asian music — has been in ongoing dialogue with Minimalism since the 1970s.

## Key Terminology
- **Phasing**: Steve Reich's technique of playing two identical patterns simultaneously but at slightly different speeds, creating an ever-shifting composite.
- **Additive rhythm**: Building a rhythmic cycle by adding one beat at a time, creating patterns of increasing length.
- **Tintinnabuli**: Arvo Pärt's compositional technique pairing a melodic line in a scale with an arpeggiated tonic triad in a companion voice.
- **Gradual process**: Reich's principle that the compositional process must be audible and proceed inevitably — the listener should be able to hear what is happening and what will happen.
- **In C (1964)**: Terry Riley's open-form piece for any instruments playing 53 melodic fragments over a pulse; the founding work of American Minimalism.
- **Post-Minimalism**: Later generation of composers (Adams, Lang, Wolfe) maintaining Minimalism's tonal language while introducing narrative complexity and emotional intensity.
