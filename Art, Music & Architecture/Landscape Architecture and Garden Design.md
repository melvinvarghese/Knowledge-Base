# Landscape Architecture and Garden Design

## Overview
Landscape architecture designs outdoor spaces — from intimate contemplative gardens to vast municipal parks and ecological wetlands — by orchestrating terrain, water, vegetation, and human circulation. Across civilizations, gardens have functioned as microcosms of paradise, expressions of political power, and vital green infrastructure for dense cities.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Ancient Persian chahar bagh ('four-part') paradise gardens divided walled enclosures into symmetrical quadrants fed by crossed water channels, a plan carried into Mughal tombs like the Taj Mahal and Spanish Moorish courtyards. In East Asia, Chinese scholar gardens and Japanese Zen dry-landscape gardens (karesansui, such as Ryōan-ji in Kyoto) rejected axial symmetry in favor of asymmetrically balanced rocks, raked gravel, moss, and 'borrowed scenery' (shakkei) framing distant mountains. In 17th-century France, André Le Nôtre expanded Renaissance Italian terraced gardens into the Baroque geometry of Versailles, whereas 18th-century English designers (William Kent, Capability Brown) swept away formal hedges to sculpt rolling 'naturalistic' pastures, lakes, and Classical follies.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Modern landscape architecture emerged as a civic profession in the 19th century through Frederick Law Olmsted and Calvert Vaux, whose design for New York's Central Park (1858) carved sunken transverse roads, lakes, and meadows to provide democratic 'lungs for the city' amid industrial crowding. In the 20th century, Roberto Burle Marx in Brazil fused modernist painting with native tropical flora, while Ian McHarg's Design with Nature (1969) pioneered ecological GIS mapping so development respects watersheds and soil.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Today, landscape architects lead urban climate adaptation: transforming abandoned elevated railways into linear parks (New York's High Line), designing 'sponge cities' and coastal flood wetlands (Kongjian Yu), and restoring post-industrial brownfields into thriving civic ecosystems.

## Key Terminology
- **Chahar bagh**: Classical Persian quadrilateral garden layout divided by four water channels symbolizing the four rivers of paradise.
- **Karesansui**: Japanese Zen dry-landscape garden using raked white gravel and carefully placed boulders to evoke water and mountains.
- **Shakkei (Borrowed scenery)**: East Asian garden principle incorporating distant mountains, pagodas, or trees outside the garden into the visual composition.
- **Picturesque**: 18th-century English aesthetic valuing irregular, painterly, asymmetrical natural landscapes over formal geometry.
- **Sponge city**: Urban landscape strategy using permeable parks, wetlands, and bioswales to absorb stormwater and prevent flooding.
- **Brownfield remediation**: Ecological restoration and landscape redesign of contaminated former industrial sites into safe public parks.
