# Brutalist Architecture and Raw Concrete

## Overview
Brutalism (c. 1950s–1970s) was an unapologetically monumental architectural movement defined by raw, unfinished concrete (béton brut), bold geometric massing, and visible structural honesty. Embracing heavy social and civic programs — universities, public housing, libraries, and government centers — Brutalism remains one of the most polarizing styles in modern history.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
The term derives not from 'brutal' in English, but from Le Corbusier's French phrase béton brut ('raw concrete'), used to describe the rough, wooden-formwork texture of his Unité d'Habitation apartment slab in Marseille (1952). British critics Alison and Peter Smithson popularized 'New Brutalism' (1953–55) as an ethical commitment to expose every material, pipe, and structural joint without decorative cladding. Following World War II, European welfare states and expanding public universities embraced raw cast-in-place concrete because it was inexpensive, fireproof, and sculpturally expressive.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Brutalist landmarks span continents with striking variety: Paul Rudolph's Art and Architecture Building at Yale (1963) with hammered vertical concrete ribs; Boston City Hall (Kallmann McKinnell & Knowles, 1968); London's Barbican Estate and National Theatre (Denys Lasdun); Moshe Safdie's modular Habitat 67 in Montreal; and Kenzo Tange's Yoyogi National Gymnasium and Yamanashi Communications Center in Japan. In Latin America and Eastern Europe — notably Oscar Niemeyer, Lina Bo Bardi's SESC Pompeia in São Paulo, and Soviet memorials — raw concrete gained dramatic civic scale.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
By the 1980s, weathered stained concrete, leaky flat roofs, and associations with austere state bureaucracy turned public opinion sharply against Brutalism, leading to high-profile demolitions. Yet in the 21st century, preservationists and younger architects celebrate its sculptural honesty, communal social ideals, and enduring monumentality, sparking global conservation campaigns (SOS Brutalism).

## Key Terminology
- **Béton brut**: French for 'raw concrete' left exposed straight from wooden molds, revealing grain marks and tie-rod holes.
- **New Brutalism**: 1950s British architectural ethic championed by the Smithsons emphasizing material honesty and exposed mechanical systems.
- **Board-formed concrete**: Concrete cast against rough wooden planks so the cured surface retains timber grain textures.
- **Megastructure**: Massive interconnected urban complex integrating housing, transit, and civic amenities into one concrete frame.
- **Bush-hammered concrete**: Concrete surface mechanically chipped after curing to expose interior stone aggregate and catch dramatic light.
- **Social housing**: State-funded public residential projects that formed a major canvas for postwar Brutalist experimentation.
