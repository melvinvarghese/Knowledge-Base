# Photorealism and Hyperrealism

## Overview
Photorealism emerged in the late 1960s and early 1970s in the United States as painters and sculptors used photographs as source material to reconstruct everyday scenes with astonishing optical precision. Later evolving into Hyperrealism, the movement reversed modernism's taboo against illusionism by scrutinizing how cameras mediate reality.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Rather than painting directly from life like 19th-century Realists, Photorealists deliberately copied the mechanical gaze of the camera lens — including shallow depth of field, lens flare, chrome reflections, and cropped framing. Chuck Close projected gridded Polaroid portraits onto massive canvases, airbrushing and hand-painting tiny cells to achieve monumental close-ups where skin pores became abstract landscapes. Richard Estes painted gleaming Manhattan storefronts and diner windows with razor-sharp reflections, Audrey Flack rendered glossy vanitas still lifes with airbrush precision, and Ralph Goings documented classic American pickup trucks and diners.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
In the 2000s, Hyperrealism extended Photorealist precision through high-resolution digital photography and sculptural illusionism. While Photorealists maintained a cool, detached fidelity to flat photographic grain, Hyperrealists often heightened emotional tension, texture, and tactile presence beyond what a normal camera records. Sculptors Duane Hanson (in the 1970s) and Ron Mueck (from the 1990s) cast polyester resin, fiberglass, and silicone figures complete with implanted hairs, veins, and real clothing — Hanson at exact life scale to fool museum visitors, Mueck at jarring colossal or miniature scales to evoke uncanny psychological vulnerability.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Critics initially dismissed Photorealism as soulless technical copying, yet it endured by anticipating a media-saturated culture where human perception is perpetually filtered through screens and lenses. Today, Photorealist and Hyperrealist painters command strong international collector interest and inspire contemporary digital 3D rendering standards.

## Key Terminology
- **Photorealism**: Late-1960s movement reproducing photographic images in paint with mechanical optical fidelity, including lens distortion.
- **Hyperrealism**: 21st-century evolution of Photorealism heightening tactile detail, depth, and emotional intensity beyond standard camera optics.
- **Grid transfer**: Method of dividing a reference photograph and canvas into matching squares to scale proportions accurately.
- **Airbrush**: Compressed-air paint sprayer used by Photorealists to achieve seamless, brushstroke-free tonal gradients.
- **Uncanny valley**: Psychological unease triggered when hyper-realistic sculptures (like Mueck's or Hanson's) appear almost, but not quite, alive.
- **Depth of field**: Optical photographic effect rendering one focal plane crisp while blurring foreground and background, faithfully copied in Photorealist painting.
