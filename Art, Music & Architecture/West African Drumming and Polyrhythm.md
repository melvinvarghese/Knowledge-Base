# West African Drumming and Polyrhythm

## Overview
West African drumming encompasses centuries-old rhythmic, linguistic, and communal traditions spanning Mali, Senegal, Ghana, and Nigeria. Centered on polyrhythm — the simultaneous layering of contrasting rhythmic meters — and speech-mimicking drums, West African musical principles form the rhythmic bedrock of jazz, Afro-Cuban music, samba, funk, and hip-hop.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
In West African performance, rhythm is inseparable from dance, social ceremony, and language. Because many West African languages (such as Yoruba, Akan, and Wolof) are tonal, hourglass-shaped tension drums like the Yoruba dùndún or Tama ('talking drum') can squeeze leather cords beneath the arm to bend pitch and literally recite proverbs, praise poetry, and messages across distances. Alongside talking drums, ensembles feature goblet-shaped djembes (producing sharp slap, open tone, and deep bass strokes), cylindrical dunun bass drums, and Ghanaian Ewe barrel drums.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Structurally, West African ensembles rely on interlocking polyrhythm and polymeter rather than a single downbeat-driven pulse. A lead bell (gankogui) or rattle plays an asymmetrical timeline — an invariant cyclic pattern (such as the 12-pulse standard bell pattern) that anchors the entire ensemble. Over this timeline, supporting drums layer duple (two-beat) and triple (three-beat) subdivisions simultaneously, creating a shifting, multi-layered rhythmic tapestry where listeners can hear multiple pulses at once. Meanwhile, West African griots (jali) — hereditary oral historians — accompany praise epics on the 21-string kora harp-lute and wooden balafon xylophone.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Transatlantic enslavement violently carried West African rhythmic concepts — call-and-response, timeline cycles, timbre variation, and polyrhythmic layering — to the Americas, surviving in Cuban rumba clave, Brazilian candomblé, New Orleans second-line parades, and eventually jazz, blues, and hip-hop. In the 20th century, Steve Reich traveled to Ghana (1970) to study Ewe drumming, directly shaping Minimalist rhythmic phasing.

## Key Terminology
- **Polyrhythm**: Simultaneous use of two or more contrasting rhythmic subdivisions (such as 3 against 2) within a shared pulse.
- **Timeline pattern**: Recurring asymmetrical rhythmic motif played on an iron bell or woodblock that orients a West African ensemble.
- **Talking drum**: Hourglass tension drum whose pitch bends under arm pressure to imitate tonal spoken language.
- **Griot (Jali)**: West African hereditary musician, poet, and oral historian preserving lineages on instruments like the kora.
- **Djembe**: Rope-tuned goblet-shaped hand drum from West Africa capable of bass, tone, and sharp slap timbres.
- **Kora**: 21-string West African bridge-harp combining calabash resonator and lute-like neck, played by griots.
