# Land Art and Environmental Sculpture

## Overview
Land Art (or Earthworks) emerged in the late 1960s as artists left commercial galleries behind to carve, arrange, and reshape remote deserts, lakes, and forests using soil, stone, and natural processes. Rejecting art-market commodification, Land Artists turned landscape scale, entropy, and celestial time into sculptural media.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Disillusioned with gallery walls and Vietnam-era materialism, American artists headed to the vast arid expanses of the American West. Robert Smithson constructed Spiral Jetty (1970) from 6,650 tons of black basalt rock and earth coiling into Utah's Great Salt Lake — a site-specific work deliberately exposed to rising water levels, salt crystallization, and entropy. Michael Heizer excavated Double Negative (1969), displacing 240,000 tons of desert mesa in Nevada to sculpt pure empty space. Walter De Maria planted 400 stainless-steel poles across a New Mexico plain in The Lightning Field (1977) to attract electrical storms, while James Turrell spent decades reshaping Arizona's volcanic Roden Crater into a naked-eye celestial observatory.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
In Britain and Europe, Land Art adopted a quieter, pedestrian footprint. Richard Long walked remote moorlands and recorded his journeys by arranging stones in circles or lines (A Line Made by Walking, 1967), and Andy Goldsworthy built ephemeral sculptures from autumn leaves, ice, and river pebbles destined to dissolve within hours, surviving only through photography. Christo and Jeanne-Claude wrapped architectural monuments, coastlines, and islands in temporary fabric installations funded entirely by selling preparatory drawings, insisting the temporary public spectacle was the artwork itself.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Land Art fundamentally expanded sculpture into ecology, site-specificity, and deep geological time. While early earthworks occasionally drew environmental criticism for heavy bulldozing, contemporary eco-artists — such as Agnes Denes (planting a two-acre wheatfield in downtown Manhattan) and Olafur Eliasson — channel Land Art's scale toward climate awareness and ecological restoration.

## Key Terminology
- **Earthworks**: Large-scale Land Art sculptures constructed directly from soil, rock, and geological features in remote landscapes.
- **Site-specific art**: Work conceived for and physically bound to a particular location, losing its meaning if relocated to a gallery.
- **Non-site**: Robert Smithson's indoor gallery installations displaying rocks, maps, and photos pointing back to an outdoor geographical site.
- **Entropy (art)**: Theme explored by Smithson emphasizing natural decay, erosion, and transformation over timeless permanence.
- **Ephemeral art**: Works built from ice, leaves, or sand designed to weather and vanish naturally, preserved only via documentation.
- **Roden Crater**: James Turrell's monumental volcanic cinder-cone observatory in Arizona engineered to frame sky and celestial light.
