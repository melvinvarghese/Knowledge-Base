# Neoclassicism in Art

## Overview
Neoclassicism arose in the mid-18th century as a principled return to Greco-Roman antiquity in art, architecture, and design. Driven by Enlightenment ideals of reason, civic virtue, and moral seriousness, it rejected Baroque exuberance and Rococo frivolity in favor of clarity, restraint, and didactic narratives drawn from ancient history.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
The excavations of Pompeii (1748) and Herculaneum (1738) electrified European artists and collectors with direct evidence of Roman domestic life and aesthetic. Johann Joachim Winckelmann's Thoughts on the Imitation of Greek Works in Painting and Sculpture (1755) and History of Ancient Art (1764) provided the theoretical framework: Greek art embodied 'noble simplicity and serene grandeur' — the ideal to which modern artists should aspire. Artists flocked to Rome, where the French Academy (Villa Medici) trained successive generations in antique study. Jacques-Louis David synthesized these influences into the most powerful Neoclassical paintings: The Oath of the Horatii (1784) presented Roman civic sacrifice in a frozen, frieze-like composition of stark geometry and cool palette that became a visual manifesto for republican virtue. The Death of Socrates (1787) and Brutus (1789) extended the program, while his enormous Revolutionary and Napoleonic commissions (The Death of Marat, 1793; Coronation of Napoleon, 1807) adapted Neoclassical gravitas to contemporary political event. In Britain, Sir Joshua Reynolds urged the Grand Style; in Scandinavia and Germany, Asmus Jacob Carstens and Anton Raphael Mengs pursued pure line and severe form.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Neoclassical style in painting favored a restricted palette of cool earth tones offset by white and crimson, shallow pictorial space that pushed figures to the foreground like a stage relief, sharp contour lines, and suppressed brushwork that rendered surfaces smooth and enamel-like. Subjects came from Greco-Roman history, legend, and philosophy — the moment of virtuous decision, sacrifice, or noble death. Sculpture by Antonio Canova and Bertel Thorvaldsen translated antique models into polished white marble with unprecedented technical refinement: Canova's Psyche Revived by Cupid's Kiss (1787–93) and Pauline Borghese as Venus (1805–08) balanced idealization with sensuous surface. Architecture embraced the Doric and Ionic orders: the Madeleine in Paris, the British Museum's Ionic colonnade, the Altes Museum in Berlin (Schinkel, 1823–30), and Jefferson's University of Virginia (1817–26) all applied archaeological correctness to civic and institutional buildings. Decorative arts produced Wedgwood jasperware, Empire furniture with ormolu mounts, and neoclassical silver that filled the homes of Enlightened elites.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Neoclassicism's political dimension proved decisive: Revolutionary France adopted it as the visual language of republicanism; Napoleon redeployed it as imperial propaganda; the young American republic used it to claim legitimacy from Roman precedent. The Greek Revival swept the United States through the mid-19th century, from bank facades to plantation houses. Neoclassicism did not so much die as morph: it shaded into Academicism (the Beaux-Arts tradition) on one side and was challenged by Romanticism's subjectivity and nationalism on the other. Its influence persists in public architecture (courthouses, museums, government buildings worldwide use neoclassical elements) and in the preference for 'timeless' classical form in civic design.

## Key Terminology
- **Grand Style**: Sir Joshua Reynolds's doctrine that history painting in the classical mode represented the highest artistic achievement.
- **Relief composition**: Arrangement of figures parallel to the picture plane, as on an ancient frieze, favored in Neoclassical painting.
- **Greek Revival**: Architectural movement applying Doric and Ionic temple forms to 19th-century public and domestic buildings.
- **Jasperware**: Wedgwood stoneware with white classical relief on a colored (typically blue) ground; epitome of Neoclassical applied art.
- **Contour line**: Sharp, unbroken boundary of a form, prized in Neoclassical painting and drawing as a mark of discipline and reason.
- **Peinture d'histoire**: French term for history painting — the highest category in academic hierarchies, depicting morally instructive scenes from antiquity.
