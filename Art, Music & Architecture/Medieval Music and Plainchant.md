# Medieval Music and Plainchant

## Overview
Medieval music (c. 500–1400 CE) encompasses the sacred chant traditions of the Christian Church and the secular songs of troubadours, trouvères, and minnesingers, as well as the first experiments in polyphony that would transform Western music. Gregorian chant — monophonic (single-line) Latin liturgical song — formed the bedrock of medieval musical culture and the foundation from which all subsequent Western musical development grew.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Early Christian chant drew on Hebrew synagogue singing and Greek musical theory, developing distinct regional liturgical traditions (Ambrosian in Milan, Mozarabic in Iberia, Gallican in France) before the Carolingian consolidation under Charlemagne (c. 800 CE) promoted the Roman rite and standardized what became known as Gregorian chant — named for Pope Gregory I (590–604 CE), to whom tradition attributed its compilation. Gregorian chant is monophonic, non-metric, and modal (based on the eight church modes derived from Greek modal theory), sung in Latin and organized around the liturgical calendar. The notation of chant — using neumes (graphic symbols indicating melodic contour) above the text — developed in the 9th century and was gradually refined toward precise pitch indication by Guido d'Arezzo (c. 991–1050), who invented the four-line staff (precursor to the modern five-line staff) and solfège syllables (Ut, Re, Mi, Fa, Sol, La — from the hymn Ut queant laxis). Secular music developed simultaneously: the troubadours of Occitania (Aquitaine, Provence) composed vernacular lyric songs in Occitan from the late 11th century, addressing themes of courtly love (fin'amor); their northern French counterparts the trouvères and German minnesingers continued the tradition through the 13th century. The motet — a vocal composition adding new texts (and later, additional melodic lines) to existing chant fragments — became the dominant polyphonic form of the late medieval period.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
The Notre-Dame School in Paris (c. 1160–1240) produced the first named composers in Western music history: Léonin and Pérotin. Léonin compiled the Magnus Liber Organi (Great Book of Organum) — a collection of two-voice settings of plainchant for the liturgical year. Pérotin extended these to three and four voices (Viderunt omnes, 1198; Sederunt principes, 1199) of extraordinary rhythmic complexity and sonic richness. These works are among the earliest surviving large-scale composed polyphonic music. Rhythmic notation developed through the rhythmic modes — patterns of long and short values borrowed from Greek poetic meters. The Ars Nova ('New Art') of the 14th century, associated with Philippe de Vitry's treatise of that name (c. 1322) and Guillaume de Machaut's compositions, introduced more complex rhythmic independence between voices (isorhythm) and the first great composer who was both poet and musician. Machaut's Messe de Nostre Dame (c. 1365) is the first known unified polyphonic Mass setting by a single composer — a landmark in the history of the form.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Medieval music's legacy is vast but often invisible to general audiences. The eight church modes that organized Gregorian chant survived as the basis of Renaissance polyphony and left traces in folk music, jazz modal theory (Miles Davis's Kind of Blue is in the Dorian mode), and film scoring. The invention of musical notation is one of the most consequential acts in cultural history, enabling the preservation, transmission, and development of musical ideas across time and geography. Contemporary early music ensembles (the Hilliard Ensemble, Anonymous 4, Sequentia) have made medieval music accessible to modern audiences through recordings and concerts, sparking a significant revival of interest. Chant continues in Benedictine monasteries and in forms of contemporary spiritual and New Age music.

## Key Terminology
- **Plainchant / Gregorian chant**: Monophonic, non-metric Latin liturgical singing that formed the foundation of Western medieval music.
- **Neumes**: Early notational symbols written above text to indicate the general melodic direction of a chant phrase.
- **Church modes**: Eight scale patterns (Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Mixolydian, etc.) organizing medieval melody, derived from Greek modal theory.
- **Organum**: Early polyphony adding one or more voice parts above a plainchant melody; the first systematic form of Western polyphony.
- **Isorhythm**: Ars Nova technique repeating a rhythmic pattern (talea) independently of pitch repetitions (color) across the voices of a motet.
- **Troubadour**: Poet-musician of Occitania (c. 1100–1300) who composed vernacular lyric songs on courtly love in the Occitan language.
