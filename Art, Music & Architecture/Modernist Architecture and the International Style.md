# Modernist Architecture and the International Style

## Overview
Modernist architecture and the International Style (c. 1920–1970) revolutionized the built environment by stripping away historical ornament in favor of steel frames, reinforced concrete, plate glass, and open floor plans. Led by Le Corbusier, Ludwig Mies van der Rohe, Walter Gropius, and Frank Lloyd Wright, modernism promised rational, hygienic, machine-age living.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Enabled by industrial steel framing, elevators, and reinforced concrete slabs, architects freed exterior walls from supporting a building's weight. Le Corbusier crystallized this revolution in his 'Five Points of Architecture' (1926), realized cleanly at Villa Savoye (1928–31): pilotis (slender concrete stilts lifting the building off damp ground), a flat roof garden, an open interior floor plan, horizontal ribbon windows, and a non-load-bearing free facade. Calling a house 'a machine for living in,' Le Corbusier also proposed sweeping urban towers set in parks (Ville Radieuse) to replace crowded historic streets.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
In Germany, Walter Gropius's Bauhaus school united architecture with industrial design, while Ludwig Mies van der Rohe honed steel-and-glass elegance into 'Less is more' — seen in the Barcelona Pavilion (1929) and New York's Seagram Building (1958), whose bronze I-beams and recessed plaza defined the postwar corporate skyscraper. Following the 1932 MoMA exhibition curated by Henry-Russell Hitchcock and Philip Johnson, this stripped-down aesthetic was dubbed the 'International Style' for its ambition to transcend local climate and tradition. Meanwhile, Frank Lloyd Wright pursued a warmer 'organic architecture' in the United States, anchoring Prairie houses and Fallingwater (1935–39) into rocky landscapes with cantilevered concrete terraces.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
After World War II, International Style glass boxes proliferated across every global skyline — praised for structural clarity and abundant daylight, yet criticized for erasing regional identity, overheating in sunny climates, and inspiring alienating concrete housing blocks. By the 1970s, Brutalism and Postmodernism emerged directly from modernism's successes and failures.

## Key Terminology
- **Five Points of Architecture**: Le Corbusier's 1926 modernist manifesto: pilotis, flat roof terrace, open plan, ribbon windows, and free facade.
- **Curtain wall**: Non-load-bearing exterior skin (typically glass and aluminum) hung from an internal steel or concrete structural frame.
- **Pilotis**: Slender ground-level columns lifting a building above the terrain to free space for circulation or gardens.
- **Cantilever**: Horizontal structural beam or slab anchored at only one end, projecting dramatically into open space (e.g., Fallingwater).
- **Open (free) plan**: Interior layout freed from load-bearing walls, allowing flexible partitions and flowing spatial continuity.
- **International Style**: Mid-20th-century modernist idiom defined by rectilinear volumes, glass facades, and rejection of regional ornament.
