# Dada and Surrealism

## Overview
Dada (1916–c.1923) and Surrealism (1924–c.1950s) were the most radical avant-garde movements of the 20th century. Dada weaponized nonsense and chance against the rational civilization it blamed for World War I; Surrealism channeled Freudian theory into art and literature that explored the unconscious, dreams, and the irrational as pathways to deeper truth.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Dada was born in Zurich's Cabaret Voltaire (1916), where Hugo Ball, Emmy Hennings, Tristan Tzara, Jean Arp, and others responded to the horror of WWI with deliberate irrationality, sound poetry, chance collage, and theatrical provocation. Their conviction: if rational Western civilization had produced industrial slaughter, reason itself was bankrupt and art should become anti-art. Marcel Duchamp in New York independently developed the readymade — existing manufactured objects exhibited as artworks: Bicycle Wheel (1913), Bottle Rack (1914), and the notorious Fountain (1917, a urinal submitted to an exhibition under the pseudonym R. Mutt) — systematically questioning what art was and what institutions legitimized it. Richard Huelsenbeck, George Grosz, and John Heartfield transferred Dada to Berlin, where it became aggressively political and photomontage (cutting and recombining photographic images) became its weapon of choice. Kurt Schwitters in Hannover built the Merzbau — a room-sized assemblage of found debris — over many years, creating a private total environment. Dada dissolved as quickly as it formed, by design.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Surrealism was formally launched by André Breton's Manifesto of Surrealism (1924), which defined it as 'pure psychic automatism' — the unfiltered expression of unconscious thought, free from reason or aesthetic control. Breton drew on Freud's theories of the unconscious, dreams, and free association. Automatism in writing (automatic writing) and drawing (frottage, grattage, decalcomania) aimed to bypass conscious control. The movement attracted Salvador Dalí, Max Ernst, René Magritte, Joan Miró, Yves Tanguy, Meret Oppenheim, and Lee Miller, among many others. Dalí's hyper-realistic dream landscapes (The Persistence of Memory, 1931; The Elephants, 1948) used technical virtuosity to render impossible imagery convincing. Magritte's philosophical conundrums (The Treachery of Images [Ceci n'est pas une pipe], 1929) questioned representation itself. Ernst's frottage and collage novels created biomorphic alien worlds. Miró developed a symbolic visual language of signs, stars, and organic forms suggesting a pre-rational cosmos. Surrealism also permeated photography (Man Ray's solarized portraits and Rayographs), film (Luis Buñuel's Un chien andalou, 1929, and L'Âge d'or, 1930), and fashion (Elsa Schiaparelli's lobster dress, designed with Dalí).

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Dada's questioning of art's institutional legitimacy directly anticipated Conceptual Art, Installation Art, and Performance Art. The readymade remains perhaps the single most consequential artwork of the 20th century in terms of its ongoing generative force. Surrealism's vocabulary permeated advertising, film, fashion, and design throughout the 20th century; its influence on cinema (David Lynch, Terry Gilliam, Jan Švankmajer) and contemporary visual art (Kara Walker, Matthew Barney, Cindy Sherman) remains extensive. Feminist scholars reclaimed overlooked Surrealist women artists — Leonora Carrington, Remedios Varo, Meret Oppenheim, Dorothea Tanning — who often explored the female body and domestic space with subversive intelligence. Breton's authoritarian management of the Surrealist group and his homophobia have been extensively critiqued in revisionist scholarship.

## Key Terminology
- **Readymade**: Marcel Duchamp's category of artwork consisting of an unmodified manufactured object exhibited as art.
- **Automatism**: Surrealist technique of creating art or writing by bypassing conscious control to access unconscious expression.
- **Frottage**: Max Ernst's technique of rubbing pencil or charcoal over a textured surface to generate random imagery.
- **Photomontage**: Collage technique combining cut photographic images, used by Berlin Dadaists for political satire.
- **Decalcomania**: Surrealist technique of pressing paint between surfaces to create random organic textures.
- **Manifesto of Surrealism**: André Breton's 1924 founding text defining Surrealism as pure psychic automatism drawing on Freudian theory.
