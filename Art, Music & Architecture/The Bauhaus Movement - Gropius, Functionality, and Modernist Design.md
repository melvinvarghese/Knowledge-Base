# The Bauhaus Movement - Gropius, Functionality, and Modernist Design

## Overview
The Bauhaus school (founded in Weimar, Germany, 1919 by Walter Gropius) revolutionized 20th-century design by uniting fine art with industrial craft under the banner 'Form Follows Function'.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Integration of Art, Craft, & Mass Production: Rejected ornamental historicist revivalism. The Bauhaus curriculum combined fine artists (Kandinsky, Klee) with industrial craftsmen, emphasizing clean geometric lines, unadorned surfaces, and honest expression of industrial materials (tubular steel, plate glass, reinforced concrete).

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Global Dispersion & The International Style: Forced to close by the Nazi regime in 1933, Bauhaus masters (Gropius, Mies van der Rohe, Marcel Breuer) fled to the United States. Mies van der Rohe's architectural aphorisms 'Less is more' and 'God is in the details' became the defining ethos of Modernist glass-and-steel corporate skyscrapers.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Foundational standard for modern industrial product design, typography, interior architecture, and corporate visual identities.

## Key Terminology
- **Form Follows Function**: Modernist design principle stating that the shape of a building or object should primarily relate to its intended function or purpose.
- **International Style**: Architectural style of the 1930s–1970s characterized by rectilinear forms, glass curtain walls, and total lack of ornament.
- **Walter Gropius**: German architect who founded the Bauhaus School in 1919 and pioneered modern functionalist architecture.
