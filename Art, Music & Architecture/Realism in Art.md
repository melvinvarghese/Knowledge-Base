# Realism in Art

## Overview
Artistic Realism emerged in France in the 1840s–1860s as a deliberate turn toward the unidealized depiction of contemporary life. Led by Gustave Courbet, Jean-François Millet, and Honoré Daumier, Realists insisted that peasants, laborers, and everyday scenes deserved the same monumental treatment previously reserved for mythological heroes and historical events.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
The term 'Realism' was championed by critic Jules-Antoine Castagnary and by Courbet himself, who displayed his controversial canvases in a private Pavillon du Réalisme at the 1855 Paris Exposition Universelle after the official jury rejected them. Courbet's manifesto declared that he could only paint what he could see — a radical rejection of the idealized imagination central to academic training. His The Stone Breakers (1849–50, destroyed in WWII) confronted viewers with the drudgery of road labor without heroism or consolation; The Burial at Ornans (1849–50) granted a provincial funeral the scale of a history painting. Millet's peasant cycles — The Gleaners (1857), The Angelus (1857–59), The Sower (1850) — combined biblical resonance with unsentimental observation of agricultural labor. Daumier's lithographs for satirical newspapers like Le Charivari brought Realist social criticism to mass audiences, skewering bourgeois hypocrisy and legal injustice. In Britain, the Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood initially shared Realist impulses (careful observation, rejection of academic convention) while pursuing very different subjects.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Realist technique prized thick, confident paint application (Courbet's use of the palette knife to lay on impasto), dark tonalities anchored by bituminous blacks and earth colors, large canvases that refused to diminish their subjects, and compositions derived from direct observation rather than academic formulae. Courbet studied Old Masters (Rembrandt, Hals, Velázquez) for their painterly directness rather than their idealized forms. Photography, emerging simultaneously, both competed with and inspired Realist painting — reinforcing the value of observed fact while prompting painters to emphasize qualities that photography could not capture (color, texture, scale, social commentary). The Realist novel (Flaubert, Zola, Tolstoy) developed in parallel, sharing the program of social documentation without moral embellishment.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Realism's legacy is pervasive. It dismantled the hierarchy of genres that had privileged history painting over scenes of everyday life. Impressionism grew directly from Realism's commitment to observed experience, though it dissolved Realist solidity into light and atmosphere. Social Realism — the tradition of art committed to depicting working-class conditions and injustice — continued through the late 19th and early 20th centuries in Europe and was institutionalized in the Soviet Union as Socialist Realism. American Realism (the Ashcan School, Thomas Eakins) adapted the program to US urban and rural life. Photorealism in the 1960s–70s revisited Realism's confrontation with mimesis in the age of mechanical reproduction. Courbet himself became a political martyr: exiled after the Paris Commune (1871), he died in Switzerland, his property confiscated.

## Key Terminology
- **Realism (movement)**: Mid-19th-century artistic movement committed to depicting contemporary life without idealization.
- **Pavillon du Réalisme**: Independent exhibition pavilion erected by Courbet in 1855 to show rejected work — an early model of the artist-led counter-exhibition.
- **Impasto**: Thick application of paint that retains the marks of brush or palette knife; associated with Courbet's direct, material approach.
- **Plein air**: Painting outdoors in front of the subject; Realists and Barbizon painters advanced this practice before Impressionists adopted it.
- **Barbizon School**: French landscape painters working in the Forest of Fontainebleau who combined Realist observation with romantic feeling.
- **Social Realism**: Later tradition extending Realist principles to critique industrial poverty, labor conditions, and class inequality.
