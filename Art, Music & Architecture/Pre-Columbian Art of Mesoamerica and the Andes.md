# Pre-Columbian Art of Mesoamerica and the Andes

## Overview
Pre-Columbian art encompasses the diverse visual traditions of the Americas prior to European contact, reaching extraordinary sophistication in Mesoamerica (Olmec, Maya, Teotihuacan, Aztec) and the Andean highlands and coast (Chavín, Moche, Nazca, Inca). Integrating monumental stone carving, polychrome murals, featherwork, metallurgy, and textiles, these arts served sacred cosmology, dynastic legitimacy, and calendrical ritual.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
In Mesoamerica, the Olmec (c. 1200–400 BCE) carved colossal basalt heads weighing up to 50 tons and luminous jade celts without metal tools. Classic Maya artists (c. 250–900 CE) excelled in narrative limestone relief, polychrome Bonampak murals, and finely painted cylinder vases recording royal court life and hieroglyphic dates. At Teotihuacan, sweeping temple murals depicted feathered serpents and rain deities. Later, the Mexica (Aztec) produced monumental monolithic carvings such as the Coatlicue statue and the 24-ton Calendar Stone, alongside exquisite turquoise mosaics and iridescent quetzal featherwork (amanteca).

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
In the Andes, textiles and metallurgy often outweighed monumental stone sculpture in cultural prestige. The Paracas and Nazca cultures wove complex embroidered funerary mantles and etched colossal geoglyphs across desert plateaus. Moche ceramicists on Peru's north coast crafted hyper-realistic portrait stirrup-spout vessels and sophisticated gilded copper ornaments. The Inca Empire (c. 1438–1533) prioritized abstract geometric precision over figural naturalism — seen in their mortarless ashlar stonework at Cusco and Machu Picchu, standardized geometric tunics (uncu), and lost-wax gold and silver figurines.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Spanish conquest violently suppressed indigenous sacred imagery and melted down vast quantities of pre-Columbian goldwork. However, indigenous aesthetic sensibilities survived in colonial mestizo architecture, Mexican Muralism (Diego Rivera's incorporation of Aztec motifs), and Henry Moore's modernist stone carvings inspired by the Maya Chac Mool. Today, archaeologists and indigenous communities collaborate to preserve and reinterpret this vast artistic heritage.

## Key Terminology
- **Mesoamerica**: Cultural region stretching from central Mexico through Belize, Guatemala, El Salvador, and Honduras, sharing calendrical and ritual traditions.
- **Chac Mool**: Recumbent Mesoamerican stone figure holding a sacrificial vessel on its belly, influential on 20th-century modernist sculpture.
- **Stirrup-spout vessel**: Characteristic Andean ceramic form with a hollow arched handle feeding into a single vertical spout, mastered by the Moche.
- **Geoglyph**: Large-scale ground drawing created by removing dark desert stones to expose lighter soil, famously the Nazca Lines.
- **Amanteca**: Specialized Aztec artisans who crafted ceremonial shields and headdresses from iridescent tropical bird feathers.
- **Ashlar masonry**: Precision-cut interlocking stonework fitted without mortar, hallmark of Inca imperial architecture.
