# Post-Impressionism and Formal Theory

## Overview
Coined by British critic Roger Fry in 1910, Post-Impressionism encompasses the diverse late-19th-century responses to Impressionism. Rather than merely capturing fleeting optical light, artists like Paul Cézanne, Georges Seurat, Vincent van Gogh, and Paul Gauguin sought deeper structural permanence, scientific color order, expressive emotion, or symbolic meaning.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Impressionism had liberated color and brushwork from academic finish, yet by the 1880s younger and dissident painters felt it lacked structural rigor and conceptual depth. Georges Seurat and Paul Signac formulated Neo-Impressionism (Divisionism or Pointillism), applying small dots of pure unmixed pigment side by side so the viewer's eye would optically blend them, guided by Michel Eugène Chevreul's laws of simultaneous contrast (A Sunday Afternoon on the Island of La Grande Jatte, 1884–86). Paul Cézanne set out to 'make of Impressionism something solid and durable, like the art of the museums,' analyzing Mont Sainte-Victoire and still lifes into faceted geometric planes — cylinders, spheres, and cones — that tilted space toward the flat picture plane.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
In contrast to Seurat's scientific method and Cézanne's architectural geometry, Paul Gauguin and Vincent van Gogh pursued expressive and symbolic autonomy. Gauguin developed Synthetism and Cloisonnism in Brittany and later Tahiti (Where Do We Come From? What Are We? Where Are We Going?, 1897–98), flattening forms inside bold dark outlines and filling them with arbitrary, saturated color fields. Van Gogh intensified directional impasto brushwork and vibrating complementary contrasts (The Starry Night, 1889) to project psychological states. Critics Roger Fry and Clive Bell later distilled Cézanne's achievements into Formalist art theory, arguing that 'significant form' — the arrangement of lines, colors, and planes — mattered more than literary subject matter.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Post-Impressionism acted as the launchpad for every major early-20th-century avant-garde. Cézanne's fractured perspective directly catalyzed Picasso and Braque's Cubism. Gauguin and Van Gogh's liberated color ignited Fauvism and German Expressionism. Seurat's systematic dots anticipated digital pixel grids and Op Art, while Formalist criticism dominated modernist art theory well into the mid-20th century under Clement Greenberg.

## Key Terminology
- **Divisionism / Pointillism**: Neo-Impressionist technique placing small dots or strokes of pure color side by side for optical mixing in the viewer's eye.
- **Cloisonnism**: Style using bold dark contours around flat areas of bold color, inspired by stained glass and cloisonné enamel.
- **Synthetism**: Approach championed by Gauguin synthesizing observed nature, artist's feelings, and simplified decorative form.
- **Significant form**: Clive Bell's Formalist concept that aesthetic emotion arises purely from relationships of lines and colors, not subject matter.
- **Passage (Cézanne)**: Cézanne's technique of leaving edges open so colors and planes bleed into adjacent forms, unifying surface and depth.
- **Simultaneous contrast**: Optical phenomenon where adjacent complementary colors intensify each other's apparent brilliance.
