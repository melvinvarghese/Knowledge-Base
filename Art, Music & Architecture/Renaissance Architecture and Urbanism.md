# Renaissance Architecture and Urbanism

## Overview
Renaissance architecture (c. 1400–1600) recovered and reinterpreted classical Greco-Roman architectural precedents, applying mathematical proportion, perspective, and humanist philosophy to the design of churches, palaces, villas, and urban spaces. Filippo Brunelleschi, Leon Battista Alberti, Donato Bramante, and Andrea Palladio created the canonical buildings and treatises that governed Western architecture for three centuries.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Filippo Brunelleschi's Florence Cathedral dome (Santa Maria del Fiore, 1420–36) is the founding achievement of Renaissance architecture — an engineering feat of unprecedented scale (outer diameter 54.8 m, only slightly less than the Pantheon) achieved without a traditional centering (wooden support frame) using a double-shell herringbone brick construction and a system of internal ribs. It solved a problem that had defeated builders for over a century and transformed Florence's skyline permanently. Brunelleschi simultaneously re-invented single-point perspective (rediscovering a mathematical system for the foreshortening of space on a two-dimensional surface) in a series of panel demonstrations — a discovery that transformed painting, sculpture, and architectural drawing. His churches (Ospedale degli Innocenti, 1419–27; San Lorenzo, 1421–60; Santo Spirito, 1436–87) applied Roman architectural elements (coffered ceilings, Corinthian pilasters, round arches on columns) in precisely proportioned compositions that established the Renaissance church interior's cool, rational clarity. Leon Battista Alberti's treatise De Re Aedificatoria (On the Art of Building, c. 1452) — the first Renaissance architectural treatise — applied humanist scholarship to Vitruvius's ancient Roman treatise, providing the theoretical framework for classical design. His buildings (Tempio Malatestiano, Rimini, 1450; Sant'Andrea, Mantua, 1472) applied Roman triumphal arch and temple facade forms to church design.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Donato Bramante's move to Rome (1499) initiated the High Renaissance architectural style: his Tempietto (1502) in the courtyard of San Pietro in Montorio — a small circular temple built on the supposed site of St. Peter's martyrdom — achieved an authority and harmony of classical proportions that made it the ideal Renaissance building. His plan for the new St. Peter's Basilica (1505, later substantially modified by Michelangelo and others) was a Greek-cross scheme beneath a great central dome, an ambition realized (in altered form) by 1626. Michelangelo's architectural work — the Medici Chapel and Laurentian Library (Florence, 1520s–30s) with its Mannerist distortions of classical convention, and the completion of St. Peter's dome — expressed a dramatic, personal relationship with classical form rather than archaeologically correct application. Andrea Palladio's Four Books of Architecture (1570) codified a rationalized version of classical design and proportional systems derived from Vitruvius that became the most influential architectural treatise in Western history: Palladio's villas in the Veneto (Villa Rotonda, 1591; Villa Barbaro, 1554–60), with their temple-portico facades applied to domestic buildings, directly inspired English Palladianism, Thomas Jefferson's architecture, and the global classical tradition.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Renaissance architecture's legacy is the foundation of the entire Western architectural tradition since 1400. The classical orders, proportional systems, and humanist design philosophy codified in Alberti, Bramante, and Palladio's work remained the standard education and reference point for Western architects until the early 20th century. English Palladianism (Inigo Jones, Christopher Wren, Colin Campbell, Lord Burlington) brought Palladio's system to Britain; it crossed to America through Jefferson and the Federal style. Neoclassicism (18th century), Beaux-Arts (19th century), and even Postmodern classicism (late 20th century) all draw on the Renaissance's re-codification of classical principles. Brunelleschi's invention of perspective transformed not only architecture but all visual representation.

## Key Terminology
- **Single-point perspective**: Mathematical system for representing three-dimensional space on a two-dimensional surface, converging parallel lines at a single vanishing point; credited to Brunelleschi.
- **Double-shell dome**: Brunelleschi's engineering solution for the Florence Cathedral dome: two concentric shells connected by ribs, reducing material and load while enabling self-supporting construction.
- **Vitruvius**: Ancient Roman architect whose De Architectura (c. 25 BCE) was the principal source for Renaissance architectural theory.
- **Tempietto**: Bramante's small circular temple (1502) in Rome, considered the ideal Renaissance building for its perfect classical proportions.
- **Palladianism**: Architectural tradition derived from Palladio's treatise and buildings; dominant in England and America through the 17th–18th centuries.
- **Rustication**: Rough-textured masonry treatment of a building's lower courses, expressing solidity; common in Renaissance palazzo facades.
