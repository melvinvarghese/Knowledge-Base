# Urban Design and Public Space

## Overview
Urban design shapes the shared spaces between buildings — streets, plazas, transit corridors, and neighborhood blocks — determining how cities feel at eye level. Moving beyond mid-century car-centric highway planning, contemporary urbanists champion walkable human scale, mixed-use density, and inclusive public spaces that foster civic life.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Mid-20th-century urban planning, epitomized by Robert Moses's expressways in New York and modernist 'tower-in-the-park' housing blocks, severed neighborhoods with multi-lane roads and segregated residential, commercial, and industrial zones. In 1961, journalist Jane Jacobs published The Death and Life of Great American Cities, demonstrating that lively, safe neighborhoods depend on 'eyes on the street,' short walkable blocks, mixed-age buildings, and diverse ground-floor shops rather than sterile monumental plazas.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Building on Jacobs's empirical observation, Danish architect Jan Gehl transformed Copenhagen from a car-choked city into a global model of pedestrian streets and protected bicycle networks, proving that 'a good city is like a good party — people stay longer than necessary because they are enjoying themselves.' In Latin America, mayors like Enrique Peñalosa in Bogotá demonstrated how Bus Rapid Transit (BRT), Sunday car-free Ciclovía streets, and public libraries transform social equity. Meanwhile, Barcelona's 'Superblocks' (Superilles) reclaim interior neighborhood intersections from through-traffic for playgrounds, trees, and outdoor cafés.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Facing climate change and housing affordability crises, 21st-century urban design embraces the '15-minute city' ideal — ensuring daily needs lie within a short walk or bike ride — alongside transit-oriented development, tactical urbanism, and universal accessibility, making public space the primary arena of democratic urban life.

## Key Terminology
- **Eyes on the street**: Jane Jacobs's principle that active sidewalks, ground-floor windows, and mixed-use buildings naturally deter crime.
- **Mixed-use zoning**: Urban planning integrating housing, shops, offices, and parks within walking distance rather than isolating them.
- **Walkability**: Measure of how safe, pleasant, and convenient an urban environment is for pedestrian travel.
- **15-minute city**: Urban model ensuring residents can reach work, groceries, schools, and parks within a 15-minute walk or bike ride.
- **Transit-Oriented Development (TOD)**: Compact, walkable mixed-use neighborhoods clustered directly around high-capacity rail or bus stations.
- **Tactical urbanism**: Low-cost, temporary street interventions (painted bike lanes, pop-up plazas) testing permanent urban design changes.
