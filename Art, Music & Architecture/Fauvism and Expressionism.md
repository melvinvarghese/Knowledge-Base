# Fauvism and Expressionism

## Overview
Fauvism (c. 1905–08) and Expressionism (c. 1905–30s) were the first fully non-naturalistic movements in Western painting, using intensified, non-descriptive color and distorted form to express emotional states. Though distinct in temperament — Fauvism joyful and Mediterranean, Expressionism angst-ridden and Northern — both movements broke definitively with the Impressionist aim of recording visual sensation.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
The Fauves ('Wild Beasts,' a label applied mockingly by critic Louis Vauxcelles at the 1905 Salon d'Automne) grouped around Henri Matisse, André Derain, Maurice de Vlaminck, Raoul Dufy, and others. Their paintings applied unmixed paint in bold strokes, using complementary colors set against each other for maximum vibration rather than to describe objects. Matisse's Joy of Life (1905–06) and his Moroccan period paintings replaced spatial depth with flat, luminous color zones. Derain's London paintings (Charing Cross Bridge, 1906) turned the Thames an acid green. The Fauves drew on Van Gogh's expressive brushwork, Gauguin's non-naturalistic color, and Cézanne's structural reduction — but accelerated all three toward pure color as the primary vehicle of emotional meaning. Fauvism as a collective moment was brief; by 1908 most participants had moved toward Cubism or personal styles. Matisse, however, pursued his 'art of balance and purity' throughout a long career, culminating in the cut-paper works of his final decade.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
German Expressionism emerged from two overlapping groups: Die Brücke ('The Bridge,' Dresden, 1905, with Ernst Ludwig Kirchner, Erich Heckel, Karl Schmidt-Rottluff) and Der Blaue Reiter ('The Blue Rider,' Munich, 1911, with Wassily Kandinsky, Franz Marc, August Macke, Paul Klee). Die Brücke artists sought a 'bridge' to authentic emotional expression, drawing on African and Oceanic art, medieval German woodcuts, and Edvard Munch's psychological intensity. Their cityscapes, nudes, and street scenes depicted modern alienation with jagged lines and clashing colors. Der Blaue Reiter was more spiritually oriented, interested in the correspondence between color, music, and inner life — Kandinsky's Concerning the Spiritual in Art (1911) provided its theoretical foundation and helped prepare the ground for complete abstraction. Max Beckmann, Käthe Kollwitz, and Otto Dix brought Expressionist intensity to subjects of World War I, Weimar-era social crisis, and working-class suffering. Expressionism also shaped theater (Bertolt Brecht) and cinema (The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari, 1920; Nosferatu, 1922).

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Fauvism's legacy was absorbed into 20th-century color theory and design education — Matisse's decorative genius fed directly into everything from fashion to graphic design and architecture. German Expressionism's influence was more turbulent: the Nazi regime classified it as 'Degenerate Art' (entartete Kunst), destroying or selling much of it in 1937. Expressionism survived in exile and re-emerged powerfully after 1945 in Abstract Expressionism (New York), CoBrA, and Neo-Expressionism (1980s). Kirchner, diagnosed with PTSD from WWI, destroyed much of his own work and committed suicide in 1938. The philosophical legacy — that art's primary function is emotional and spiritual expression rather than representation — remains central to contemporary practice.

## Key Terminology
- **Fauvism**: French movement (c. 1905–08) using intense non-naturalistic color to express emotion, led by Matisse and Derain.
- **Die Brücke**: German Expressionist group (1905–13) in Dresden using raw color and distorted form to express modern alienation.
- **Der Blaue Reiter**: Munich Expressionist group (1911–14) interested in spiritual and musical analogies for color and abstraction.
- **Entartete Kunst**: Nazi classification of avant-garde art as 'degenerate' used to persecute and purge Expressionist and other modernist works.
- **Complementary colors**: Colors opposite each other on the color wheel (e.g., red–green, blue–orange) that intensify each other when juxtaposed.
- **Woodcut**: Relief printmaking technique; Die Brücke artists revived it for its raw, primal graphic qualities.
