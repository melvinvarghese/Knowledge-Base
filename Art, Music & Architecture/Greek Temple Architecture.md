# Greek Temple Architecture

## Overview
Greek temple architecture (c. 700–100 BCE) is the foundation of the Western architectural tradition. The three orders — Doric, Ionic, and Corinthian — defined a system of proportional relationships between column, capital, entablature, and overall structure that was absorbed by Rome, revived in the Renaissance, and institutionalized in Western civic and academic architecture from the 18th century to the present.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
The Greek temple functioned not as a congregational space for worshippers but as the house of a god — the cult statue resided inside the cella (inner chamber), while religious rituals, sacrifices, and processions took place outside. The peristyle (a colonnaded porch surrounding the cella on all four sides) was the defining external feature. Temple design followed the three orders: the Doric order (originating in mainland Greece and the western colonies, Sicily, and southern Italy) is the oldest and most massive, with fluted columns rising directly from the platform (stylobate) without a base, topped by a plain capital (abacus and echinus), supporting an entablature of alternating triglyphs and metopes. The Parthenon (447–432 BCE, architects Iktinos and Kallikrates, sculptor Pheidias) is the supreme example, its apparently simple geometry in fact corrected by subtle optical refinements (entasis — the slight swelling of columns; the slight inward lean of outer columns; the slight convexity of the stylobate) designed to counteract the visual distortions of perfect geometric regularity. The Ionic order (originating in Ionia, western Anatolia) is more slender and ornate: columns have a base and are topped by a capital with distinctive volutes (spiral scrolls); the entablature may carry a continuous sculptural frieze rather than triglyphs. The Erechtheion on the Acropolis (421–406 BCE) with its Porch of the Caryatids (columns in the form of female figures) is the most celebrated Ionic building. The Corinthian order, developed in the 4th century BCE, substituted an elaborate capital of acanthus leaf volutes for the Ionic volutes; it became the preferred order for Roman monumental buildings.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Greek temple builders achieved extraordinary technical precision using simple tools and manual labor: marble blocks were quarried on-site (the Parthenon used Pentelic marble from Mount Pentelicus, 16 km away), transported by wooden sleds and oxen, and lifted into place with rope-and-pulley systems without mortar — the blocks were pinned with iron clamps set in lead. The sculptural programs were as important as the architecture: the Parthenon's frieze (now largely in the British Museum as the Elgin Marbles, controversially retained since 1816), east and west pediments (depicting the birth of Athena and the contest between Athena and Poseidon), and 92 metopes (depicting gigantomachy, Trojan War, Amazonomachy, and Centauromachy) formed a comprehensive mythological program celebrating Athenian civilization. Greek sanctuaries (Delphi, Olympia, Epidaurus, Delos) were complexes of multiple temples, treasuries, stoas (colonnaded halls), and altars whose spatial relationships were as designed as the individual buildings.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Greek architecture's influence on Western building is total. Rome absorbed and transformed the orders, applying them to concrete structures of unprecedented scale (Pantheon, 118–128 CE). Renaissance architects (Brunelleschi, Alberti, Palladio) studied Roman buildings that preserved Greek precedents and codified the orders in treatises that governed European architecture for centuries. Neoclassicism directly revived Greek forms for public buildings in Britain, America, and Germany. The Greek Revival (1800–1850) produced some of the most celebrated public buildings in the Western world: the British Museum (Smirke, 1823–47), the Altes Museum (Schinkel, 1823–30), the Lincoln Memorial (1922), and the Supreme Court of the United States (1935) all draw explicitly on Greek temple precedents. Contemporary architects continue to reference the orders in civic and institutional buildings as a visual claim to permanence and cultural authority.

## Key Terminology
- **Doric order**: Oldest and most austere of the three Greek architectural orders: heavy columns with no base, plain capitals, and triglyph-metope entablature.
- **Ionic order**: Greek order originating in Ionia, with slender columns on a base and capitals featuring distinctive spiral volutes.
- **Corinthian order**: Most ornate Greek order, with capitals of acanthus-leaf volutes; preferred by Rome and the dominant order in Western civic architecture since the Renaissance.
- **Entasis**: Subtle convex swelling of a column shaft, correcting the optical illusion of concavity that would result from perfectly straight sides.
- **Cella**: The inner chamber of a Greek temple housing the cult statue; not a congregational space.
- **Peristyle**: Colonnaded porch surrounding a temple on all sides; the defining external feature of the canonical Greek temple type.
