# Romanticism in Art

## Overview
Romanticism in visual art (c. 1780–1850) prioritized emotion, individual imagination, the untamed power of nature, and the 'Sublime' over Neoclassical order and Enlightenment rationalism. Spanning Britain, France, Germany, and Spain, Romantic painters depicted shipwrecks, tempests, revolutions, and solitary wanderers confronting vast landscapes.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Romantic art arose alongside political upheaval — the French Revolution, Napoleonic Wars, and Industrial Revolution — as artists questioned the sufficiency of reason. In Germany, Caspar David Friedrich transformed landscape into spiritual allegory: Wanderer above the Sea of Fog (c. 1818) and The Monk by the Sea (1808–10) placed diminutive human figures before infinite expanses of mist, ocean, and sky. In Britain, J.M.W. Turner dissolved solid form into turbulent light, steam, and atmospheric color (Rain, Steam and Speed, 1844; The Slave Ship, 1840), while John Constable celebrated the English countryside with empirical cloud studies and luminous brushwork (The Hay Wain, 1821). In France, Théodore Géricault's The Raft of the Medusa (1818–19) turned a contemporary maritime disaster into a monumental indictment of government incompetence, and Eugène Delacroix championed painterly color and dramatic movement over Ingres's linear discipline in Liberty Leading the People (1830) and The Death of Sardanapalus (1827).

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Philosophically, Romanticism leaned heavily on Edmund Burke's distinction between the Beautiful (small, smooth, harmonious) and the Sublime (vast, terrifying, awe-inspiring). Romantic artists sought the Sublime in Alpine peaks, stormy seas, medieval ruins, and psychological extremes. William Blake forged an idiosyncratic visionary art combining hand-colored relief etching with prophetic poetry. Francisco Goya bridged Enlightenment satire and dark Romanticism in his Los Caprichos etchings, The Third of May 1808 (1814), and the haunting Black Paintings. Orientalism also emerged within French Romanticism, as Delacroix and Jean-Léon Gérôme painted North African and Near Eastern subjects blending ethnographic curiosity with European colonial fantasy.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Romanticism permanently shifted Western aesthetics by establishing originality, subjective feeling, and creative genius as the highest artistic values. Turner's late atmospheric abstractions anticipated Impressionism and 20th-century Color Field painting. Delacroix's color theory — observing that shadows contain complementary hues rather than pure black — directly informed the Impressionists and Neo-Impressionists. In the United States, the Hudson River School (Thomas Cole, Frederic Edwin Church, Albert Bierstadt) adapted Romantic landscape conventions to celebrate the American wilderness and Manifest Destiny, shaping national conservation consciousness.

## Key Terminology
- **The Sublime**: Aesthetic quality of vastness, terror, and awe exceeding human comprehension, theorized by Edmund Burke and central to Romantic landscape.
- **Painterly (malerisch)**: Style emphasizing visible brushwork, color masses, and atmospheric blending over sharp linear contours.
- **Hudson River School**: Mid-19th-century American landscape movement applying Romantic Sublime aesthetics to the North American wilderness.
- **Orientalism (art)**: 19th-century European artistic depictions of Middle Eastern and North African cultures, blending observation with exoticized fantasy.
- **Poussinistes vs. Rubénistes**: Academic debate between champions of drawing/line (Ingres) and color/movement (Delacroix) revived during Romanticism.
- **Rückenfigur**: Compositional device showing a figure from behind gazing at a landscape, inviting the viewer into their contemplation (e.g., Friedrich).
