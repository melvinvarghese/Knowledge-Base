# Karst Topography - Caves, Sinkholes, and Carbonate Dissolution

## Overview
Karst is a distinctive geomorphic landscape formed by the chemical dissolution of soluble carbonate bedrock, primarily limestone, dolomite, and gypsum.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Chemical Dissolution Kinetics: Rainwater absorbs atmospheric and soil $CO_2$, forming mild carbonic acid: $H_2O + CO_2 \rightleftharpoons H_2CO_3$. Percolating through bedrock joints, the acidic water dissolves calcium carbonate: $CaCO_3 + H_2CO_3 \rightleftharpoons Ca^{2+} + 2 HCO_3^-$, progressively enlarging fractures into cavernous subterranean conduit systems.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Surface Landforms & Speleothems: Surface dissolution creates sinkholes (dolines), disappearing streams, and tower karst (e.g., Guilin, China). Subterranean caverns precipitate secondary mineral formations (speleothems): stalactites hanging from ceilings, stalagmites rising from floors, and flowstones.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Karst groundwater aquifers providing drinking water to 25% of the global human population, speleothem isotopic paleoclimatology.

## Key Terminology
- **Karst**: Landscape underlain by limestone or other soluble rock, characterized by sinkholes, caves, and underground drainage.
- **Speleothem**: Secondary mineral deposit formed in a cave, including stalactites, stalagmites, and flowstones.
- **Doline (Sinkhole)**: Depression or hole in the ground caused by the collapse or dissolution of the surface carbonate rock layer.
