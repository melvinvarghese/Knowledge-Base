# The Paleocene-Eocene Thermal Maximum (PETM) - Hyperthermal Carbon Spike

## Overview
The Paleocene-Eocene Thermal Maximum (PETM, c. 56 Ma) was a rapid global hyperthermal warming event that serves as the premier ancient analog for modern anthropogenic carbon emissions.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Massive Carbon Injection & Carbon Isotope Excursion (CIE): Within roughly 5,000 years, an estimated 3,000 to 7,000 gigatons of carbon were released into the atmosphere-ocean system. Sediment cores exhibit a sharp negative **Carbon Isotope Excursion ($\delta^{13}C$)**, pointing to the catastrophic dissociation of submarine seafloor methane clathrates or volcanic intrusion into North Atlantic petroleum basins.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Global Ecological Consequences: Global mean temperatures surged by $5\text{–}8^\circ\text{C}$; polar regions became subtropical (palm trees and crocodiles in the Arctic); the deep oceans suffered extreme acidification, wiping out 30–50% of deep-sea benthic foraminifera. Mammals underwent transient evolutionary dwarfing alongside rapid intercontinental dispersal.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Critical deep-time climate sensitivity analog, geochemical modeling of oceanic carbon neutralization timescales (taking 100,000 years to recover).

## Key Terminology
- **PETM**: Paleocene-Eocene Thermal Maximum; rapid global warming event 56 million years ago caused by a massive greenhouse gas release.
- **Carbon Isotope Excursion (CIE)**: Pronounced shift in the ratio of carbon isotopes ($^{13}C/^{12}C$) indicating the injection of isotopically light carbon.
- **Methane Clathrate**: Solid ice-like cage structure containing trapped methane molecules within deep-sea marine sediments.
