# James Webb Space Telescope Mid-Infrared Instrument (MIRI)

## Overview
The Mid-Infrared Instrument (MIRI) aboard the James Webb Space Telescope operates between 4.9 and 28.8 micrometers, penetrating dense cosmic dust to observe the earliest stars, exoplanet atmospheres, and protoplanetary disks.

---

## 1. Theoretical Foundations & Governing Principles
Cryocooler Thermal Physics: While JWST's sunshield passively cools the spacecraft to ~40 K, MIRI requires an active closed-cycle helium pulse-tube cryocooler to reach its operating temperature of **6.7 Kelvin (-266.5°C)**, preventing instrument self-emission thermal noise.

---

## 2. Structural Analysis & Mathematical Modeling
Silicon-Arsenic Impurity Band Detectors: Utilizes arsenic-doped silicon (Si:As) IBC detector arrays, paired with coronagraphic masks to block direct stellar glare and reveal faint thermal glow from orbiting exoplanets.

---

## 3. Real-World Applications & Scientific Impact
Detecting polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) across cosmic time, imaging protostellar accretion jets, and measuring exoplanet phase curves.

---

## Key Terms
- **MIRI**: Mid-Infrared Instrument on the James Webb Space Telescope observing 5–28 micrometer wavelengths.
- **Cryocooler**: Active mechanical refrigeration unit cooling detectors close to absolute zero.
- **Coronagraph**: Telescope attachment designed to block the direct light from a star to reveal faint surrounding objects.
