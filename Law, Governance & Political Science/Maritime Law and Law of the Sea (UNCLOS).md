# Maritime Law and Law of the Sea (UNCLOS)

## Overview
Covering over 70% of Earth's surface, the Law of the Sea balances freedom of global maritime navigation with coastal nations' sovereignty over offshore fisheries, oil, and seabed minerals. Codified in the 1982 UN Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS), it serves as the 'constitution for the oceans.'

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
For centuries, Hugo Grotius's *Mare Liberum* ('freedom of the seas,' 1609) limited coastal control to a narrow three-mile cannon-shot belt. Post-WWII offshore oil drilling and distant-water fishing fleets triggered fierce maritime claims, resolved by UNCLOS (entered into force 1994) into concentric legal zones: a 12-nautical-mile Territorial Sea (full sovereignty subject to 'innocent passage'), a 24-mile Contiguous Zone, and a 200-nautical-mile Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ) granting coastal states sovereign rights over fish, oil, and wind energy while preserving foreign navigation and overflight.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Beyond national EEZs lie the High Seas ('the common heritage of mankind') and the deep seabed overseen by the International Seabed Authority. Meanwhile, private Admiralty law governs shipping collisions, salvage, marine insurance, and flags of convenience.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
UNCLOS arbitration faces intense modern tests in the South China Sea, Arctic shipping lanes opening from climate melt, and deep-sea mining disputes.

## Key Terminology
- **UNCLOS (1982)**: Comprehensive UN Convention on the Law of the Sea establishing maritime zones and dispute tribunals.
- **Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ)**: 200-nautical-mile offshore zone granting coastal states resource rights while preserving free navigation.
- **Innocent passage**: Right of foreign vessels to transit peacefully through another state's territorial sea.
- **High Seas**: International waters beyond national jurisdiction open to all states for navigation, fishing, and cable-laying.
- **Flag of convenience**: Registering merchant ships in foreign states with minimal taxes or labor/safety oversight.
- **Archipelagic waters**: Sovereign maritime waters enclosed by straight baselines connecting outermost islands of nations like Indonesia.
