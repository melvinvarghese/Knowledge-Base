# World Trade Organization and Trade Law

## Overview
Created in 1995 out of the 1947 GATT tariff agreements, the World Trade Organization (WTO) lowered global trade barriers to historic lows and established binding appellate dispute resolution — before facing existential paralysis amid US-China strategic rivalry and industrial-policy revival.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Global trade law rests on two non-discrimination pillars: Most-Favored-Nation (MFN — any tariff cut granted to one WTO member must extend automatically to all 160+ members) and National Treatment (once imported goods clear customs, domestic taxes cannot discriminate against them). Its crowning achievement was a binding Dispute Settlement Body capable of authorizing retaliatory tariffs against rule-breakers.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Yet consensus rules stalled the Doha Development Round, while China's state-subsidized industrial rise, digital trade gaps, and national-security exceptions shattered postwar free-trade consensus. Since 2019, US blocks on Appellate Body judicial appointments crippled top-level appeals, accelerating bilateral trade wars, 'friend-shoring' supply chains, and green subsidy disputes.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Reforming WTO subsidy rules while reconciling climate border adjustments (EU CBAM) with developing-nation growth defines modern trade governance.

## Key Terminology
- **Most-Favored-Nation (MFN)**: WTO rule requiring equal tariff treatment across all trading partners (except authorized FTAs).
- **National Treatment**: Obligation treating imported goods no worse than equivalent domestic products inside internal markets.
- **GATT (1947)**: General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade, the multilateral tariff-cutting foundation preceding the WTO.
- **Appellate Body**: WTO's highest dispute-resolution panel, paralyzed since 2019 over judicial appointment blocks.
- **Friend-shoring**: Redirecting critical semiconductor and clean-tech supply chains toward geopolitical allies.
- **Carbon Border Adjustment**: Tariff equalizing carbon costs on imported steel or cement from countries lacking domestic climate pricing.
