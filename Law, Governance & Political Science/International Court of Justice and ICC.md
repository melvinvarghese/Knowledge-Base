# International Court of Justice and ICC

## Overview
International justice rests on two distinct Hague courts often confused in headlines: the International Court of Justice (ICJ), settling legal disputes between sovereign states, and the International Criminal Court (ICC), prosecuting individual leaders for genocide, war crimes, and crimes against humanity.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Established in 1945 as the UN's principal judicial organ, the ICJ adjudicates state-to-state border, maritime, and treaty disputes (plus advisory opinions) — yet can hear contentious cases *only* when both states consent via treaty clauses or optional declarations. Conversely, following ad hoc 1990s Nuremberg-inspired tribunals for Yugoslavia (ICTY) and Rwanda (ICTR), the 1998 Rome Statute created the permanent ICC (2002) to prosecute *individuals* regardless of official head-of-state immunity.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Operating under strict 'complementarity,' the ICC steps in *only* when national courts are unwilling or genuinely unable to prosecute core atrocities. However, lacking its own police force and unratified by major powers (US, China, India, Russia), the ICC faces fierce legitimacy criticism over selective enforcement and reliance on state arrests.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Alongside universal jurisdiction trials in domestic European courts, the ICJ and ICC steadily narrow impunity for mass atrocities.

## Key Terminology
- **ICJ (World Court)**: UN's principal court in The Hague resolving legal disputes exclusively between sovereign states.
- **ICC (International Criminal Court)**: Permanent treaty-based tribunal prosecuting individuals for genocide, war crimes, and crimes against humanity.
- **Complementarity principle**: Rome Statute rule restricting ICC intervention to cases national courts refuse or fail to prosecute genuinely.
- **Universal jurisdiction**: Doctrine permitting any national court to prosecute severe atrocity crimes regardless of where or by whom committed.
- **Crimes against humanity**: Widespread or systematic attacks directed against civilian populations (murder, torture, deportation).
- **Genocide Convention (1948)**: Treaty criminalizing acts committed with intent to destroy a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group.
