# Civil Disobedience and Nonviolent Resistance

## Overview
Civil disobedience is the public, nonviolent, conscientious breach of law undertaken to protest unjust statutes or policies while accepting legal consequences. Practiced by Henry David Thoreau, Mahatma Gandhi, and Martin Luther King Jr., empirical research proves nonviolent campaigns succeed far more often than armed insurrections.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Coined after Thoreau's 1849 essay refusing poll taxes supporting slavery and the Mexican-American War, civil disobedience was scaled into mass political strategy by Gandhi's *Satyagraha* ('truth-force') and Martin Luther King Jr.'s US Civil Rights Movement. In his *Letter from Birmingham Jail* (1963), King argued citizens hold a moral duty to disobey unjust laws openly, lovingly, and with willingness to accept arrest to awaken public conscience — distinguishing peaceful protest from covert self-interested lawbreaking.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
John Rawls defined civil disobedience as a stabilizing constitutional 'safety valve' appealing to a society's shared sense of justice when normal electoral channels fail. Strikingly, Erica Chenoweth and Maria Stephan's dataset of 323 resistance campaigns (1900–2006) proved nonviolent campaigns succeeded 53% of the time versus 26% for violent insurgencies — because nonviolence lowers participation barriers, wins over security forces, and sustains broad coalitions.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Modern climate activists, anti-apartheid movements, and digital-era pro-democracy protests adapt nonviolent tactics across streets, boycotts, and courts.

## Key Terminology
- **Civil disobedience**: Public, nonviolent, conscientious violation of law aimed at reforming unjust policies while accepting penalty.
- **Satyagraha**: Gandhi's philosophy and practice of nonviolent 'truth-force' converting opponents without hatred.
- **Letter from Birmingham Jail**: MLK's classic 1963 defense of nonviolent direct action against unjust segregation laws.
- **Nonviolent discipline**: Maintaining peaceful conduct even under state provocation to maximize public sympathy and defections.
- **Conscientious objection**: Refusal on moral or religious grounds to obey specific mandates such as military conscription.
- **3.5% rule (Chenoweth)**: Empirical observation that nonviolent movements mobilizing ~3.5% of a population rarely fail.
