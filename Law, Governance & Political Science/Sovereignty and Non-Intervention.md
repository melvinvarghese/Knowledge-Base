# Sovereignty and Non-Intervention

## Overview
Sovereignty — supreme legal authority within fixed territorial borders paired with freedom from external interference — is the organizing bedrock of modern international politics. Yet from the Myth of 1648 Westphalia to modern humanitarian and cyber challenges, sovereignty has always been negotiated rather than absolute.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Conventionally traced to the 1648 Peace of Westphalia ending Europe's Thirty Years' War, Stephen Krasner dissects sovereignty into four distinct dimensions often unbundled in practice: domestic sovereignty (internal control), interdependence sovereignty (policing cross-border flows), international legal sovereignty (diplomatic recognition), and Westphalian sovereignty (excluding external meddling in domestic politics).

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Article 2(1) and 2(7) of the UN Charter enshrine sovereign equality and non-intervention, shielding weaker postcolonial states from great-power imperialism. Yet globalization, transnational carbon emissions, cross-border cyber intrusions, and horrific mass atrocities steadily eroded claims that tyrants enjoy unconditional license behind borders — reframing sovereignty in Kofi Annan's words as 'sovereignty as responsibility.'

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Contested recognitions (Taiwan, Kosovo, Somaliland) show diplomatic sovereignty remains a deeply political gatekeeping tool.

## Key Terminology
- **Westphalian sovereignty**: Norm excluding external actors from interfering in a state's domestic authority structures.
- **Territorial integrity**: International legal prohibition against seizing or altering another state's borders by force.
- **Krasner's four sovereignties**: Distinction among domestic control, border policing, legal recognition, and non-intervention.
- **De jure vs. De facto state**: Formal international diplomatic recognition versus actual empirical control over territory and population.
- **Sovereignty as responsibility**: Francis Deng's doctrine conditioning non-intervention immunity on a state's duty to protect its own citizens.
- **Uti possidetis juris**: Legal principle preserving colonial administrative boundaries as new post-independence international borders.
