# Liberalism and Global Governance

## Overview
Liberal International Relations theory challenges realist pessimism by showing how international institutions, economic interdependence, and shared democratic norms overcome anarchy to build durable, rules-based cooperation.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Rooted in Immanuel Kant's *Perpetual Peace* and Woodrow Wilson's post-WWI vision, modern IR Liberalism rests on three pillars. First, 'neoliberal institutionalism' (Robert Keohane, Robert Axelrod) proves repeated games and institutions (WTO, IMF, ICAO) solve Prisoner's Dilemma cheating by lowering transaction costs, sharing verification data, and lengthening the 'shadow of the future.' Second, 'commercial liberalism' argues deep trade and supply-chain interdependence raise the economic cost of war.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Third, the 'Democratic Peace Theory' (Michael Doyle, Bruce Russett) documents one of political science's strongest empirical regularities: consolidated democracies almost never fight wars against one another — anchored by shared peaceful conflict norms, transparent voter checks, and mutual trust (though democracies still fight non-democracies frequently).

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Contemporary crises — great-power decoupling, WTO gridlock, and democratic backsliding — test whether liberal institutions can adapt to a multipolar world.

## Key Terminology
- **Neoliberal institutionalism**: Keohane's theory showing international organizations sustain cooperation under anarchy by reducing cheating and transaction costs.
- **Democratic Peace Theory**: Robust empirical finding that mature democracies virtually never go to war against one another.
- **Complex interdependence**: Keohane and Nye's model of dense cross-border economic, NGO, and bureaucratic ties reducing military force utility.
- **Shadow of the future**: Expectation of ongoing repeated interaction incentivizing states to honor agreements rather than cheat.
- **Rules-based international order**: Post-1945 network of multilateral treaties, trade norms, and institutions constraining unilateral force.
- **Absolute gains**: Liberal focus on mutual total wealth or security increases rather than zero-sum relative advantage.
