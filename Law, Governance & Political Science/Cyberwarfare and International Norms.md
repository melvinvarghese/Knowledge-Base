# Cyberwarfare and International Norms

## Overview
As malware like Stuxnet damaged physical Iranian centrifuges and NotPetya caused billions in global shipping losses, international lawyers and strategists rushed to adapt analog laws of war to borderless cyberspace — codified in the Tallinn Manual alongside fragile UN norm talks.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Applying the UN Charter and Geneva Conventions to code poses sharp doctrinal puzzles: when does a cyber operation cross from routine espionage (legal under international law) into a prohibited 'use of force' or 'armed attack' triggering self-defense? The *Tallinn Manual* — drafted by NATO-affiliated legal scholars — concludes cyberattacks causing physical destruction, injury, or severe critical-infrastructure paralysis equivalent to kinetic bombs violate international humanitarian law, requiring distinction between military targets and civilian hospitals.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Unlike nuclear deterrence, 'cyber deterrence' struggles with delayed forensic attribution, low entry costs for non-state hackers, and the 'use-it-or-lose-it' fragility of zero-day exploits (which vanish once patched). Consequently, states shift toward 'defend forward' persistent engagement and collective public attribution naming culprits.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Global UN Group of Governmental Experts (GGE) agreements affirm norms against peacetime attacks on hospitals, elections, and CERT emergency teams, though enforcement remains contested.

## Key Terminology
- **Tallinn Manual**: Authoritative scholarly restatement applying existing international laws of war to cyber operations.
- **Cyber use of force**: Threshold where digital sabotage causes physical harm or critical-infrastructure destruction akin to kinetic force.
- **Zero-day exploit**: Previously unknown software vulnerability usable for intelligence or sabotage until vendor patching.
- **Persistent engagement**: US cyber doctrine emphasizing continuous proactive disruption of adversary networks short of war.
- **Espionage vs. Sabotage**: Passive intelligence theft (largely tolerated internationally) versus destructive disruption of systems.
- **Civilian-military dual use**: Blurring of civilian cloud servers and satellites used by military operations, complicating targeting law.
