# Social Movements and Collective Action

## Overview
How do ordinary people without armies or vast wealth force historic shifts in suffrage, civil rights, labor, and climate policy? Social movement scholarship explains how grievances ignite into sustained collective action when political opportunities open and mobilizing networks align.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Overcoming Mancur Olson's 'free-rider problem' — why individuals stay home when they still enjoy movement wins — requires solidarity networks (churches, campuses, unions), shared injustice framing, and evolving 'repertoires of contention' (Charles Tilly: petitions, strikes, marches, boycotts, sit-ins). Sidney Tarrow's 'Political Process Theory' shows movements surge not merely when misery peaks, but when 'political opportunity structures' widen via elite splits or shifting allies.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Social movements also transform culture even when initial bills fail: changing acceptable public language, shifting generational norms, and forcing mainstream parties to co-opt movement demands. Digital networks accelerate rapid 'connective action' (e.g., Arab Spring, #MeToo), yet Zeynep Tufekci warns leaderless viral protests often lack durable organizational discipline for slow post-protest legislative negotiation.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Durable social change typically pairs disruptive street mobilization with patient legal advocacy and electoral coalition building.

## Key Terminology
- **Free-rider problem**: Collective-action dilemma where rational individuals benefit from public goods without paying protest costs.
- **Political opportunity structure**: External shifts (elite fractures, regime crises, sympathetic courts) opening openings for mobilization.
- **Repertoire of contention**: Historically learned toolkit of protest tactics available to citizens in a given era.
- **Resource mobilization**: Theory emphasizing organizational capacity, money, media, and leadership over raw grievance alone.
- **Frame alignment**: Linking movement diagnostic/prognostic slogans to citizens' everyday moral values.
- **Connective action**: Digitally networked protest mobilization coordinated via hashtags rather than formal membership hierarchies.
