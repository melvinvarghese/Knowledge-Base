# Healthcare Policy and Universal Coverage

## Overview
Every wealthy nation except the United States guarantees universal health coverage, yet they do so through radically different institutional models — from tax-funded public hospitals to regulated non-profit insurers — balancing universal access, cost control, and clinical wait times.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Comparative health policy maps four major archetypes: (1) the Beveridge model (UK NHS, Spain, Nordics), where government owns hospitals and funds care directly through general taxes; (2) the Bismarck model (Germany, Japan, Netherlands, Switzerland), using mandatory multi-payer non-profit sickness funds financed by employer-employee payroll contributions with strict price caps; (3) National Health Insurance (Canada, Taiwan, South Korea), pairing a single public government insurer with mostly private doctors and hospitals; and (4) the US hybrid market mixing employer tax-subsidized private plans, Medicare (elderly), Medicaid (low-income), and uninsured gaps.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Healthcare markets suffer classic market failures: 'adverse selection' (only sick people buying voluntary insurance unless mandated), 'information asymmetry' (patients unable to price-shop emergency surgery), and 'moral hazard.' Consequently, nations with centralized drug-price negotiation and unified billing spend roughly half as much per capita as the US (~9–11% vs. ~17%+ of GDP) while achieving longer life expectancy.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Aging societies now balance universal coverage sustainability against expensive biologics, preventive primary care, and digital telemedicine.

## Key Terminology
- **Beveridge model**: Tax-financed national health service where government directly funds and often operates clinics (e.g., UK NHS).
- **Bismarck model**: Payroll-funded universal coverage delivered through regulated competing non-profit sickness funds (e.g., Germany).
- **Single-payer (NHI)**: Unified public insurance fund paying private and public providers under negotiated fee schedules (e.g., Canada, Taiwan).
- **Adverse selection**: Insurance market failure where high-risk individuals disproportionately enroll, driving premiums upward.
- **Out-of-pocket catastrophic risk**: Financial ruin from medical bills avoided by universal pooling systems.
- **Health Technology Assessment**: Cost-effectiveness evaluation (e.g., UK NICE) guiding public drug coverage decisions.
