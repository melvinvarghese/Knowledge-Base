# Jurisprudence and Legal Philosophy

## Overview
Jurisprudence investigates the nature, sources, and moral authority of law. Spanning classical Natural Law, H.L.A. Hart's Legal Positivism, American Legal Realism, and Ronald Dworkin's interpretivism, legal philosophy asks what distinguishes a valid law from mere coercive command.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Natural Law theory — rooted in Aristotle, Cicero, and Thomas Aquinas — holds that human laws derive legitimacy from universal moral principles discoverable by reason; an unjust statute fails as true law (*lex iniusta non est lex*). Conversely, Legal Positivism (Jeremy Bentham, John Austin, and refined by H.L.A. Hart in *The Concept of Law*, 1961) insists on the 'separability thesis': law's existence depends on social facts and a community's 'rule of recognition,' not its moral merit — allowing citizens to recognize wicked laws while still condemning them morally.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
In the early 20th century, Legal Realists (Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr., Karl Llewellyn) challenged formalist judging, arguing judges respond to facts, policy, and intuition before rationalizing via doctrine. Ronald Dworkin countered Hart by arguing judges in hard cases rely on underlying moral 'principles' (like 'no one may profit from their own wrong') woven into legal integrity.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Modern Critical Legal Studies, Feminist Jurisprudence, and Law and Economics extend these debates into courtroom interpretation, statutory construction, and algorithmic legal prediction.

## Key Terminology
- **Natural Law**: Legal philosophy grounding law's validity in universal moral reason and justice.
- **Legal Positivism**: Theory holding law's validity rests on social pedigree and institutional rules rather than moral worth.
- **Rule of Recognition**: H.L.A. Hart's foundational social rule identifying which norms count as binding law in a society.
- **Legal Realism**: View that judicial outcomes reflect pragmatic policy, context, and human psychology beyond abstract rules.
- **Law as Integrity**: Ronald Dworkin's interpretive theory requiring judges to read law in its best coherent moral light.
- **Separability thesis**: Positivist claim that legal validity and moral merit are conceptually distinct.
