# Property Law and Ownership Rights

## Overview
Property law governs legal relationships among people concerning scarce resources — land, buildings, personal goods, and financial assets. Conceptualized not as a simple 'thing' but as a 'bundle of rights' (to exclude, use, transfer, and lease), secure property rights anchor investment, credit, and personal autonomy.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Legal realists popularized the 'bundle of sticks' metaphor: ownership comprises separable rights that can be unbundled across time and people — life estates, leases, mortgages, co-ownership, trusts, and easements (rights to cross or run utilities across a neighbor's land). Land systems balance clear public title registries (Torrens or deed recording) against doctrines like 'adverse possession,' rewarding open, continuous productive use over neglected absentee title.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Property is never absolute: nuisance law curbs harmful spillovers, municipal zoning regulates land use, and constitutional 'eminent domain' (takings power) allows states to acquire private land for public roads or parks only upon paying 'just compensation.' Hernando de Soto highlighted how formalizing clear property titles unlocks trillions in 'dead capital' for collateralized credit in developing economies.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Contemporary property law grapples with shared commons governance (Elinor Ostrom), Indigenous land title recognition, and digital ownership of virtual assets.

## Key Terminology
- **Bundle of rights**: Legal conception of property as separable entitlements to possess, use, exclude, and transfer.
- **Easement**: Non-possessory right to use another person's land for a specific limited purpose (e.g., driveway access).
- **Eminent domain**: Sovereign state power to expropriate private property for public use with just compensation.
- **Adverse possession**: Doctrine transferring legal title to an open, hostile, continuous occupant after a statutory period.
- **Fiduciary trust**: Legal arrangement splitting legal title (held by trustee) from beneficial enjoyment (held by beneficiary).
- **Tragedy of the Commons**: Overuse dilemma in open-access resources resolved via private title, regulation, or Ostrom's community norms.
