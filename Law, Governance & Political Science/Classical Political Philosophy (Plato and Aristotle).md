# Classical Political Philosophy (Plato and Aristotle)

## Overview
Western political philosophy began in 5th–4th century BCE Athens as Plato and Aristotle asked what constitutes a just city (*polis*), who should rule, and how political institutions cultivate human flourishing (*eudaimonia*).

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Disillusioned by Athenian democracy's execution of Socrates, Plato's *Republic* envisioned an ideal hierarchical state ruled by philosopher-kings — guardians trained in mathematics and dialectic who discern the Form of the Good, freed from private property or family temptations to prevent corruption. Yet Plato's *Laws* later conceded that rule of law is safer than unchecked philosopher-rulers.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Aristotle's *Politics* countered Plato's utopian unity with empirical realism, studying 158 Greek constitutions. Declaring humans 'by nature political animals' (*zōon politikon*) fulfilled only inside civic speech and shared governance, Aristotle classified six regime types by who rules (one, few, many) and whether they rule for common good or self-interest: healthy Monarchy, Aristocracy, and *Polity* versus corrupt Tyranny, Oligarchy, and mob-rule Democracy. He championed a mixed 'Polity' anchored by a broad middle class.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Plato and Aristotle's debate between elite expert rule (*epistocracy*) and broad mixed constitutionalism echoes directly in modern democratic theory and technocracy.

## Key Terminology
- **Zōon politikon**: Aristotle's phrase ('political animal') asserting human flourishing requires active civic community.
- **Philosopher-king**: Plato's ideal ruler combining political power with philosophical wisdom in *The Republic*.
- **Mixed constitution (Polity)**: Aristotle's favored balanced regime blending oligarchic expertise with democratic broad participation.
- **Eudaimonia**: Human flourishing and virtuous living viewed by Aristotle as the ultimate aim of political community.
- **Epistocracy**: Political system restricting or weighting voting power based on formal political knowledge.
- **Cycle of regimes**: Classical idea (Plato, Polybius) that pure regimes inevitably decay and cycle into opposing forms.
