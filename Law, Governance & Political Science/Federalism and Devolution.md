# Federalism and Devolution

## Overview
Federalism divides constitutional sovereignty vertically between a central national government and regional states, provinces, or cantons. Contrasted with top-down unitary states and reversible legislative 'devolution,' federalism accommodates vast geographic scale and deep ethnic or linguistic diversity.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
In a true federation (United States, Germany, Canada, India, Switzerland, Brazil), both central and regional governments draw independent authority directly from a supreme constitution that neither side can unilaterally abolish. Federalism applies the 'subsidiarity' principle — handling decisions at the closest competent local level — while allowing states to act as Justice Brandeis's 'laboratories of democracy' testing policies before national adoption.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
By contrast, unitary states (France, Japan, UK) concentrate sovereignty in one national parliament, though the UK and Spain grant substantial reversible 'devolution' to Scotland, Wales, Catalonia, and the Basque Country. 'Fiscal federalism' balances local tax autonomy against central equalization transfers ensuring poorer regions afford equal schools and hospitals.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
While federalism diffuses tyranny and accommodates regional identities, it risks coordination failures during pandemics, stark civil-rights disparities across state lines, and 'race-to-the-bottom' tax competition.

## Key Terminology
- **Federal vs. Unitary state**: Constitutionally divided vertical sovereignty versus single supreme national legislature.
- **Devolution**: Statutory delegation of powers from a central parliament to regional assemblies without entrenched constitutional lock-in.
- **Subsidiarity**: Governance rule assigning tasks to the smallest, most local competent authority unless central action is needed.
- **Laboratories of democracy**: Brandeis's metaphor for regional states experimenting with novel policies before national scaling.
- **Fiscal federalism**: Allocation of taxing powers, spending duties, and equalization grants across government tiers.
- **Cooperative vs. Dual federalism**: Intertwined joint central-state policy funding versus strictly separated 'layer-cake' spheres.
