# Regulatory Capture and Governance

## Overview
Regulatory capture occurs when a government agency created to protect the public interest ends up advancing the commercial or political interests of the very industry it was charged with policing. Diagnosed by Nobel laureate George Stigler, capture explains why well-intentioned laws often entrench incumbent monopolies.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Building on Mancur Olson's 'Logic of Collective Action,' capture arises from stark political asymmetry: concentrated industries (banks, airlines, telecoms) have billions at stake and organize high-powered lobbyists effortlessly, whereas millions of dispersed consumers lose only a few dollars each and remain rationally inattentive. Through 'revolving door' career pipelines (regulators leaving for lucrative industry consulting jobs), technical information dependency, and subtle cultural sympathy ('cognitive capture'), watchdogs soften enforcement.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Even seemingly tough rules — complex occupational licensing or high compliance paperwork — can be weaponized by incumbents as 'barriers to entry' crushing small startups. Classic historical examples include pre-1970s airline/trucking rate boards and lax financial derivatives oversight prior to 2008.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Anti-capture reforms mandate cooling-off bans on revolving-door lobbying, transparent conflict-of-interest disclosures, independent consumer advocates inside agencies, and open public data.

## Key Terminology
- **Regulatory capture**: Subversion of a regulatory agency to serve the commercial priorities of its regulated industry.
- **Cognitive capture**: Subtle alignment of regulators' worldview and assumptions with industry insiders through social proximity.
- **Revolving door**: Personnel pipeline moving officials back and forth between government regulatory posts and corporate lobbying.
- **Collective action problem**: Olson's asymmetry where small concentrated groups out-organize large dispersed public majorities.
- **Barrier to entry**: Licensing or compliance hurdle exploited by incumbent firms to block new competitors.
- **Cooling-off period**: Statutory waiting period barring former officials from lobbying their old agency.
