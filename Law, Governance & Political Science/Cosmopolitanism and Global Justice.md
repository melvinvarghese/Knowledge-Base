# Cosmopolitanism and Global Justice

## Overview
Cosmopolitanism holds that all human beings belong to a single moral community regardless of national borders. Challenging strict nationalist priority, modern global-justice philosophers debate open borders, global wealth redistribution, and cosmopolitan democratic institutions.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Dating to Diogenes the Cynic declaring himself a 'citizen of the world' (*kosmopolitēs*) and Stoic universalism, cosmopolitanism reached modern institutional form in Immanuel Kant's *Perpetual Peace* (1795), proposing a federation of free republics bound by universal 'cosmopolitan right' to peaceful hospitality across borders.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Following Rawls, cosmopolitan theorists (Charles Beitz, Thomas Pogge, Martha Nussbaum) argued that in an interdependent global economy, one's birthplace nationality is just as morally arbitrary as race or class — demanding global poverty alleviation and fair trade rules rather than confining Rawls's Difference Principle inside rich borders. Conversely, 'liberal nationalists' (David Miller) counter that deep trust and welfare solidarity require bounded national communities.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Cosmopolitan ethics animates international criminal tribunals, pandemic vaccine equity campaigns, and debates over global carbon budgets.

## Key Terminology
- **Moral cosmopolitanism**: Ethical view that every human holds equal moral worth independent of nationality or citizenship.
- **Political cosmopolitanism**: Institutional proposal for global governance bodies, courts, or federated world democracy.
- **Perpetual Peace**: Kant's 1795 blueprint linking republican constitutions, free federation, and cosmopolitan hospitality.
- **Global Difference Principle**: Cosmopolitan extension of Rawlsian redistribution across international borders.
- **Luck egalitarianism**: View that inequalities stemming from unchosen brute luck (like birthplace) demand compensation.
- **Communitarian critique**: Argument that moral duties grow thickest within concrete shared communities rather than abstract humanity.
