# Postcolonial Political Thought

## Overview
Postcolonial political theory examines how European colonialism reshaped global law, racial hierarchy, economic dependency, and cultural identity — and how colonized thinkers from Frantz Fanon and Mahatma Gandhi to Edward Said and Gayatri Spivak imagined liberation beyond merely swapping foreign rulers for local elites.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Emerging alongside mid-20th-century Asian and African independence struggles, postcolonial thinkers exposed the hypocrisy of Enlightenment universalism coexisting with imperial conquest and racial subjugation. Frantz Fanon's *The Wretched of the Earth* (1961) diagnosed colonial psychology and warned that post-independence 'national bourgeoisie' often mimic Western elites rather than transforming feudal poverty. Mahatma Gandhi forged *Satyagraha* (nonviolent mass civil resistance) and *Swaraj* (moral and economic self-rule) rejecting industrial imperial violence.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Later scholars deepened cultural critique: Edward Said's *Orientalism* (1978) showed how Western scholarship constructed the 'East' as backward and exotic to justify imperial tutelage, while Gayatri Spivak and Subaltern Studies asked how marginalized peasants ('the subaltern') are silenced inside elite national archives.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Postcolonial theory now guides debates on sovereign debt relief, climate reparations, museum artifact repatriation, and reforming Eurocentric international law.

## Key Terminology
- **Orientalism (Said)**: Western academic and artistic discourse framing Eastern societies as static, irrational, and needing imperial rule.
- **Subaltern**: Gramscian term adapted by South Asian historians for subordinated groups excluded from hegemonic power.
- **Swaraj**: Gandhian concept uniting political national independence with personal moral self-mastery.
- **Neocolonialism**: Continued economic, monetary, and geopolitical dominance over formally independent former colonies.
- **Epistemic injustice**: Discrediting or marginalizing colonized peoples' indigenous knowledge and voices.
- **Wretched of the Earth**: Frantz Fanon's landmark 1961 analysis of colonial violence, psychology, and decolonization pitfalls.
