# Environmental and Climate Law

## Overview
Environmental law regulates human impacts on air, water, biodiversity, and climate stability. Evolving from 1970s command-and-control pollution statutes to market cap-and-trade and constitutional climate litigation, it translates ecological limits into enforceable legal duties.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Modern environmental law crystallized around 1970 with the US National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA — pioneering mandatory Environmental Impact Assessments), Clean Air and Clean Water Acts, and the 1972 Stockholm Declaration. Two foundational legal principles anchor global environmental governance: the 'Polluter Pays Principle' (internalizing environmental cleanup costs into production prices rather than socializing harm) and the 'Precautionary Principle' (lack of full scientific certainty must not delay preventing serious, irreversible ecological damage).

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Instrument design balances command-and-control technology mandates against market mechanisms like emissions cap-and-trade (successfully curbing US acid rain in the 1990s) and carbon pricing. Recently, 'climate litigation' surged worldwide: in *Urgenda v. Netherlands* (2019) and *Neubauer v. Germany* (2021), supreme courts ruled weak climate targets violate citizens' constitutional rights to life and future liberty.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Emerging frontiers grant legal personhood to rivers and ecosystems (e.g., Whanganui River in New Zealand) and police corporate 'greenwashing' disclosures.

## Key Terminology
- **Precautionary principle**: Legal rule advising protective action against severe environmental risk even amid scientific uncertainty.
- **Polluter Pays Principle**: Doctrine assigning cleanup and externalized environmental damage costs directly to the polluting actor.
- **Environmental Impact Assessment**: Mandatory pre-project public review evaluating ecological consequences and alternatives.
- **Cap-and-trade**: Market regulatory system capping total emissions while allowing firms to trade permits.
- **Climate litigation**: Lawsuits compelling governments or fossil-fuel corporations to cut emissions under human-rights or tort duties.
- **Rights of nature**: Legal frameworks recognizing rivers, forests, or ecosystems as rights-bearing legal persons.
