# Antitrust and Competition Law

## Overview
Antitrust (competition) law protects open markets from monopolies, price-fixing cartels, and anticompetitive mergers. Born in the 1890 Sherman Act against Gilded Age trusts, modern competition policy is undergoing a fierce revival to police Big Tech digital platforms.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Competition law targets three core threats: horizontal collusion (rival firms secretly fixing prices, rigging bids, or dividing markets — universally treated as *per se* illegal), anticompetitive mergers eliminating head-to-head rivals, and monopolization / abuse of dominant market power (predatory pricing, exclusive dealing, or tying).

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
From the late 1970s, the Chicago School (Robert Bork) narrowed US antitrust around the 'Consumer Welfare Standard,' tolerating mega-mergers and vertical integration whenever short-term retail prices stayed low. In the 2020s, 'Neo-Brandeisian' reformers (Lina Khan, Tim Wu) and European Commission regulators (DMA/DSA) challenged this narrow price lens, arguing zero-price digital giants (Google, Amazon, Meta, Apple) entrench gatekeeper monopolies via network effects, self-preferencing, and 'killer acquisitions' of nascent startups.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Effective antitrust preserves both consumer prices and vibrant startup innovation, labor wage competition, and democratic dispersion of economic power.

## Key Terminology
- **Sherman Act (1890)**: Foundational US antitrust statute banning trade-restraining cartels and unlawful monopolization.
- **Consumer Welfare Standard**: Chicago School antitrust benchmark evaluating mergers primarily by short-term consumer price and output.
- **Network effects**: Platform dynamic where a service grows more valuable as more users join, creating natural winner-take-all moats.
- **Cartel / Price fixing**: Secret horizontal agreement among competitors to set prices or restrict output.
- **Vertical vs. Horizontal merger**: Combining supply-chain stages (supplier + retailer) versus combining direct competitors.
- **Digital Markets Act (DMA)**: Landmark EU ex-ante regulation imposing strict interoperability and anti-self-preferencing rules on tech gatekeepers.
