# Humanitarian Intervention and R2P

## Overview
When a sovereign government slaughters its own citizens or collapses into genocidal civil war, does the world stand by behind non-intervention borders or intervene militarily? The 2005 Responsibility to Protect (R2P) doctrine sought to resolve this agonizing dilemma.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Haunted by inaction during Rwanda's 1994 genocide and NATO's unauthorized 1999 Kosovo bombing, UN member states unanimously endorsed the Responsibility to Protect (R2P) at the 2005 World Summit. R2P rests on three pillars: (1) every state bears primary responsibility to protect its population from genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing, and crimes against humanity; (2) the international community pledges to assist states in building protective capacity; and (3) if a state 'manifestly fails,' the UN Security Council may authorize timely, decisive collective action — including force as a last resort.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Drawing on classical 'Just War' criteria (right intention, last resort, proportional means, reasonable prospect of success), R2P initially gained traction — yet NATO's 2011 Libya intervention, which expanded beyond civilian protection into regime change, alienated Russia, China, and major Global South powers, stalling subsequent R2P action in Syria and Myanmar.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Today, atrocity-prevention diplomats focus heavily on R2P's non-military Pillar One and Two tools — early warning, targeted sanctions, and mediation — alongside ongoing debates over UN veto restraint during mass atrocities.

## Key Terminology
- **Responsibility to Protect (R2P)**: 2005 UN norm holding states responsible for preventing atrocities and empowering collective action when states fail.
- **Just War Theory**: Ethical tradition specifying criteria (*jus ad bellum*, *jus in bello*) for morally permissible warfare.
- **Jus ad bellum vs. Jus in bello**: Legal/moral rules governing the decision to enter war versus humanitarian conduct *during* combat.
- **Humanitarian intervention**: Cross-border military force used to halt mass atrocities, controversial when lacking UNSC authorization.
- **Uniting for Peace**: 1950 UN General Assembly procedure allowing emergency recommendations when P5 vetoes paralyze the Security Council.
- **Regime change backlash**: Political fallout after 2011 Libya intervention that undermined consensus for armed R2P missions.
