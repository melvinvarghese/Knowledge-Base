# International Law and Treaty Systems

## Overview
Public international law governs legal relations among sovereign states, international organizations, and increasingly individuals. Lacking a world police force or global parliament, international law operates horizontally through consent-based treaties, customary state practice, and reciprocal reputation.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Article 38 of the International Court of Justice Statute enumerates international law's primary sources: international treaties (*pacta sunt servanda* — agreements must be kept), customary international law (widespread state practice accepted as legally obligatory, *opinio juris*), general principles of law recognized by civilized nations, and judicial/scholarly writings. Above ordinary rules sit *jus cogens* peremptory norms — banning genocide, slavery, torture, and aggressive war — which no treaty may override.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
How international law enters domestic courts divides into 'monist' states (where ratified treaties apply directly as internal law) and 'dualist' states (requiring parliamentary implementing statutes). Despite realist skepticism over weak enforcement, Louis Henkin famously observed that 'almost all nations observe almost all principles of international law almost all of the time' across aviation, postal, trade, and diplomatic immunity.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Modern international law navigates tensions between classic state sovereignty and expanding human-rights, climate, and cyber accountability.

## Key Terminology
- **Pacta sunt servanda**: Foundational international law principle that ratified treaties are binding and must be performed in good faith.
- **Customary international law**: Binding norms emerging from consistent state practice paired with sense of legal obligation (*opinio juris*).
- **Jus cogens**: Peremptory, non-derogable international norms (e.g., bans on genocide, torture, slavery).
- **Monism vs. Dualism**: Constitutional models treating international and domestic law as one unified order versus separate systems.
- **Diplomatic immunity**: Vienna Convention protections shielding accredited diplomats from host-state arrest and prosecution.
- **State responsibility**: Legal rules governing when states owe reparations for internationally wrongful acts.
