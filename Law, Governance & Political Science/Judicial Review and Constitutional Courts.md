# Judicial Review and Constitutional Courts

## Overview
Judicial review empowers courts to strike down statutes or executive acts that violate a constitution. Splitting globally between decentralized US-style review and specialized European Kelsenian constitutional courts, it raises the enduring 'counter-majoritarian difficulty' of unelected judges overturning elected legislatures.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
In the United States, Chief Justice John Marshall famously asserted judicial review in *Marbury v. Madison* (1803), declaring it 'emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is.' Under this decentralized model, any ordinary judge can refuse to apply an unconstitutional statute in a concrete dispute.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
After World War II, traumatized by fascist legislative majorities, Europe, Latin America, and parts of Asia widely adopted Hans Kelsen's centralized model: dedicated Constitutional Courts (e.g., Germany's *Bundesverfassungsgericht*, South Africa's Constitutional Court) exercising abstract *a priori* or *a posteriori* review independently of ordinary appeals. Judges deploy rival interpretive methods — originalism/textualism versus living-tree pragmatism and global proportionality balancing.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
While constitutional courts protect minority rights and electoral fairness, critics warn against 'juristocracy' when polarized politics shifts legislative battles into the courtroom.

## Key Terminology
- **Judicial review**: Judicial authority to invalidate legislative statutes or executive actions conflicting with constitutional law.
- **Counter-majoritarian difficulty**: Alexander Bickel's phrase for the democratic tension of unelected judges overruling elected majorities.
- **Kelsenian model**: Centralized European system vesting constitutional review exclusively in a specialized Constitutional Court.
- **Proportionality test**: Global constitutional balancing standard assessing whether a rights restriction is suitable, necessary, and proportionate.
- **Originalism**: Interpretive doctrine anchoring constitutional meaning in public understanding at the time of enactment.
- **Standing (Locus standi)**: Threshold requirement that a litigant suffered concrete personal injury to bring a lawsuit.
