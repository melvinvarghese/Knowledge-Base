# Parliamentary vs. Presidential Systems

## Overview
Democracies organize executive-legislative relations around two primary blueprints: parliamentary systems fusing cabinet leadership with legislative majorities, and presidential systems separately electing a fixed-term chief executive — alongside hybrid semi-presidential models.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
In parliamentary democracies (UK, Germany, Japan, India, Canada), voters elect the legislature, and the Prime Minister and cabinet survive only while holding a parliamentary majority (removable at any time via a vote of no confidence, or calling snap elections). This fusion ensures decisive policy implementation and unified accountability, though multi-party coalitions can trigger frequent cabinet reshuffles unless stabilized by tools like Germany's 'constructive vote of no confidence.'

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
In presidential systems (US, Brazil, Indonesia, Nigeria), voters separately elect a fixed-term president and legislature, creating 'dual democratic legitimacy.' Juan Linz famously warned of 'perils of presidentialism': fixed terms prevent peaceful mid-term exits during scandals, winner-take-all executive races polarize politics, and divided government breeds bitter gridlock — though semi-presidentialism (France, Poland) splits power between an elected president and a parliament-accountable premier.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Comparative scholars note parliamentary systems historically enjoy higher survival rates in young democracies, whereas robust courts and federalism help stabilize presidential republics.

## Key Terminology
- **Vote of no confidence**: Parliamentary mechanism forcing a Prime Minister and cabinet to resign upon losing legislative majority.
- **Constructive no-confidence**: German rule requiring parliament to simultaneously elect a replacement Chancellor before ousting the incumbent.
- **Dual democratic legitimacy**: Linz's term for presidential conflict when both president and congress claim independent popular mandates.
- **Divided government**: Presidential stalemate where opposing parties control the executive branch and at least one legislative chamber.
- **Semi-presidentialism**: Hybrid system pairing a directly elected president with a Prime Minister accountable to parliament.
- **Cohabitation**: French semi-presidential scenario where president and parliamentary majority belong to rival parties.
