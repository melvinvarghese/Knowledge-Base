# Human Rights Law and Global Institutions

## Overview
Modern international human rights law affirms that every human being holds inherent dignity and fundamental entitlements independent of citizenship or state generosity. Anchored in the 1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR), regional courts and UN treaties translate moral claims into enforceable legal standards.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Shocked by Holocaust atrocities, the UN General Assembly adopted the UDHR in 1948 under Eleanor Roosevelt's chairmanship, later codified into two binding 1966 covenants: the ICCPR (civil and political rights: free speech, fair trial, torture ban) and ICESCR (economic, social, and cultural rights: education, health, labor). Together, these form the 'International Bill of Human Rights,' expanded by specialized treaties on racial discrimination, women's rights (CEDAW), children (CRC), and disability.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Enforcement varies starkly by region: while UN treaty committees rely largely on periodic reporting and 'naming and shaming,' the European Court of Human Rights (Strasbourg) and Inter-American Court issue binding judgments awarding damages and forcing national law reforms across dozens of countries.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Critics debate universalism versus cultural relativism and selective geopolitical enforcement, yet human rights treaties remain vital tools for domestic activists, constitutional judges, and asylum seekers.

## Key Terminology
- **UDHR (1948)**: Landmark UN Universal Declaration of Human Rights setting foundational global human-dignity standards.
- **ICCPR and ICESCR**: Twin 1966 binding UN Covenants covering civil-political liberties and socio-economic rights.
- **Non-derogable rights**: Core rights (life, freedom from torture and slavery) that may never be suspended even in war or emergency.
- **European Court of Human Rights**: Supranational Strasbourg court adjudicating individual human-rights petitions against 46 European states.
- **Margin of appreciation**: Strasbourg doctrine granting national democracies reasonable cultural leeway in implementing rights.
- **Universalism vs. Cultural relativism**: Debate over whether human rights apply identically worldwide or vary with local traditions.
