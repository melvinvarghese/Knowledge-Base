# Cybersecurity Law and Critical Infrastructure

## Overview
As power grids, hospitals, water utilities, and banking networks run on connected code, cybersecurity law bridges national security and corporate regulation — mandating breach disclosures, resilience standards for critical infrastructure, and cross-border cybercrime cooperation.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Traditional computer-crime statutes (like the 1986 US Computer Fraud and Abuse Act) focused narrowly on criminalizing unauthorized hacking, often leaving legitimate security researchers fearing prosecution when disclosing flaws responsibly. Modern frameworks — such as the EU's NIS2 Directive and US sector-specific rules (HIPAA healthcare, SEC material breach reporting) — shift responsibility onto corporate boards and essential-service operators to maintain encryption, multi-factor authentication, and incident-response plans.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Ransomware attacks on hospitals and fuel pipelines exposed severe public-private coordination gaps: private firms own ~85% of critical infrastructure yet hesitate to share breach telemetry with intelligence agencies out of liability fears, prompting mandatory federal 72-hour breach reporting laws and international treaties like the Budapest Convention on Cybercrime.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Balancing mandatory security baselines, safe-harbor vulnerability disclosure, and rapid international law-enforcement takedowns defines modern cyber governance.

## Key Terminology
- **Critical infrastructure**: Essential physical and digital systems (energy, water, finance, healthcare) vital to national security and public safety.
- **Breach notification law**: Statute requiring organizations to alert regulators and affected individuals promptly after data exposure.
- **Coordinated Vulnerability Disclosure**: Safe-harbor legal framework letting ethical hackers report software flaws without prosecution.
- **NIS2 Directive**: Updated EU cybersecurity law expanding mandatory risk-management rules across vital sectors.
- **Budapest Convention**: First international treaty harmonizing cybercrime offenses and cross-border digital evidence sharing.
- **Software Bill of Materials (SBOM)**: Transparent inventory of third-party software components increasingly mandated for federal procurement.
