# Welfare State Models

## Overview
Modern industrial democracies cushion citizens against old age, illness, disability, and unemployment through welfare states. Gøsta Esping-Andersen's classic typology revealed three distinct institutional architectures — Liberal, Conservative-Corporatist, and Social Democratic — each reflecting different bargains among state, market, and family.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Tracing from Otto von Bismarck's 1880s German social insurance (binding workers to the state) and Britain's 1942 Beveridge Report ('cradle-to-grave' universalism), Esping-Andersen (1990) classified welfare states by 'decommodification' (survival independence from market wages). Liberal regimes (US, UK, Australia) rely heavily on means-tested poverty aid, modest flat benefits, and private employer insurance; Conservative regimes (Germany, France, Italy) tie generous earnings-related pensions to employment history and traditional family support; and Social Democratic regimes (Sweden, Denmark, Norway) fund universal, high-quality public childcare, healthcare, and education for all citizens via broad taxes.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
A persistent policy puzzle ('the paradox of redistribution,' Walter Korpi) shows universal programs benefiting the middle class alongside the poor sustain far stronger political durability than stigmatized 'poor-only' means-tested programs.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Facing aging populations and automation, contemporary debates test Universal Basic Income (UBI) pilots, sovereign pension funds, and active labor-market retraining.

## Key Terminology
- **Three Worlds of Welfare**: Esping-Andersen's typology dividing welfare states into Liberal, Conservative-Corporatist, and Social Democratic.
- **Means-tested vs. Universal**: Restricting benefits strictly to low-income applicants versus providing services to all citizens equally.
- **Social insurance**: Contributory payroll-funded programs (pensions, unemployment) pooling risks across workers.
- **Paradox of redistribution**: Empirical finding that universal middle-class-inclusive programs achieve deeper poverty reduction than narrow targeting.
- **Active labor market policy**: Job retraining, placement coaching, and wage subsidies helping unemployed workers re-enter employment.
- **Universal Basic Income (UBI)**: Proposed unconditional regular cash grant paid to every resident regardless of work or income.
