# Political Parties and Party Systems

## Overview
Political parties are indispensable democratic intermediaries — aggregating messy citizen interests into coherent platforms, recruiting candidates, structuring legislative votes, and organizing peaceful transitions of power. Yet weakening party loyalty and algorithmic media are reshaping party systems worldwide.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Lipset and Rokkan (1967) traced European party systems to deep historical social 'cleavages' — center vs. periphery, church vs. state, rural vs. urban, and owner vs. worker — freezing alignments for decades. Parties evolved from 19th-century elite 'cadre' clubs through 20th-century 'mass parties' anchored in labor unions and churches into modern 'catch-all' and 'cartel' parties reliant on state subsidies and media consultants.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Party-system structure depends heavily on electoral rules: two-party systems (US) force broad internal coalitions competing for median voters, whereas multi-party PR systems allow green, liberal, Christian-democratic, and populist parties to compete distinctly before negotiating post-election governing coalitions. Meanwhile, open primary elections democratized candidate selection yet often empowered ideological activists over general-election moderates.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
When traditional parties hollow out, personalist outsider movements surge rapidly — highlighting why healthy, locally rooted parties remain vital democratic anchors.

## Key Terminology
- **Social cleavage theory**: Lipset and Rokkan's model linking enduring party divides to historical class, religious, and regional rifts.
- **Mass vs. Catch-all party**: Class-anchored membership organizations versus broad, ideologically flexible election-winning machines.
- **Cartel party**: Katz and Mair's concept of established parties colluding with state subsidies to fend off newcomers.
- **Median voter theorem**: Anthony Downs's spatial model predicting two-party competition converges toward the ideological center.
- **Coalition government**: Multi-party parliamentary cabinet formed when no single party wins an outright majority.
- **Primary election**: Internal party contest selecting general-election nominees, shifting power from party bosses to primary voters.
