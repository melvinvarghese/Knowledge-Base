# Environmental Political Theory

## Overview
Environmental political theory rethinks democracy, property, and justice when human economies collide with planetary boundaries. Moving beyond anthropocentric resource extraction, green theorists debate ecocentrism, Elinor Ostrom's commons governance, and intergenerational climate justice.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Traditional political theories assumed nature as an infinite passive backdrop. Green political thought splits broadly between 'anthropocentric' environmentalism (protecting ecosystems to sustain human health and future generations) and 'ecocentrism' or Deep Ecology (Arne Næss), which attributes intrinsic moral worth to non-human species and ecosystems independent of human utility.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Challenging Garrett Hardin's pessimistic 'Tragedy of the Commons' — which claimed shared pastures inevitably collapse without privatization or state coercion — Nobel laureate Elinor Ostrom proved real communities worldwide sustainably govern fisheries, forests, and irrigation commons for centuries through clear boundaries, local monitoring, and graduated sanctions. Meanwhile, 'environmental justice' exposes how toxic landfills and heat islands disproportionately burden poor and racialized communities.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Contemporary green theory confronts 'intergenerational justice' (how democratic voters represent unborn future citizens), 'just transition' for fossil-fuel workers, and green growth versus degrowth debates.

## Key Terminology
- **Ecocentrism**: Environmental ethic placing intrinsic moral value on whole ecosystems and non-human life rather than humans alone.
- **Governing the Commons**: Elinor Ostrom's empirical framework showing local communities sustainably self-manage shared resources.
- **Intergenerational justice**: Moral and legal obligations owed by living voters to protect planetary habitability for future generations.
- **Environmental justice**: Movement confronting unequal exposure of low-income and minority communities to pollution and climate risk.
- **Just transition**: Policy framework ensuring shifts to clean energy protect displaced workers and vulnerable regions.
- **Anthropocene**: Proposed geological epoch marking humanity's decisive planetary alteration of climate and biosphere.
