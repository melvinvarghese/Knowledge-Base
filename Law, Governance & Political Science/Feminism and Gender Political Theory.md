# Feminism and Gender Political Theory

## Overview
Feminist political theory exposes how traditional legal and philosophical categories silently treated male heads of household as universal citizens while relegating women's unpaid care work to a 'private sphere' outside justice. Across successive historical waves, feminist thought reshaped suffrage, family law, and global human rights.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
First-wave feminism (Mary Wollstonecraft's 1792 *Vindication of the Rights of Woman*, Seneca Falls 1848, Suffragettes) demanded formal legal personhood, property rights, education, and the vote. Second-wave theorists (Simone de Beauvoir, Betty Friedan, Carole Pateman) coined 'the personal is political' — proving unequal domestic labor, marital rape exemptions, and workplace glass ceilings stem from systemic patriarchy rather than private bad luck.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Crucially, Black feminist thinkers — bell hooks, Audre Lorde, and legal scholar Kimberlé Crenshaw — introduced 'intersectionality' (1989) to show how race, class, and gender oppression overlap rather than stack separately (e.g., Black women facing distinct legal blind spots overlooked by white feminism or male civil-rights law). Meanwhile, Susan Moller Okin extended Rawlsian justice inside the family, and Martha Nussbaum built global 'Capabilities Approach' benchmarks.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Feminist legal reforms criminalized domestic violence, banned pregnancy discrimination, and established gender-quota parliaments worldwide, while ongoing debates address reproductive autonomy and care-economy valuation.

## Key Terminology
- **The personal is political**: Second-wave feminist insight that private household and bodily inequalities reflect public power structures.
- **Intersectionality**: Kimberlé Crenshaw's framework analyzing how overlapping identities (race, gender, class) create distinct legal vulnerabilities.
- **Public vs. Private sphere**: Classical division that historically shielded domestic hierarchy from constitutional justice.
- **Capabilities Approach**: Amartya Sen and Martha Nussbaum's justice metric focusing on real freedoms to live flourishing lives.
- **Substantive vs. Formal equality**: Achieving equal real-world outcomes and opportunity versus merely gender-neutral paper statutes.
- **Care economy**: Unpaid and underpaid caregiving labor sustaining all formal market productivity.
