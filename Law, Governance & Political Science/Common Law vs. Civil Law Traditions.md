# Common Law vs. Civil Law Traditions

## Overview
Most modern national legal systems descend from two great European traditions: Anglo-American Common Law, built incrementally by judges through binding precedent, and Continental Civil Law, rooted in Roman law and comprehensive statutory codes.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Originating in post-1066 Norman England's royal courts, Common Law operates inductively through *stare decisis* ('to stand by things decided'): appellate judicial decisions create binding legal rules for lower courts in future analogous cases. Adversarial trials pit opposing attorneys before an impartial judge and lay jury, evolving flexibly alongside social change (used in the UK, US, Canada, India, and Australia).

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
By contrast, Civil Law — descending from Emperor Justinian's *Corpus Juris Civilis* (534 CE) and culminating in Napoleon's 1804 *Code Civil* and Germany's 1900 *BGB* — operates deductively from comprehensive, systematically organized legislative codes. Civil Law judges act as inquisitorial investigators applying statutory text rather than binding precedent (dominant across continental Europe, Latin America, East Asia, and Francophone Africa).

## Real-World Impact & Systems
In practice, the two systems increasingly converge: Common Law nations rely heavily on dense statutes and regulatory codes, while Civil Law supreme courts publish persuasive jurisprudence shaping consistent interpretation.

## Key Terminology
- **Stare decisis**: Common-law doctrine obligating courts to follow established precedent from higher or equal tribunals.
- **Codification**: Systematic compilation of legal rules into unified statutory codes characteristic of Civil Law.
- **Adversarial vs. Inquisitorial**: Trial models contrasting party-driven courtroom contest against judge-led factual investigation.
- **Corpus Juris Civilis**: 6th-century Byzantine compilation of Roman law ordered by Justinian I, foundational to Civil Law.
- **Ratio decidendi**: Binding core legal reasoning necessary to decide a Common Law case, distinct from passing remarks (*obiter dicta*).
- **Equity**: Parallel English Chancery body of fairness remedies (injunctions, trusts) supplementing rigid Common Law writs.
