# Social Contract Theory (Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau)

## Overview
Social contract theory grounds political legitimacy not in divine right of kings, but in the rational consent of free individuals escaping an imagined 'state of nature.' Thomas Hobbes, John Locke, and Jean-Jacques Rousseau forged the three foundational blueprints of modern state authority, property, and popular sovereignty.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Writing amid the English Civil War, Thomas Hobbes's *Leviathan* (1651) pictured the state of nature as a terrifying 'war of all against all' where life is 'solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short.' To secure survival, individuals surrender nearly all freedom to an undivided sovereign Leviathan capable of enforcing peace. Forty years later, John Locke's *Second Treatise of Government* (1689) depicted a milder state of nature governed by natural rights to life, liberty, and property — so citizens create a *limited* government as a fiduciary trust, retaining the right to revolution if rulers violate those rights.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Jean-Jacques Rousseau's *The Social Contract* (1762) inverted Hobbes and Locke: civilization and private property corrupted innocent humans ('man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains'). True freedom survives only when citizens obey laws they prescribe themselves as equal participants in the 'general will' (*volonté générale*) aiming at the common good.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Lockean consent directly inspired the US Declaration of Independence, while Rousseau shaped the French Revolution and modern participatory democracy.

## Key Terminology
- **State of nature**: Hypothetical pre-political condition used by contract theorists to justify legitimate state authority.
- **Leviathan**: Hobbes's metaphor for an all-powerful sovereign state necessary to prevent anarchic civil war.
- **Natural rights**: Locke's pre-political entitlements to life, liberty, and property limiting legitimate government.
- **Right of revolution**: Lockean principle that citizens may dissolve a government that persistently breaches its fiduciary trust.
- **General Will (*Volonté générale*)**: Rousseau's concept of the collective civic will aiming at the shared public good rather than private factions.
- **Consent of the governed**: Doctrine that political legitimacy derives from citizens' voluntary agreement rather than heredity or force.
