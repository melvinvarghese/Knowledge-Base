# Bretton Woods and Global Finance

## Overview
At Bretton Woods, New Hampshire in 1944, Allied economists led by John Maynard Keynes and Harry Dexter White designed the postwar international monetary architecture — birthing the International Monetary Fund (IMF), World Bank, and US dollar anchor that still govern sovereign debt crises and development finance.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Aiming to prevent 1930s competitive devaluations and depression, Bretton Woods pegged global currencies to the US dollar (convertible to gold at \$35/oz) while creating the IMF to extend short-term balance-of-payments loans and the World Bank to finance postwar reconstruction and infrastructure. When President Nixon severed gold convertibility in 1971, major currencies floated yet the US dollar retained global reserve hegemony.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
During 1980s–90s developing-nation debt crises, IMF and World Bank 'structural adjustment' loans conditioned bailouts on 'Washington Consensus' austerity, privatization, and trade liberalization — drawing fierce critique for cutting health/education budgets while Beijing's rival Belt and Road Initiative and AIIB offered unconditional infrastructure lending (creating new sovereign-debt restructuring deadlocks).

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Reforming IMF Special Drawing Rights (SDRs), climate-debt swaps, and Paris Club-China creditor coordination anchors modern global financial diplomacy.

## Key Terminology
- **International Monetary Fund (IMF)**: Global financial institution providing emergency balance-of-payments loans and macroeconomic surveillance.
- **World Bank**: Multilateral development bank funding long-term poverty reduction, schools, and infrastructure.
- **Washington Consensus**: 1990s policy package emphasizing fiscal discipline, privatization, deregulation, and trade openness.
- **Dollar reserve hegemony**: Dominant global role of the US dollar in central-bank reserves, oil pricing, and SWIFT trade settlement.
- **Conditionality**: Policy reforms (tax hikes, subsidy cuts) required by the IMF in exchange for emergency bailouts.
- **Special Drawing Rights (SDR)**: Supplementary international reserve asset created by the IMF pegged to a basket of five major currencies.
