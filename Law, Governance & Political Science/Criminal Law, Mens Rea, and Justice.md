# Criminal Law, Mens Rea, and Justice

## Overview
Criminal law defines public offenses against society and authorizes state punishment — from fines and probation to incarceration. Built on the twin pillars of guilty act (*actus reus*) and culpable mental state (*mens rea*), modern criminal justice balances deterrence, retribution, and rehabilitation against strict due-process protections.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Under Common and Civil Law alike, mere bad thoughts cannot be punished without a voluntary prohibited act or omission (*actus reus*), and accidents rarely incur severe criminal guilt without *mens rea* — graded across purposeful intent, knowledge, recklessness, and criminal negligence (aside from narrow 'strict liability' regulatory offenses). Affirmative defenses include self-defense, necessity, duress, and legal insanity.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Constitutional criminal procedure shields suspects from state power via the presumption of innocence ('beyond a reasonable doubt'), bans on warrantless unreasonable searches, privilege against self-incrimination, and right to defense counsel (*Gideon v. Wainwright*). Yet reality diverges sharply from jury-trial ideals: in the US and elsewhere, over 90–95% of criminal convictions resolve via prosecutor-driven plea bargaining.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Contemporary reform debates confront mass incarceration, racial sentencing disparities, cash bail, and restorative justice alternatives uniting victims and offenders in mediated repair.

## Key Terminology
- **Actus reus and Mens rea**: The physical guilty act and culpable mental state generally required together for criminal liability.
- **Beyond a reasonable doubt**: Highest evidentiary burden of proof required for criminal conviction.
- **Plea bargaining**: Negotiated agreement where a defendant pleads guilty to lesser charges or reduced sentencing to avoid trial.
- **Presumption of innocence**: Foundational rule placing the burden of proof wholly on the prosecution.
- **Restorative justice**: Justice model prioritizing victim healing, offender accountability, and community repair over pure punishment.
- **Exclusionary rule**: Procedural bar prohibiting illegally seized or unconstitutionally obtained evidence at trial.
