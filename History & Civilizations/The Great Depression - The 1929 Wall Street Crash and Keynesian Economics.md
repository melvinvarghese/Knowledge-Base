# The Great Depression - The 1929 Wall Street Crash and Keynesian Economics

## Overview
The Great Depression (1929–1939) was the deepest and most widespread economic collapse in the industrialized capitalist world, restructuring modern macroeconomics and state intervention.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
The 1929 Crash & Deflationary Spiral: Triggered by the Wall Street stock market crash on 'Black Tuesday' (October 29, 1929), fueled by speculative margin borrowing and banking panics. Over 9,000 U.S. banks failed, shrinking money supplies, collapsing industrial production by 50%, and driving unemployment above 25% across the United States and Germany.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
The Keynesian Revolution & The New Deal: Classical economic doctrine asserted markets would self-correct via wage cuts. British economist John Maynard Keynes demonstrated in his 1936 *General Theory* that aggregate demand shortfalls can cause permanent underemployment, advocating counter-cyclical government deficit spending and public works. Franklin D. Roosevelt enacted the New Deal, creating federal social security and financial regulatory oversight (SEC, FDIC).

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Birth of modern welfare states, macroeconomics aggregate demand management, rise of radical political extremism in Weimar Germany.

## Key Terminology
- **Black Tuesday**: Catastrophic collapse of the New York Stock Exchange on October 29, 1929, sparking the Great Depression.
- **Keynesian Economics**: Macroeconomic theory arguing that government intervention and deficit spending are necessary to stimulate aggregate demand during recessions.
- **New Deal**: Series of relief, recovery, and financial reform programs enacted by President Franklin D. Roosevelt between 1933 and 1939.
