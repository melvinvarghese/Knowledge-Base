# The Civil Rights Movement - Nonviolence, Legal Desegregation, and Voting Rights

## Overview
The American Civil Rights Movement (1954–1968) was a mass social movement that dismantled legal racial segregation and disenfranchisement across the American South through nonviolent direct action.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Judicial Breakthroughs & Grassroots Direct Action: Initiated legally with the Supreme Court's 1954 *Brown v. Board of Education* ruling striking down 'separate but equal'. Mass direct action gained momentum with the 1955 Montgomery Bus Boycott (Rosa Parks, Martin Luther King Jr.), Freedom Rides, and lunch counter sit-ins challenging southern Jim Crow laws.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Landmark Federal Legislation: The 1963 March on Washington galvanized national conscience, leading President Lyndon B. Johnson to sign the Civil Rights Act of 1964 (outlawing public segregation and employment discrimination) and the Voting Rights Act of 1965 (abolishing literacy tests and imposing federal voter oversight following the Selma to Montgomery marches).

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Global tactical model for nonviolent civil disobedience, human rights legal protections, advancement of egalitarian democracy.

## Key Terminology
- **Civil Rights Act of 1964**: Landmark federal legislation outlawing segregation in public facilities and banning racial discrimination in employment.
- **Voting Rights Act of 1965**: Federal statute prohibiting racial discrimination in voting, overcoming state disenfranchisement mechanisms.
- **Jim Crow Laws**: State and local statutes that legalized and enforced racial segregation across the American South.
