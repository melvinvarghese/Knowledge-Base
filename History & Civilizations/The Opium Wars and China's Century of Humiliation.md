# The Opium Wars and China's Century of Humiliation

## Overview
The Opium Wars (1839–1860) forced Qing China open to Western imperial commerce through military gunboat diplomacy, initiating what China terms its 'Century of Humiliation'.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
First Opium War (1839–1842): To balance trade deficits caused by British demand for Chinese tea, the British East India Company smuggled industrial quantities of Indian-grown opium into China. When Imperial Commissioner Lin Zexu confiscated and destroyed 20,000 chests of opium at Humen, Britain deployed steam warships to shatter Qing coastal defenses.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Treaty of Nanking & The Unequal Treaties: The 1842 Treaty of Nanking forced China to cede Hong Kong Island, open five treaty ports to British merchants, pay massive silver indemnities, and grant extraterritoriality (British citizens immune to Chinese law). The subsequent Second Opium War (1856–1860) led to the burning of the Old Summer Palace by Anglo-French forces.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Catalyst for internal anti-Qing uprisings (Taiping Rebellion: 20–30 million deaths), collapse of imperial tributary prestige.

## Key Terminology
- **Treaty of Nanking**: 1842 treaty ending the First Opium War; the first of China's 'unequal treaties' with Western imperial powers.
- **Extraterritoriality**: State of being exempted from the jurisdiction of local law, granted to foreign nationals in imperial treaty ports.
- **Taiping Rebellion**: Massive civil war in China (1850–1864) led by Hong Xiuquan against the ruling Manchu Qing Dynasty.
