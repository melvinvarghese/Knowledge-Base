# Intelligence and Psychometrics

## Overview
Intelligence research and psychometrics seek to define, measure, and explain individual differences in human cognitive ability. Spanning Charles Spearman's general factor 'g,' Raymond Cattell's fluid-crystallized distinction, and debates over cultural bias and environmental malleability, intelligence testing remains psychology's most empirically predictive yet socially contested domain.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
In 1905, Alfred Binet and Théodore Simon created the first practical intelligence scale in Paris to identify schoolchildren needing extra support — explicitly viewing intelligence as trainable, not fixed. Lewis Terman adapted it into the Stanford-Binet IQ test (ratio of mental age to chronological age × 100), while David Wechsler later introduced deviation IQ scores (mean 100, SD 15) combining verbal and performance subtests. Analyzing test correlations, Charles Spearman (1904) discovered the 'positive manifold' — people scoring high on one cognitive task tend to score high on others — proposing a shared general intelligence factor ('g').

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Later psychometricians refined 'g' hierarchically. Raymond Cattell and John Horn separated fluid intelligence (Gf: novel abstract reasoning and pattern recognition, peaking in young adulthood) from crystallized intelligence (Gc: accumulated vocabulary, factual knowledge, and cultural expertise, rising across much of life). John Carroll's Three-Stratum Theory unified these under 'g.' Alternative broader frameworks — Howard Gardner's Multiple Intelligences and Robert Sternberg's Triarchic Theory (analytical, creative, practical) — challenged narrow academic testing, though psychometricians note Gardner's domains often correlate under 'g' or reflect personality/talents rather than independent intelligences.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
IQ scores correlate meaningfully with educational attainment, job complexity, and health outcomes, yet are shaped dynamically by environment: the 'Flynn effect' documented steady ~3-point-per-decade global IQ gains across the 20th century driven by nutrition, schooling, and visual complexity. Modern behavioral genetics emphasizes high heritability alongside profound environmental sensitivity, rejecting simplistic racial or fatalistic interpretations.

## Key Terminology
- **General factor (g)**: Spearman's statistical construct capturing shared variance across diverse cognitive ability tests.
- **Fluid vs. Crystallized intelligence**: Novel abstract problem-solving capacity (Gf) versus accumulated knowledge and language skills (Gc).
- **Flynn effect**: Observed generational rise in average IQ scores across the 20th century driven by environmental improvements.
- **Deviation IQ**: Standardized score comparing an individual's performance to same-age peers on a bell curve (mean 100, SD 15).
- **Factor analysis**: Statistical method identifying underlying latent dimensions explaining correlations among test items.
- **Stereotype threat**: Situational anxiety over confirming a negative group stereotype that can depress cognitive test performance.
