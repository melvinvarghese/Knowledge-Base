# Behaviorism and Classical Conditioning

## Overview
Behaviorism dominated psychology from the 1910s to the 1950s by insisting science study only observable stimulus-response relationships rather than unmeasurable introspection. Initiated by Ivan Pavlov's classical conditioning and John B. Watson's manifesto, it uncovered precise associative learning laws that remain central to neuroscience and clinical therapy.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Russian physiologist Ivan Pavlov discovered classical (respondent) conditioning in the 1890s while studying canine digestion: dogs began salivating to footsteps or a bell before food arrived. By repeatedly pairing a neutral Conditioned Stimulus (CS, a bell) with an automatic Unconditioned Stimulus (US, food) eliciting an Unconditioned Response (UR, salivation), the CS alone came to trigger a Conditioned Response (CR). Pavlov also mapped acquisition, extinction (weakening when US is withheld), spontaneous recovery, stimulus generalization, and discrimination.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
In 1913, John B. Watson launched American behaviorism, famously (and controversially) conditioning 'Little Albert' to fear white rats via loud noise pairings. Later, Robert Rescorla and Allan Wagner (1972) revolutionized associative theory by showing classical conditioning is not blind contiguity, but predictive error-correction: animals learn only when the US is surprising relative to current expectations — directly inspiring temporal-difference reinforcement learning in AI.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Classical conditioning explains drug tolerance (environmental cues trigger compensatory physiological responses, making unfamiliar settings riskier for overdose), conditioned taste aversions, and trauma triggers. Clinically, systematic desensitization and exposure therapy rely directly on classical extinction to treat phobias and PTSD.

## Key Terminology
- **Classical conditioning**: Associative learning in which a neutral stimulus acquires the capacity to elicit a response after pairing with a biologically potent stimulus.
- **Extinction**: Gradual weakening of a conditioned response when the conditioned stimulus is repeatedly presented without the unconditioned stimulus.
- **Spontaneous recovery**: Reappearance of an extinguished conditioned response after a rest period, showing extinction suppresses rather than erases learning.
- **Stimulus generalization**: Tendency for stimuli similar to the conditioned stimulus to evoke the conditioned response.
- **Rescorla-Wagner model**: Mathematical learning rule stating conditioning depends on prediction error — the discrepancy between expected and actual outcomes.
- **Conditioned taste aversion**: Rapid, often single-trial association between a novel flavor and subsequent nausea, evolved for poison avoidance.
