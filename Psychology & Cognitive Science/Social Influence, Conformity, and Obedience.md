# Social Influence, Conformity, and Obedience

## Overview
Social influence research demonstrates how powerfully group consensus, unspoken norms, and legitimate authority shape individual behavior. Landmark post-WWII experiments by Solomon Asch and Stanley Milgram revealed that ordinary, non-malicious people frequently override personal perception and conscience under social pressure.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
In the 1950s, Solomon Asch asked participants to match simple line lengths alongside confederates who unanimously gave blatantly wrong answers. Roughly 75% of participants conformed at least once, and about 37% of critical trials yielded conformity — driven by normative influence (fear of social rejection) and informational influence (doubting one's own eyes when uncertain). Crucially, having just one dissenting ally slashed conformity dramatically.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
A decade later, Stanley Milgram probed obedience to authority: participants instructed by a lab-coated experimenter delivered what they believed were increasingly severe electric shocks (up to 450 volts) to a protesting learner. Roughly 65% complied fully to the maximum voltage, though subsequent archival reviews highlighted nuanced resistance, coercion, and experimenter prods. Similarly, Philip Zimbardo's 1971 Stanford Prison Experiment illustrated how assigned institutional roles can escalate abuse — though modern historians critique Zimbardo's active coaching of 'guards,' emphasizing instead Alex Haslam and Stephen Reicher's 'engaged followership' model where people obey authorities they identify with morally.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Social influence science illuminates whistleblower courage, bystander apathy (Darley and Latané's diffusion of responsibility), jury deliberation reforms, and how social norms messaging (highlighting what peers actually do) curbs littering, binge drinking, and energy waste.

## Key Terminology
- **Normative vs. Informational influence**: Conforming to gain social approval and avoid rejection versus conforming because others seem better informed.
- **Asch conformity paradigm**: Line-judgment experiments demonstrating public compliance with an obviously incorrect unanimous group.
- **Milgram obedience studies**: Controversial 1960s experiments showing high rates of compliance with harmful instructions from a perceived authority.
- **Diffusion of responsibility**: Bystander phenomenon where individuals feel less personal obligation to act when others are present.
- **Engaged followership**: Modern reinterpretation showing obedience relies on active identification with an authority's perceived noble cause.
- **Minority influence**: Serge Moscovici's finding that consistent, confident minorities can gradually convert majority opinion.
