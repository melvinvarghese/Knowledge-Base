# Time Perception and Subjective Duration

## Overview
Unlike vision or hearing, humans have no dedicated sense organ for time, yet the brain tracks milliseconds for speech and music, seconds for action, and years in memory. Psychological time stretches under threat or boredom and seems to accelerate with age — driven by attention, arousal, and memory density.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Cognitive models distinguish prospective timing (paying active attention to elapsed seconds, explained by Treisman and Gibbon's Scalar Expectancy pacemaker-accumulator clock — where attention acts as a switch gating internal pulses) from retrospective timing (looking back over an hour or decade, judged by how many distinct, novel memories were encoded). This solves the classic 'holiday paradox': a novel vacation feels brief while occupied yet expansive afterward in memory, whereas monotonous routines drag in the moment yet vanish quickly in hindsight.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Emotional arousal and surprise distort subjective duration dramatically. David Eagleman's 'oddball effect' shows unexpected stimuli feel ~20–30% longer than identical-duration familiar items because novelty triggers deeper encoding and transient neural suppression. Similarly, terrifying falls do not actually speed up visual frame rates, as Eagleman proved using wrist-mounted strobing chronometers — rather, dense amygdala-driven memory encoding makes the crisis feel stretched in immediate recall. Dopamine levels and body temperature also modulate internal clock speed.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Time perception research informs ADHD impulsivity (altered temporal discounting), Parkinson's motor timing, cinema pacing, and user-experience design (making loading waits feel shorter through engaging progress feedback).

## Key Terminology
- **Prospective vs. Retrospective timing**: Judging duration while actively monitoring time versus estimating elapsed time afterward from memory.
- **Holiday paradox**: Contrast between novel experiences feeling fast in the moment yet long in retrospect, and routines doing the opposite.
- **Pacemaker-accumulator model**: Internal clock model where an attentional switch gates neural pulses into an accumulator compared against memory.
- **Oddball effect**: Perceptual illusion making a novel, unexpected stimulus appear to last longer than repeated identical stimuli.
- **Tachypsychia**: Subjective sensation that time slows down during extreme danger or trauma due to dense retrospective memory encoding.
- **Scalar property**: Weber's-law principle that timing variability grows proportionally with the total interval being timed.
