# Cognitive Load and Multitasking

## Overview
Cognitive Load Theory, developed by John Sweller in the late 1980s, examines how the narrow bottleneck of working memory constrains learning and complex performance. Paired with research on task-switching and 'attention residue,' it proves that human 'multitasking' is a costly neurological illusion.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Sweller divided total working memory demand during learning into three components: intrinsic load (inherent complexity and element interactivity of the material), extraneous load (unnecessary mental effort caused by poor presentation, cluttered slides, or split attention), and germane load (productive mental effort devoted to building durable long-term schemas). Effective instructional design minimizes extraneous load — for instance, integrating labels directly onto diagrams or pairing spoken narration with clean visuals rather than redundant on-screen text.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Simultaneously, cognitive scientists proved the brain cannot execute two attention-demanding tasks truly in parallel; instead, it rapidly switches mental rule sets back and forth. Each switch incurs 'switch costs' — slower reaction times and higher error rates — plus Sophie Leroy's 'attention residue,' where lingering thoughts about an unfinished email or interrupted task degrade performance on the next task for minutes afterward. Chronic heavy media multitaskers often perform worse on distractor-filtering tasks than single-taskers.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Cognitive load research directly guides medical simulation training, cockpit ergonomics, e-learning interface design, and workplace productivity norms. Protecting uninterrupted deep-work blocks and reducing visual clutter significantly improves comprehension, accuracy, and mental stamina.

## Key Terminology
- **Intrinsic cognitive load**: Mental effort dictated by the inherent complexity and interconnectedness of the subject matter itself.
- **Extraneous cognitive load**: Wasted working memory effort imposed by poorly designed instructions, clutter, or distracting formats.
- **Germane cognitive load**: Constructive cognitive effort invested in organizing new information into long-term schemas.
- **Switch cost**: Measurable slowdown and spike in errors when shifting attention between different cognitive rules or tasks.
- **Attention residue**: Persistence of cognitive focus on a prior interrupted task, impairing performance on a subsequent task.
- **Split-attention effect**: Learning impairment occurring when learners must mentally integrate separated text and visual diagrams.
