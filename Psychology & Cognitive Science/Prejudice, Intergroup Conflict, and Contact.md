# Prejudice, Intergroup Conflict, and Contact

## Overview
Intergroup psychology investigates why humans rapidly divide the social world into 'us' and 'them,' generating prejudice, stereotyping, and discrimination — and identifies evidence-based conditions under which intergroup hostility gives way to cooperation.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Henri Tajfel's 'minimal group' experiments proved how effortlessly tribalism ignites: strangers randomly sorted by coin flip or preference for Klee vs. Kandinsky paintings immediately favored in-group members when allocating points. Tajfel and John Turner formalized Social Identity Theory: because part of our self-esteem derives from group membership, people seek positive distinctiveness for their in-group — often via out-group derogation and perceiving out-groups as uniformly alike ('out-group homogeneity effect').

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Muzafer Sherif's 1954 Robbers Cave field experiment demonstrated how Realistic Conflict Theory operates: two groups of boys at an Oklahoma camp turned fiercely hostile when pitted against each other in zero-sum competitions, and neither moral lectures nor simple proximity healed the rift. Only 'superordinate goals' — shared emergencies like a sabotaged water supply requiring joint teamwork — dissolved hostility. Building on Gordon Allport's Contact Hypothesis (1954), Thomas Pettigrew and Linda Tropp's meta-analysis of over 500 studies confirmed intergroup contact reliably reduces prejudice, especially when groups share equal status, common goals, cooperation, and institutional support.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Prejudice research informs post-conflict reconciliation, integrated schooling, anti-bias organizational design, and peacebuilding interventions that replace zero-sum framing with shared superordinate identities.

## Key Terminology
- **Social Identity Theory**: Tajfel and Turner's model explaining how group membership shapes self-esteem and drives in-group favoritism.
- **Minimal group paradigm**: Experimental finding that arbitrary, trivial group assignments immediately trigger in-group bias.
- **Realistic Conflict Theory**: Model proposing intergroup hostility escalates when groups compete over scarce zero-sum resources.
- **Superordinate goal**: Shared objective requiring cooperative effort from rival groups to achieve, reducing intergroup hostility.
- **Contact Hypothesis**: Gordon Allport's principle that structured equal-status interaction between groups reduces prejudice.
- **Out-group homogeneity**: Perceptual bias viewing members of outside groups as more similar to one another than members of one's own group.
