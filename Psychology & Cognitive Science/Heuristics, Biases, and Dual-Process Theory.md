# Heuristics, Biases, and Dual-Process Theory

## Overview
Human reasoning routinely departs from formal logic and probability theory because the mind relies on fast mental shortcuts called heuristics. Pioneered by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, heuristics-and-biases research — unified under Dual-Process Theory — revealed systematic, predictable errors in human judgment.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
In the 1970s, Kahneman and Tversky challenged the classical 'rational actor' assumption by cataloging intuitive shortcuts that work well under time pressure yet misfire predictably. Three foundational heuristics stand out: the availability heuristic (judging frequency or probability by how easily vivid examples come to mind — causing people to fear plane crashes over car accidents), representativeness (judging likelihood by similarity to a stereotype while ignoring statistical base rates, as in the 'Linda problem' conjunction fallacy), and anchoring and adjustment (relying too heavily on an initial arbitrary number when making estimates).

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Dual-Process Theory, popularized in Kahneman's Thinking, Fast and Slow (2011), organizes cognition into two interacting modes: System 1 (fast, automatic, associative, effortless, emotionally charged) and System 2 (slow, effortful, rule-governed, deliberative, easily fatigued). Because System 2 is metabolically lazy, it frequently endorses System 1's intuitive impressions without checking base rates or sample sizes. Meanwhile, Gerd Gigerenzer countered with an 'ecological rationality' perspective, arguing that 'fast and frugal' heuristics often outperform complex statistical models in uncertain real-world environments.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Heuristics-and-biases research transformed medicine (diagnostic checklists countering confirmation bias), law (mitigating anchoring in sentencing), intelligence analysis, and behavioral economics — earning Kahneman the 2002 Nobel Prize in Economics.

## Key Terminology
- **Availability heuristic**: Estimating the likelihood of an event based on how easily vivid instances can be recalled from memory.
- **Representativeness heuristic**: Judging probability by similarity to a prototype while neglecting statistical base rates or sample size.
- **Anchoring effect**: Cognitive bias where an initial reference value disproportionately skews subsequent numerical judgments.
- **Confirmation bias**: Tendency to search for, interpret, and remember evidence that confirms pre-existing beliefs.
- **System 1 vs. System 2**: Dual-process distinction between fast, automatic intuition and slow, effortful analytical reasoning.
- **Base-rate neglect**: Failure to incorporate prior statistical probabilities when evaluating specific descriptive evidence.
