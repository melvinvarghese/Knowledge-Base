# Third-Wave Therapies (ACT, DBT, MBCT)

## Overview
Emerging in the 1990s–2000s, 'third-wave' behavioral therapies shifted focus from directly disputing thought content toward changing one's relationship to thoughts and feelings. Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT), Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT), and Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT) blend behavioral science with mindfulness, acceptance, and values-driven action.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Whereas traditional CBT asks whether a thought is logical or true, third-wave models target 'experiential avoidance' — the exhausting struggle to suppress unwanted anxiety, urges, or memories. Steven Hayes's Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) cultivates 'psychological flexibility' across six interconnected hexaflex processes: cognitive defusion (stepping back to view thoughts as passing words rather than literal commands), acceptance, present-moment contact, self-as-context, clarifying personal values, and committed action.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Marsha Linehan forged Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT) specifically for chronic suicidality and Borderline Personality Disorder, balancing radical acceptance ('you are doing the best you can') with urgent behavioral change ('and you must do better'). DBT teaches four concrete skill modules: mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation, and interpersonal effectiveness. Meanwhile, Zindel Segal, Mark Williams, and John Teasdale created MBCT to prevent depressive relapse by helping recovered patients notice early brooding spirals without sliding into full episodes.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Large clinical trials validate DBT as the gold standard for self-harm reduction, ACT across chronic pain and anxiety, and MBCT for recurrent depression — reshaping modern clinical training around compassion and mindfulness.

## Key Terminology
- **Psychological flexibility**: ACT's core goal: contacting the present moment fully and persisting or changing behavior in service of chosen values.
- **Cognitive defusion**: Stepping back from literal belief in thoughts ('I am having the thought that...') to reduce their behavioral grip.
- **Experiential avoidance**: Rigidly attempting to escape or suppress unpleasant internal thoughts, emotions, or bodily sensations.
- **Dialectical synthesis**: DBT's core balance validating painful emotional reality while simultaneously coaching behavioral change.
- **Distress tolerance**: DBT crisis-survival skills for enduring intense emotional pain without impulsive self-harm.
- **Decentering**: Observing thoughts and feelings as transient mental events rather than permanent truths about the self.
