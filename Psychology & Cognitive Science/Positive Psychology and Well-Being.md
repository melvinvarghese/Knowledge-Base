# Positive Psychology and Well-Being

## Overview
Launched formally in 1998 by Martin Seligman, positive psychology applies rigorous empirical methods to study what makes life worth living — shifting focus from repairing pathology alone toward cultivating strengths, resilience, meaning, and sustainable well-being.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
While psychology made immense strides treating depression and trauma after World War II, Seligman argued the field had neglected human thriving. Rather than endorsing simplistic 'positive thinking,' positive psychology measures subjective well-being across hedonic dimensions (life satisfaction and positive affect) and eudaimonic dimensions (purpose, mastery, and contribution). Seligman's PERMA model organizes flourishing into five measurable pillars: Positive emotion, Engagement (flow), Relationships, Meaning, and Accomplishment.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Empirical studies challenged popular assumptions about happiness. Brickman and Campbell's 'hedonic treadmill' demonstrated that lottery winners and paraplegics adapt surprisingly back toward baseline happiness levels over time, though major life events like chronic unemployment or close bereavement leave lasting shifts. Randomized controlled trials show reliable, modest gains from structured interventions: gratitude journaling, savoring positive moments, using signature character strengths (Peterson and Seligman's VIA taxonomy), and prosocial spending — spending money on others consistently boosts happiness more than self-indulgence.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Positive psychology shaped corporate well-being programs, military resilience training, and 'positive education' in schools. Critics rightly warn against 'toxic positivity' or blaming systemic poverty on individual mindset; mature second-wave positive psychology integrates shadow emotions, grief, and social context into authentic flourishing.

## Key Terminology
- **PERMA model**: Seligman's five-pillar framework of well-being: Positive emotion, Engagement, Relationships, Meaning, and Accomplishment.
- **Hedonic vs. Eudaimonic well-being**: Pleasure and positive mood versus deeper purpose, virtue, and self-realization.
- **Hedonic treadmill**: Tendency for individuals to adapt emotionally back toward a baseline happiness level after major positive or negative events.
- **Signature strengths**: Core character virtues (e.g., curiosity, kindness, perseverance) identified in the VIA classification.
- **Post-traumatic growth**: Positive psychological transformation, deeper relationships, or renewed purpose emerging after navigating severe adversity.
- **Prosocial spending**: Using financial resources to benefit others, reliably elevating subjective well-being across cultures.
