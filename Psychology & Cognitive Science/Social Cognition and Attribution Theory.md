# Social Cognition and Attribution Theory

## Overview
Social cognition examines how people perceive, interpret, and remember information about themselves and other people. Attribution theory reveals how everyday observers act as 'naive psychologists' explaining behavior — routinely overestimating personality traits while underestimating invisible situational pressures.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Fritz Heider launched attribution theory by distinguishing internal dispositional causes (personality, effort) from external situational causes (luck, social pressure). Harold Kelley's covariation model added that we judge causality using consistency, distinctiveness, and consensus. Yet Lee Ross identified the Fundamental Attribution Error (correspondence bias): observers overwhelmingly attribute others' actions — even coerced essays or irritable driving — to fixed character flaws rather than context. Conversely, the actor-observer bias explains our own mistakes via bad luck or stress while judging others dispositionally.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Social categorization automatically activates schemas and stereotypes that conserve cognitive energy yet distort judgment. Susan Fiske's Stereotype Content Model maps how groups are evaluated along two universal axes: Warmth (friend-or-foe intent) and Competence (capability to act). Meanwhile, Dual-process researchers distinguish explicit attitudes from rapid, unconscious implicit associations (measured via the Implicit Association Test, IAT) — though intense debate surrounds how strongly IAT scores predict real-world discrimination versus broader structural factors.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Understanding attribution errors and social heuristics helps defuse workplace blame, improve cross-cultural empathy, and refine organizational hiring (using structured blind rubrics rather than unstructured gut impressions).

## Key Terminology
- **Fundamental Attribution Error**: Tendency to overemphasize internal personality traits and underestimate situational forces when explaining others' behavior.
- **Actor-observer asymmetry**: Attributing one's own behavior to situational circumstances while attributing others' identical behavior to disposition.
- **Self-serving bias**: Crediting personal successes to internal talent while blaming failures on external bad luck.
- **Stereotype Content Model**: Fiske's framework classifying social groups along two primary dimensions: Warmth and Competence.
- **Implicit bias**: Automatic, unendorsed associative evaluations operating outside deliberate conscious control.
- **Just-world hypothesis**: Cognitive bias assuming people ultimately get what they deserve, often fueling victim-blaming.
