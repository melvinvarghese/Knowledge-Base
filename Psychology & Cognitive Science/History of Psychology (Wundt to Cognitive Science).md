# History of Psychology (Wundt to Cognitive Science)

## Overview
Tracing psychology's evolution from 19th-century philosophy and physiology into an empirical science illuminates why today's field spans neurons, unconscious defenses, behaviorist laws, and computational minds.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Psychology's institutional birth is dated to 1879, when Wilhelm Wundt opened the first experimental psychology laboratory in Leipzig, Germany, using controlled reaction times and trained 'introspection' to dissect conscious experience — later formalized by Edward Titchener as Structuralism. In America, William James's Principles of Psychology (1890) championed Functionalism: inspired by Darwin, James asked *why* consciousness, habit, and the 'stream of thought' evolved to help organisms adapt.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
The early 20th century fractured into rival schools: Sigmund Freud's Psychoanalysis probed unconscious conflict; Max Wertheimer's Gestalt psychology studied holistic perception; and John B. Watson and B.F. Skinner's Behaviorism banished mental talk for four decades. In the 1950s–60s, Abraham Maslow and Carl Rogers launched Humanistic Psychology while George Miller, Ulric Neisser, and Noam Chomsky sparked the Cognitive Revolution — which later merged with neuroscience, AI, linguistics, and anthropology into interdisciplinary Cognitive Science.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Rather than one school erasing the others, modern psychology synthesizes their enduring tools: psychophysics from Wundt, evolutionary function from James, reinforcement laws from Skinner, alliance from Rogers, and computational brain models from cognitive neuroscience.

## Key Terminology
- **Structuralism**: Early psychological school (Wundt, Titchener) analyzing basic elements of conscious sensation via introspection.
- **Functionalism**: William James's evolutionary approach studying how mental processes and habits help organisms adapt.
- **Stream of consciousness**: William James's metaphor capturing thought as continuous, personal, ever-changing flow rather than static blocks.
- **Introspection**: Systematic self-observation of immediate conscious sensations, abandoned as unreliable for unconscious processes.
- **Biopsychosocial model**: George Engel's integrative framework uniting biological, psychological, and social levels of explanation.
- **Cognitive science**: Interdisciplinary study of mind and intelligence uniting psychology, neuroscience, AI, linguistics, and philosophy.
