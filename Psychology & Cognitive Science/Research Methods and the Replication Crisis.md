# Research Methods and the Replication Crisis

## Overview
Around 2011, psychology confronted a methodological reckoning known as the Replication Crisis when landmark priming and social experiments failed to reproduce in large independent samples. Rather than weakening science, this crisis catalyzed an Open Science revolution transforming statistical rigor across behavioral and biomedical research.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
In 2015, Brian Nosek's Reproducibility Project attempted to replicate 100 studies published in top psychology journals: while 97% of original papers reported statistically significant results (p < 0.05), only ~36% replicated significantly, with average effect sizes shrinking by half. Methodologists Joseph Simmons, Leif Nelson, and Uri Simonsohn identified pervasive 'researcher degrees of freedom' or 'p-hacking': running small underpowered samples, dropping inconvenient conditions, peeking at data mid-collection, and Hypothesizing After Results are Known (HARKing) — amplified by journals refusing to publish null findings.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Psychology responded with sweeping institutional reforms. Researchers adopted pre-registration (publicly locking hypotheses, sample sizes, and analysis plans on OSF before collecting data), Registered Reports (journals peer-reviewing and accepting studies based on methodology *before* results are known), open data/code sharing, and larger multi-site collaborations boosting statistical power.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Ten years later, replication rates in pre-registered Registered Reports exceed 60–90%, establishing psychology as a pioneer of scientific self-correction now emulated across economics, neuroscience, and preclinical medicine.

## Key Terminology
- **Replication crisis**: Methodological reckoning triggered when many published experimental findings failed to reproduce in independent trials.
- **P-hacking**: Flexible data-analysis practices (dropping outliers, testing multiple outcomes) inflating false-positive significance.
- **HARKing**: Hypothesizing After the Results are Known — presenting exploratory post-hoc discoveries as planned predictions.
- **Pre-registration**: Publicly timestamping study hypotheses, sample size, and statistical plan prior to data collection.
- **Registered Report**: Publishing format where peer review evaluates study design before results exist, eliminating publication bias.
- **Statistical power**: Probability that a study will detect a genuine effect of a given size, requiring adequate sample sizes.
