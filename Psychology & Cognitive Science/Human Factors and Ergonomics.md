# Human Factors and Ergonomics

## Overview
Human factors (ergonomics) applies cognitive psychology, perception, and biomechanics to design tools, cockpits, medical devices, and software that fit human capabilities rather than forcing humans to adapt to error-prone machines. Pioneered after WWII aviation accidents, it saved countless lives by treating 'human error' as a design symptom.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
During World War II, identical-looking flap and landing-gear levers placed side by side caused seasoned pilots to crash planes upon landing; psychologist Alphonse Chapanis redesigned controls with distinct tactile shapes ('shape coding'), eliminating the errors overnight. Donald Norman later brought cognitive ergonomics to everyday objects and software in The Design of Everyday Things (1988), introducing 'affordances' (perceivable action possibilities like a flat plate inviting pushing), 'signifiers,' 'natural mapping' (stove dials arranged matching burner geometry), and 'forcing functions' preventing dangerous mistakes (microwave doors cutting power when opened).

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
James Reason's 'Swiss Cheese Model' of organizational accidents frames catastrophes as rare alignments of latent design flaws, fatigue, and frontline slips rather than single scapegoats. Aviation adopted Crew Resource Management (CRM), flattened cockpit hierarchies, and standardized checklists — later adapted into Atul Gawande's surgical safety checklists, slashing hospital infections and mortality.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Today, human factors shapes autonomous vehicle handoff interfaces (preventing vigilance failures when drivers must suddenly take control), electronic health records, and accessible universal design.

## Key Terminology
- **Affordance**: Perceivable physical or digital property of an object suggesting how it can be used (e.g., a handle for pulling).
- **Natural mapping**: Spatial alignment between controls and their real-world effects (such as left-to-right burner knobs).
- **Forcing function**: Design constraint physically preventing a user from proceeding without completing a critical safety step.
- **Swiss Cheese Model**: James Reason's accident model showing failures occur when multiple imperfect defense layers align simultaneously.
- **Slips vs. Mistakes**: Norman's distinction between execution errors during automatic habits (slips) and flawed planning/goals (mistakes).
- **Situational awareness**: Continuous mental tracking of environmental elements, their meaning, and near-future projection (crucial in aviation).
