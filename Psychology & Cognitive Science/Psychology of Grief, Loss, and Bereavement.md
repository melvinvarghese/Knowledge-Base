# Psychology of Grief, Loss, and Bereavement

## Overview
Grief is the natural, multifaceted psychological and physiological response to losing a loved one or cherished life bond. Replacing rigid five-stage myths, contemporary bereavement science highlights oscillation between loss and restoration, continuing internal bonds, and remarkable human resilience.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Elisabeth Kübler-Ross's famous five stages (Denial, Anger, Bargaining, Depression, Acceptance, 1969) were originally observed in terminally ill patients facing their own deaths, never intended as a linear checklist for bereaved survivors. Modern research by George Bonanno proves most bereaved individuals do not march through neat stages; roughly 50–60% follow a resilient trajectory of acute sorrow that gradually stabilizes without clinical pathology.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Margaret Stroebe and Henk Schut's Dual Process Model captures healthy grieving as natural oscillation between Loss-Oriented coping (crying, yearning, reviewing memories) and Restoration-Oriented coping (handling finances, learning new roles, taking breaks from grief). Furthermore, 'Continuing Bonds' theory replaced Freud's old demand to detach emotionally from the deceased, recognizing healthy ongoing internal connection through rituals and memory. For a minority (~7–10%), intense yearning stalls into Prolonged Grief Disorder (added to DSM-5-TR and ICD-11), treated effectively with targeted Complicated Grief Therapy.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Bereavement science guides compassionate palliative care, workplace leave policies, and nuanced clinical screening distinguishing normal deep mourning from clinical depression or prolonged grief.

## Key Terminology
- **Dual Process Model of Grief**: Stroebe and Schut's framework describing healthy oscillation between confronting loss and rebuilding daily life.
- **Continuing bonds**: Ongoing, adaptive internal emotional connection maintained with a deceased loved one.
- **Prolonged Grief Disorder**: Persistent, disabling yearning and preoccupation lasting beyond a year after bereavement.
- **Anticipatory grief**: Mourning occurring prior to an expected death during terminal illness.
- **Disenfranchised grief**: Sorrow minimized or unacknowledged by society (e.g., miscarriage, pet loss, estranged partners).
- **Resilience trajectory**: Most common bereavement pattern showing temporary disruption followed by stable psychological recovery.
