# Motivation, Self-Determination, and Drive

## Overview
Motivation investigates the psychological forces initiating, directing, and sustaining goal-oriented behavior. Evolving from biological drive-reduction models and Maslow's hierarchy of needs to Self-Determination Theory, modern research explains why autonomy, competence, and relatedness fuel enduring intrinsic engagement.

## Core Mechanics & Foundations
Early motivation models centered on biological homeostasis (Clark Hull's drive-reduction theory: hunger or thirst create tension reduced by action) or Abraham Maslow's humanistic Hierarchy of Needs (1943), progressing from physiological survival and safety through belonging and esteem toward self-actualization. While Maslow's pyramid remains culturally iconic, empirical research showed humans frequently pursue art, altruism, or ideals even when lower material needs go unmet.

## Modern Applications & Advanced Significance
Edward Deci and Richard Ryan's Self-Determination Theory (SDT) became the leading empirical framework by distinguishing intrinsic motivation (doing an activity for inherent enjoyment) from extrinsic motivation (acting for money, grades, or avoiding punishment). SDT identifies three universal psychological nutrients required for flourishing: Autonomy (feeling ownership over choices), Competence (experiencing mastery), and Relatedness (meaningful social connection). Crucially, Deci's 'overjustification effect' showed that paying people for an already enjoyable activity can undermine intrinsic interest by shifting perceived causality outward.

## Real-World Impact & Systems
Goal-setting theory (Locke and Latham) further proved that specific, challenging goals outperform vague 'do your best' intentions, while Carol Dweck's mindset research linked mastery-oriented goals to resilience after setbacks. SDT now shapes progressive workplace management, game design, and student-centered pedagogy.

## Key Terminology
- **Self-Determination Theory (SDT)**: Deci and Ryan's macro-theory positing that autonomy, competence, and relatedness are innate needs driving intrinsic motivation.
- **Intrinsic vs. Extrinsic motivation**: Engaging in an activity for inherent satisfaction versus external rewards or pressure.
- **Overjustification effect**: Paradoxical drop in intrinsic motivation after introducing tangible external rewards for an already enjoyable task.
- **Hierarchy of Needs**: Maslow's motivational pyramid progressing from physiological survival toward self-actualization.
- **Self-efficacy**: Albert Bandura's construct of an individual's belief in their capability to execute actions required for specific goals.
- **Approach vs. Avoidance motivation**: Behavioral orientation energized toward positive outcomes versus away from threats or failure.
